# Cardinal Matthäus Schiner to Henry VIII, Trent, 14 June 1516 (Büchi 1925 no. 541): English translation

Most Sacred [Majesty], etc. — From Your Majesty's most kind ⟦letter⟧ sent to me from London on the 8th of last month ⟦I
see that you take it ill that I have not⟧ offered Your Majesty anything of the course of events, of the outcome of
things and of the affairs of war, ⟦nor⟧ in full. Which indeed ⟦I regret; but with the utmost⟧ prayers I beg the same
⟦Majesty⟧ to deign to ⟦weigh⟧ the cause of this neglect. For from the day on which I set out from the Val di Sole to
the Imperial Majesty's ⟦camp, to direct⟧ the armies and the affairs of war, I never [had the means of writing] to Your
Majesty; [but, intent] on the remaining pending matters and on guarding against dangers, I at last reached [Verona]
safe, and having now come as far as here, I have stayed three days to speed certain ex[peditions]. And since news of
such [matters] does not come easily or safely either by letters or by messengers, it would [be] more right to confer
and discuss everything by word of mouth [in person], which ought to be kept hidden under the veil of secrecy, since
[minds of] good faith are strangers in these times. For in the past days, when I [noticed] that one of the [agents]
was abounding in his own sense and proceeding by certain ways and means [against it, and I wished] to bring him onto
the path that seemed to me both fitter and fairer for the business, having exacted an oath that he would not disclose
to any creature, rational or irrational, by word [or] writing, the [secrets] I was to reveal to him, I showed him what
the Imperial Majesty ⟦proposed to Your Royal Majesty⟧ and agreed, through my ministry, concerning ⟦the duchy of Milan and
the crown⟧ ⟦of the Holy Empire to be conferred⟧ on a [friend], thinking them safely and faithfully hidden where h — —.
[But while] talking ⟦with the lord Duke of Bari⟧, who [is] and always has been truly most devoted to me ⟦and has
honoured me as a father⟧, he began to disclose to me how someone had told [him] that I had put forward dealings and ⟦had
offered and promised Your Majesty [that I would do all] to have the duchy of Milan conferred on it⟧; and, as he [would]
not [tell] me anything more, [I asked him to] trust me. But he would not reveal the person who had told him, and
stead[fastly] refused to believe of me [what I had said]. Indeed I understand that such a word has already become known
to the lord Galeazzo Visconti [and] those around him. I, however, more cautiously, wh[en] I knew the matter, dissembled
and denied it, although I was greatly astonished, [since I knew that I] had revealed this matter to no one else; and I
began to [think] various things within myself and to fear that perhaps, just as the teller himself did not keep quiet,
so another(?), knowing something, might lightly taint it further, since it is most dangerous for such a thing to be
heaped up among so many varieties of men, and each is drawn by his own pleasure: on this side the Swiss, [on that] the
French [could] interpret most tortuously, forestall, taint or provoke what would not then be done fittingly after the
fact or from the dealings already touched upon. [But lies] of whatever kind I do not fear, by the saying of Saint
Jerome, and so much the more strongly because Galeazzo, [even now], as often, double, who ⟦had earlier usurped the duchy
of Milan for himself, then offered it to [the Catholic King], that he might become its governor in it; and they say
that the same Catholic King refused it on behalf of his nephew, and he is said to have offered it to Your Majesty⟧. The
Emperor therefore walks with uncertain feet, and, placed [among such men], could neither hide it from them nor so taint
matters greatly, that I believed it would be imputed to me as a crime to keep silent about [them towards] Your
[Majesty] or not to reveal them, leaving [the judgement] of such hazards to Your Majesty's discretion, unless I have
erred in this, that I dared to disclose it to him whom, had I not known him to be Your Royal Majesty's ambassador and
most trusted, I would have kept silent [until] death.

As for the rest that pertains to what is to be conferred and negotiated with the Imperial Majesty, [although I was not
with] him, I have nevertheless striven by letters and other means so much [that] I can promise, and on his behalf I
promise, that in all things and for all things he will act, proceed and persevere most fairly and most confidently [in
what has been said] and promised, with a marvellous [advance] and increase of ardour, love and [fervour], so that after
the divine goodness sor — — (one line illegible). [Nevertheless I had wished,] to obey Your Majesty's command and to
satisfy the duty I owe, to make known to it the [reasons for my] departure from there. But being compelled, as
circumstances required, to [take part in] the articles [of peace] and to be busy in those places and to stay in the face
of the enemy, who were [unused] to messengers, there was less opportunity. And since in all these things that have been
done [so far] other agents of Your Royal Majesty have intervened, and nothing has been committed worthy of honour or
[praise], but rather much worthy of reproach, both in hiring [soldiers and] in proceeding in the matter and in laying out
and pouring out money, [I feared] that if I wrote the truth — since I have never been used to do otherwise — I would
have had to reproach what was done. [Therefore, lest, wishing] to recount it, I should rather seem to judge it, I
thought I should abstain; and [I grieve] at this the more strongly [because] I have learned that some pursue me with
bites and hatred, and tear me to pieces before the Royal Majesty in writing and in words; who, would that they had
lent their ears to my warnings and their steps to my directions! — unless it had pleased God, best of all, otherwise —
they would have made the Royal Majesty master of all its wishes, and themselves loaded with merit and praise before it.
For I have never been found, over a long time, to deceive anyone with the French, since I have been used to have
neither dealings nor practices with them, nor subjection, nor wife, nor children, nor gifts from them; nor prodigal,
nor to have ill administered the money and the province entrusted to me; on the contrary, to spend my own, not to ruin
what belongs to others; and as such I have been held also by the Roman pontiffs and others for whom I have
administered.

I also have twenty years' continuous practice, knowledge and exercise with the Swiss, and speech without an
interpreter. To them, if they judge rightly, I have been for the heaping up and restoring of much honour and advantage;
and when they followed my warnings they abounded in honours; but when they did otherwise, it went otherwise with them.
But those among them who favour the French, and they are countless, and others, wherever anything adverse and contrary
befalls them and their fortune, like the French themselves, strive to derive the origin and cause from me and to turn
vengeance back on me, as though they thought that every straw of opposition would be rooted out once I was trodden
down. And if they redouble their darts against me everywhere, I have also scented from afar that some rivals have
reported even to Your Royal Majesty and elsewhere that the Swiss, out of the hatred with which they were moved against
me, made dissension, tumult and withdrawal at Bergamo, so as not to serve with me. A marvellous thing: when they heard
and saw me coming to them, all of them, suddenly moved with joy and immense applause, ran to meet me, filling the
roads, gates and windows, so that even the stones and everything seemed to resound with joy at my coming, and many
were wet with tears; who, if they had burned with hatred or envy against me, since it has been known of old to every
judge and to men that those whom they hate ⟦they cannot look upon with glad eyes or a cheerful face⟧. But as the light is
hateful to owls and to sick eyes, so certain directors, the royal agents and captains of troops, and the business,
⟦which they took care should succeed otherwise than rightly⟧ (!), envying my honour, when they saw that by it the things
they had ill done and their frauds were being repaired and recognised, began from then on to grumble and to say that
the business was not going according to my view, but otherwise; and so much that for two days and nights [they tried]
to stamp the distrust of a third pay, and to sow evil and tumult among the Swiss, so that it is most notorious to all
where they led matters. From then on everything began to be in danger and to go [backwards]; and if I had not repaired
it by promises and by the pledge of my own person, they [themselves] would have gone off together with the Swiss, so
that in one and the same course both the troops and the engines [called] blidin of the Imperial army would have been
the prey of the French, and whatever the Emperor [had] in Italy would have come to the enemy. And the evil grew so far
that, before fittingly — — — Brescia, whose revenues are 100,000 ducats, was surrendered to the enemy, [unless] 60,000
florins had been applied. And they were already losing Verona, [and some of the French] began, having laid waste the
fields and burnt [the villages, to besiege the city], garrisoned with soldiers; then, because they [heard] that 4,000 Swiss
were to come down from here, [they not only fled], but [left behind] also some boats thrown across as a bridge, anchors
and other things, so much that from Brescia, as the scouts report, they strove both [to sally out and to snatch the
citadel from the enemy], having left 300 peasants as a garrison there — — the hope certainly hangs on the forces, the
city and citadel having been recovered by them — — were shaken. For the soldiers are not [inferior] in strength,
experience and up[rightness, but] the want of gold and of one whole month's pay has hindered us, with which we could
sufficiently both increase the infantry and [hire] the horsemen, whom we greatly need, drive the French from Italy and
shake the Venetians. [All of which] could now be readily achieved and had at once and together. Other — — and other
things are lacking, which, had together, would bestow the whole victory, after — — from now on will be recovered in the
lands, that not only not further other pet — — restore, and we would be able to pursue the whole war even into France.

The [Bishop] of York, in his letter to me, speaks of a peace to be negotiated sufficiently by the Imperial Majesty. In
truth there is no one, nor the Emperor, who has not long wished for it; for [whose restoration] two Roman pontiffs, kings
and princes have done much. But they [never] wished to play except with wrappings, nor ever to come to fitting
articles. [Which was] the greatest cause, for so many years, of the [cavilling] not only of Italy but of all Christendom.
If they had been content with just pacts, peace would long since have been restored, and to the Teucri(?) too, and
France would have been [conquered] by Your Royal Majesty while the Imperial Majesty was coming down to visit it. Then
they pressed the Imperial Majesty with new and most bitter wars [to adhere], and so all wishes on both sides came to
nothing. And n[ow the French], after taking Brescia, are so puffed up that they would not [yet] entertain any
conditions of pe[ace]. They also claim — and so they have pacts with the French — that everything long [usurped by
others], which the Roman Church and every other have recovered, should come into their p[ower]; and the Frenchman is
already said to have ced[ed] Crema [to the French] on the pretext of having recovered Brescia, which [I take as a sign]
not only of such ambition and greed but truly of their ruin, [nay rather] that Brescia can be recovered, if a few aids
are not lacking to the Emperor, and, [once Brescia is taken and the French] driven out, Crema with the duchy would be
recovered. And if the Venetians held Crema, there would [never be peace]. I fear indeed, and I have heard the Pope
judge, that the Venetians will never be quiet unless they have usurped Italy, nor can any rest be promised to Italy
unless they are [driven back] to their usual marshes. When this is rightly understood, there will be no need to argue
it. Yet I do not believe that the Imperial Majesty rejects a fitting peace, nor shall I [ever] fail to persuade His
Majesty of it lawfully, so that I [may] serve Your Royal Majesty. If at last there were any other things concerning the
earlier expedition and [to be reported] about it, I have taken care to make them known point by point to Your
Majesty's ambassadors and agents, [and I do not doubt] that they will have fulfilled their duty and mine towards Your
same Majesty by informing Your Majesty. I for my part abstained from letters, so as not to heap the troubles of a
troublesome business on Your Royal Majesty by letters; but if the same [Majesty] wishes to know every particular, I
shall fulfil its wishes as it commands, who also these s — — — except too much then of my honour, which, as I have
kept it unstained, [I shall keep], because with all the strength of soul and body heaped together, understanding
above — — I have consecrated my devotion to Your Most Sacred Majesty, to have [increased] whose [praise] I shall rejoice
in more than to have gained all the treasures of the world, — — fearing the loss of these, unwillingly to preserve
with — — — to be taken away and the Royal favour — — — I communicate my mind, although the Archdeacon, my servant,
[left] Your Majesty not long ago, who [reported] one by one every piece of news here and what continually [happened].
May it deign to hear them, and to number me among [all] its perpetual and most faithful little servants and hold me
commended, remembering at some time my help — — most [—] and of the sacred faith and of the Christian commonwealth,
for increasing honours — — — and advantages, as it is believed to be decreed in heaven, to be accomplished on earth.
Safe — — — Trent, 14 June.

Address: To the most Christian and most sacred lord, the lord King of England and France, his most dread lord.
