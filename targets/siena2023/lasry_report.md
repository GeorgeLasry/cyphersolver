Ciphertext
No. 20 carries about 1,040 cipher symbols in 16 lines on one page, framed by a clear salutation (Sacra Regia
Maiestas … ante terre pedum oscula) and a clear date line (Scriptum Perusii die penultimo mensis Martii)
with an unread abbreviated subscription; no. 23 carries about 3,220 symbols in 54 lines on one page and is
wholly in cipher, both Memoriale headings included, except for the two short clear phrases como è ditto di
sopra and [prout?] superius dictum est.
Key
60 distinct signs. One homophonic key serves both sheets: four signs for a, four for i, three each for e, o and
u, two each for g, l and s and one for every other consonant, single signs for the groups ci, et, ff, qu, re, rr
and ss, eleven nulls, no word dividers beyond an occasional point, and a small nomenclator of seven name
signs ([Paolo Orsini?], [Sforza?], [Tartaglia?] and four unidentified persons or places).
Signs as cut from the manuscript, with their number of occurrences; homophones stand under their letter.

No. 20 is a captain's letter to King Ladislaus from Perugia. 'That traitor' [Paolo Orsini?], the writer complains,
has long tried to ruin him, to take his lands and his company, and has already taken a good two hundred of
his horses; the king has repeatedly promised to intervene with the pope ('nostro Segnore'), yet nothing has
been done. He therefore announces that tomorrow, the last day of the month, he will ride out against the
traitor and do him all the harm he can, even at the risk of his own head, sparing the lands, men and livestock
of the Church. No. 23 holds two royal memoranda. The first, for Iacobo Spinello going to the Marche,
reverses an earlier order: [Tartaglia?] is to go in person to the passes of Ascoli, the Tronto, Arquata and
Montemonaco and, with Rodolfo (da Varano), [Name3] and [Sforza?], make sure that [Paolo Orsini?] cannot


<!-- report p. 6 -->

get out of the Marche, under the cover story of protecting the king's lands and finishing the works on the
Tronto. The captains are to keep good spies, deal secretly with the Malatesti, stand ready to attack or
besiege Orsini, give out that [Name3] and [Sforza?] came because Orsini took their companions and is their
enemy, and keep Orsini, messer Ludovico and the Malatesti from harming the king's allies; [Sforza?] is also to
recover the castles of the contado of [Name5] from the exiles with the 140 lances left there by Dominico de
[Name4], and Spinello is to stay in the Marche and report. The second, for Francesco Gattola going to
Tartaglia, summons ? with his whole company, foot and baggage to the camp at the Mazzone delle Rose by 4
May at the latest, hands him 1,000 ducats which the king says he does not owe, and takes his promise under
seal to come.
Historical context and dating
Both sheets belong to King Ladislaus's campaign of spring 1413, between the peace of San Felice (June 1412)
and his seizure of Rome (8 June 1413); no other year 1409–1414 fits the king's movements, Tartaglia's
freedom and the Malatesti's stance. Paolo Orsini entered the Marche on 15 February 1413 and attacked
Monterubbiano with Ludovico Migliorati on 12–13 April; about 16 April royal troops at Forca di Presta and,
under Sforza, at Caldarola and Sarnano stood 'ut dominus Paulus Ursinus non posset recedere de Marchia …
ad petitionem Regis Apulie' (Cronaca fermana, ed. De Minicis 1870, pp. 39–40): the first memorandum
carried out. On 10 May Orsini broke out to Rocca Contrada and was besieged there (Montauri, RIS² XV/6, pp.
771–776). The Mazzone delle Rose was the royal muster plain near Capua (Faraglia, Giovanna II, 1904;
Tummulillis, Notabilia temporum); the king was at Naples on 3 May. Sforza, at Perugia from February 1413
and Orsini's enemy by 8 April, had long accused Orsini of stealing his company (Minuti, Vita di Sforza, pp.
156–169): the probable writer of no. 20.

Sacred Royal Majesty. Most serene prince and most dread lord, first the kisses of the ground before your feet. As
Your Royal Majesty can and must know, and as I have written to you several times, that traitor [Paolo Orsini?]
has up to now always contrived to undo me and to take from me my lands and my company; and up to now he
has taken from me a good two hundred horses, and many times I have complained of it to Your Royal Majesty,
and you have always comforted me and promised to work with our Lord [the pope] so that these things would
cease; and never dessut [Paolo Orsini?] has anything been done. Rather the said traitor has from day to day

always done worse; so that, seeing him [to be?] my mortal enemy, I have resolved, even if I were certain that
Your Royal Majesty would have my head cut off, to do to him, as far as lies in my power, what he has wished to
