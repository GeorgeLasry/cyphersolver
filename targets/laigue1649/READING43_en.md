# No. 43: Conti's mémoire for Laigue, Paris, 5 March 1649 (BnF fr. 3854 no. 43, ff. 117r-120r): English translation

Translation (Claude Opus 5.5, 23 Oct 2026) of the edition of the whole mémoire in `READING43.md` ("The whole mémoire,
ff. 117r-120r"): the first twelve lines of f. 117r decoded in this project, the rest the contemporary interlinear
decipherment. Same marks: `[x]` letters supplied, `[…]` a gap, `(?)` a doubtful reading. Clear and deciphered words
are not distinguished in the edition, nor here.

## Translation

**For Monsieur de Laigue.** Because we do not doubt that the Cardinal will put a bad interpretation on all their
actions, and that he will principally make his efforts to put the Archduke in distrust of our good intentions on the
subject of the conference which [the] Parlement has granted, I have thought fit to have you instructed of it, so that
you may turn aside the bad effects of the Cardinal's artifices, and make the Archduke understand that this parley was
caused and resolved only by the delays the Archduke brings to succouring us, and that we are still in our same
designs, without anything being able to alter the word we ha[ve given] to work strongly to procure for the two crowns
tranquillity and peace by the removal of those who oppose it. As we hope also that the Archduke, not being capable of
failing in the assurances he has had given us of treating with us, will have given no credit to the Cardinal's
envoys, and will not have let himself be dazzl[ed] by what this common enemy may have had said to him on the pretext
that we are thinking of a separate accommodation in this conference; on the subject of which you will point out to
the Archduke what its motives have been, what the design, and the state in which things are, according to what
follows.

[The] Parlement having thought it a matter of propriety to inform the Queen of the arrival of Dom Joseph and of his
proposals, the Cardinal, who saw well that his ruin would follow from this true approach to peace, in order to turn
aside the blow, or at least to delay its execution, had a conference proposed which might draw things out and give
him leisure to turn aside the Archduke's good intentions by making us suspected of relaxing our rights; for which
Vautorte was employed. It would have been very easy for us to refuse this conference if the Archduke's succour had
been ready, and if we had not been so hard pressed by the need of victuals. But in these two very pressing
conjunctures, on which I have already despatched three persons to you, we thought it fitting, while awaiting news
from you, not to prevent the conference, so as to give the Archduke a little more time to put himself in a state to
come to us, and meanwhile to be able to keep this great city alive until our joined forces have raised the blockade.
The conference has therefore been accepted, but on this condition, that there shall be treated in it not only the
[…] of this kingdom but the general peace; whereby the Archduke will be able to judge that even in this we are [w]ell
willing to show that our design always tends to doing nothing without treating with him, as we have engaged
ourselves to do. The present advantage that comes to us from conferring is that, making no suspension of arms, and
being on the contrary in a position to undertake this very day something very considerable to get victuals into
Paris, besides some [c]onvoys our troops are making and the small help of the peasants, the ministers of
Saint-Germain grant us to let a hundred muids of wheat come from Corbeil every day, beginning with this one, which is
the first of the conference, as long as it lasts; which may let us get through some time yet, but, to tell the
truth (?), very little, while waiting for you.

As to the effect of the conference, you will make the Archduke understand that, since we have been masters of the
nomination of the deputies, both of the Parlement and of the other sovereign companies, and the most just party has
easily prevailed everywhere, and these deputies moreover being all honest men, in zeal for the general peace and in
an irreconcilable hatred of the Cardinal's ministry, the conference will either drag on or break off, according as
the good of our affairs and the projects of the peace we wish to treat with the Archduke shall seem to us to require,
without anything being able to be concluded in it unless the Cardinal withdraws; in which case, the Archduke being
called to it, the treaty of the general peace will be concluded without difficulty. But as there is no ground to hope
that mere mildness and the reasoning of the decrees will persuade him to leave affairs, and force will have to be
used for it, so it is also impossible that this treaty, in the state it is in, can have any effect. You will also
point out to the Archduke on this article that the power given to the deputies to treat must in no way make him fear
that they could conclude, since, even if they were as much creatures of the Cardinal as they are honest men and
devoted to the good cause, they have not the power to relax the least thing on the article of the Cardin[al], […]
that he is condemned by [the] Parlement, and that, according to the laws and the institution of […] company, […] not
[…] ever, not annul, but merely weaken one of its decrees. For my own part, and that of the Messieurs the generals, I
consider that the Archduke will have every reason to be pleased that neither I nor the others would attend the
conference; which we did for several reasons, of which the apparent ones are to show openly to all the world that our
interest does not lead us, and that if we have some interests to look after, we leave the entire decision of them to
the Parlement, of whose deputies we are sure; which always establishes our reputation, gains us credit in the
provinces and strengthens the justice of our cause. But the solid and effective reasons that keep us away from the
parley are that we wished to remain in a position to break it off whenever it seemed good to us and we judged it
fitting for the establishment of the peace of the two crowns; having had regard in this principally to showing the
Archduke that we neglect not a single measure that we do not take to make him know that, being united by our word
for the general peace, we keep ourselves in a state where nothing could oblige us to fail him in it.

On all this you can positively assure the Archduke, first, that, the people of Paris being disposed [and] much pressed
by the dearth of victuals, and wanting to be led to battle, which we did not think we ought to hazard, the fall of the
whole party being at stake in it, we accepted a conference in order to be able to live among our discomforts a
little longer and to give the Archduke the opportunity to succour us; and, in the second place, that, the said
conference being composed on our side of honest men, and being moreover unable to come to anything during the
ministry of the Cardinal condemned by the Parlement, and neither I nor the Messieurs the generals having been willing
to be [there], so as always to remain its masters and to resolve with the honest men of the Parlement on the course
that would seem to us most fitting to carry out the good designs we have in common with the Archduke, you can assure
the Archduke, and give him my express word, that from the moment he enters France this conference will break off, we
having sure (?) and certain means of it, and that another, more sincere and more assured, will be begun with his
deputies, which will soon give us peace.

But as we are extraordinarily pressed, and if the succour does not arrive (?) as promptly as our need of it is great,
affairs could fall into an extremity from which […] would not be capable of raising them, press the Archduke, but
without respite, on this: that there is not a single moment more to lose in succouring us, and that he can well judge
how much our necessities have increased since the 22nd of February, when I despatched to him to tell him that they
were very pressing. Solicit him further to bring […] (?), to whom, according as and as far as you judge fitting, you
will impart these things; solicit him to have us succoured with all their forces, of which we have an entire
necessity, besides which that will lead the sooner to a general accommodation. And if any difficulty is found on the
Archduke's part, which I will not believe, about not entering without a treaty, always advance […], and see to it
that it does not depend on that whether we are succoured, repeating again to the Archduke the word I have had given
him by all my envoys, that I assure him he will be satisfied on everything he could wish, and that, he and I
conspiring with all our forces for the peace of the peoples of Europe and the assured and firm union of the two
crowns, there is nothing on my side capable of obliging me not to conclude what I also promise myself from the
Archduke.

**At Paris, the 5th of March 1649.**
