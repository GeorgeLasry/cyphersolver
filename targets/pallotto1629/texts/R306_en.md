---

Last week I made effective representations to Prince Eggenberg, and to His Excellency himself, for the settlement of
Italian affairs, as I wrote in other letters of mine, suggesting, as speculative talk and without committing not only the
masters but not even the parties, that the difficulty did not lie in the point of reputation, as they seemed to think,
since on this side it is believed that much advantage would be given to the Emperor's; but in the suspicions and
misgivings that the interested parties conceive, and, as they claim, not without grounds, about the substance: namely
that, once the French have withdrawn, the aim is to have the deposit again; that the decision of the case is to turn not
on the point of the succession but rather on the devolution, because of the alleged disobedience and the calling-in of
the French, so as to have the free disposal of those states, unless Nevers consents to the exchange, the garrison or the
demolition to satisfy the Spaniards, and to give up to Savoy, Lorraine and Guastalla as much of the states as will
content them, weaken the Duke and make French help harmful to him; and finally that the aim is not then to leave the
passes of the Rhaetia free. And since they always profess the very opposite of these ideas, I had room to press that,
this being so, the settlement would perhaps become easy, which they likewise declare they desire. Meanwhile the Palatine
of Hungary and the Archbishop of Esztergom arrived to deal with the affairs of that kingdom, which they found in the
greatest danger, as much with Bethlen Gabor alive as with his death; and both made most effective representations for the
settlement of Italian affairs, as necessary in order to be able to meet the needs and prevent the ruin of that kingdom.

Prince Eggenberg told Monsignore that he had talked with me at length and had made certain arrangements; but when I told
Monsignore what had passed and what I thought necessary to reach some conclusion, he went back to the Prince the same day
and at once reported to me the views and arrangements made with His Excellency. In accordance with them I drafted a
letter to give an account of it by express courier to Your Most Illustrious Lordship, and showed it the next day to the
said Monsignore; since he approved it from the first to the last word, and particularly in the substantial points as
agreeing with what Your Most Illustrious Lordship had arranged with His Excellency, I almost believed I had concluded the
business, since, having discreetly sounded the Bishop of Mantua and the Venetian resident here, I did not find them far
off. But when I then went to the Prince and read him that paper, I found His Excellency's views very different: in effect,
that the King of France should write to His Imperial Majesty and at the same time withdraw his arms not only from
Montferrat but from Susa too; that the Duke of Mantua should ask pardon for the disobedience and for calling in the
French, acknowledging the error committed; and on the other side that His Majesty should neither promise nor assure the
Duke the possession of the states (though he hinted, without commitment, that they would be left to him), but would
grant him grace and pardon for the errors committed in such a way that His Majesty's goodwill would be seen, and that the
King of France's intercession had been of use to the Duke (on the understanding, however, that His Excellency's words
could come true even if only part of the states were granted to him); and finally that as to the passes of the Rhaetia
it would be put in negotiation and the interested parties heard, mentioning the Bishop of Chur, and that the passes
belong to that church, and that Our Lord ought to be glad that they should remain in its power rather than in heretics';
adding that it matters far more to the House of Austria not to give up these passes than to remove France from that of
Susa.

Although I saw things set on such hard terms, I nevertheless tried not to cut the thread of the negotiation altogether.
As to the possession, I said that, since the fears of partiality were much lessened, it seemed that His Majesty, of his
own free will, as the reasons that moved him to the sequestration were ceasing, and not to satisfy others, could allow
that nothing be changed about the possession against the will of the parties. As to the pardon, that this formality and
the Duke's confession could be considered prejudicial to the solid reputation of the Duke and of his succession, in which
Austrian blood was indelibly mixed, and even to His Majesty's dignity, it being much more that it had not been offended
than that, offended, it had granted pardon; and that therefore His Majesty's goodness and generosity would have ample
field to exercise itself without prejudice to the Duke, and much less to his grandsons and great-grandsons, who are
without doubt innocent. On the point of the passes I suggested that one could negotiate taking as an expedient that
until the decision of the case His Majesty should remain in the Grisons and France in Susa, and once the rest was
settled a remedy would be found for this too.

When I reported to the Archbishop of Esztergom what I had discussed with the Prince, he was equally confused and
displeased, and assured me again and again that the business stood as he had told me and I had written; and both
Monsignore and the Palatine, stressing the most dangerous state of that kingdom and saying that within a few weeks we
shall see the fire from our windows (which I know they said in full council), told me they could not grasp the motive or
the ground of so strange a decision to engage so deeply without necessity in the wars of Italy, while His Majesty's own
kingdoms and hereditary states are in such danger and need that they can expect nothing but to be the invader's prey,
lacking all assistance and all help, without even the fortresses being out of this danger, without munitions of war,
without victuals and almost without soldiers, the few who are there having gone unpaid for years and being in extreme
need and almost despair; the Empire not being quiet, and the affairs of Flanders in the very bad state that is seen.

From the Prince's talk and from other weighty indications I see that they consider it points of great importance to the
House of Austria to keep the passes of the Rhaetia and to see that the Duke of Nevers is humbled and damaged, so that no
example of advantage, but of the contrary, should remain in Italy from French help.

There are those who believe it is also thought necessary to humble and weaken, if it cannot be destroyed, the power of
Venice, and that they consider it an opportune moment that the Emperor, powerful in the number and quality of his
soldiery, and Spain, through the quality of the leader commanding its arms and the effort in men and money they are
putting together with Savoy, Florence, Parma and Modena, are now united; the Pope is neutral, Mantua destroyed; France,
they expect to divert it, or that its forces will not arrive in time, or at least will not suffice for the help needed
and to overcome at the same time the obstacles on Savoy's side, of whom it is presumed that, to reassure the Spaniards
further, he will give them the posts of Avigliana, Pinerolo and the others to guard; and finally Venice, that it will
not be able to resist force from several sides and the secret understandings and leanings towards the Empire claimed to
exist in their principal cities, above all with Collalto leading the army; I have heard these designs about him from a
serious person, and from another, that he has at other times let it be understood that if the Emperor made war on the
Venetians, and in earnest, and he led it, he too would hope to attain the title of Highness.
