# R305 — Pallotto to Barberini, Vienna, 1 September 1629: English translation

Translation of `R305_reading.md` (22 Oct 2026, Claude Opus 5.5), with the same holes. Paragraphs 1-3 follow the Roman
decipherment printed by Kiewning (1897), Nr. 170; paragraphs 4-6 are read from the figures and the clear words on the
sheet.

---

In a long conversation I had the day before yesterday with Prince Eggenberg, while we spoke of current Italian affairs,
His Excellency declared that if the King of France wrote to the Emperor to assure him of the good intention His Majesty
had had in coming into Italy, and of not prejudicing the dignity and jurisdiction of His Imperial Majesty, and asked him to
hold the cause and interests of the Duke of Nevers recommended for his sake, and at the same time withdrew his troops from
Montferrat and Susa, Christendom and Italy would at once have peace, and the Most Christian King would see by deeds the
esteem the Emperor has for His Majesty and how much his intercession had been of help and use to the Duke.

I went on suggesting to the Prince the difficulties and suspicions that this arrangement raises, and the moral security
that would be needed to remove the fear that, with the deposit, the alleged disobedience and the calling-in of the French
might be held against Nevers, and that instead of help it might have brought him ruin with the loss of his rights; and
that the passes of the Grisons might always remain closed to foreign help from princes not trusted by the House of
Austria. I likewise suggested the remedy for the first without damage to imperial reputation: to grant the investiture
without prejudice to the rights of the other parties, and in substance without sanctioning the Duke's rights, only that
things should stay as they are until the decision. For the second, that the parties be summoned to set out the rights
they hold over the states by the death of the last Duke, Vincenzo. For the third, that once the King of France had first
withdrawn from Montferrat, the passes of Susa and of the Rhaetia should be given up at the same time.

The Prince showed that he made no difficulty about the substance, but about the circumstance: that while the arms of
France were in Italy, whatever the Emperor did would seem to be done for fear of those arms. I brought various reasons to
the contrary. On the first point the Prince came out with the view that at the instance of another prince the investiture
could be granted in that form; but when I wanted to press this point he again said that it could not be done unless
France first withdrew, because it would seem done out of the same fear. Although I replied that it had already been shown
to the world that the King of France was not feared, nor his demands heeded, accompanied as the Prince said by threats,
with the very clear and resolute answers given to Sabran; and I added that the said investiture could be given in
confidence, not to be shown until the King had left, and that it could be dated after the said withdrawal. But the Prince
held firm throughout on that difficulty: if he will not withdraw, the Duke of Friedland will be sent in, and then, when His
Majesty gives the sentence, it will be seen that he does not give it for fear of the King, and if there is difficulty in
carrying it out, it will be carried out with the sword.

The Duke of Friedland seems to be in no great hurry now; they say it is because they are waiting to hear the King of
France's resolution [?after] Sabran's negotiation; but others put it down to the lack of provisions and money, to the
obstacles and difficulties they find with the Spa[?niards], who aim meanwhile to wear out the Venetians and other princes
of Italy with the expense until the new season; and finally that they fear to strip this country of men, things being
troubled, and above all with the efforts of the French, of [?which] the Prince himself complained, exaggerating them
sharply. But on the other hand it is known that the Maestro di campo Aldringen had advanced towards the passes with a
large body of men, and that in the lands on those borders mustering places and musters were being given out and stages
assigned for twenty-two thousand [?men].

Our Prince told me of the representation made by the imperial ambassador there on the matter of the promotion, and that
he was answered with […], without specifying the particulars Your Most Illustrious Lordship mentioned to me. I do not
know whether, so as not to consider himself [907] obliged to open himself further [?about] the [?affairs] of the peace,
or because nothing else has been [?specified] to him by that ambassador.

On the [?rank] of [?legate] he told me he had in answer that Our Lord would inform himself of the usual practice and would
consider what [?he can] do; and he mentioned that at the wedding of Mat[?thias], when he was King of Hungary, Signor [277]
was deputed as [?legate]; the King went out to meet him, but made the entry and gave him first place in his carriage;
which carriage, however, I have heard at other times from Signor [207] was so made that it had neither first nor second
place. I did not want to omit mentioning this to Your Most Illustrious Lordship. From another reliable quarter I have
word that with Lorraine one must go very carefully in these affairs, and that France keeps its eyes well open with him.
