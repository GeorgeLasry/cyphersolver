Vienna, 18 August 1629. A. 43.

As to the affairs of Italy I have nothing else to report, except that the same ideas and disposition towards peace are
maintained, but no effort is spared to give every greater [443] […] to the provisions for war and to the advance of
troops towards [?the frontiers]. The suspicions of the designs of France and of the [290] keep growing, that they are
aimed at disturbing the affairs of the Empire.

The [372], after obtaining the […] imperial sentence in his favour in the [?county] of Saarwerden, took possession of it
by force of arms within a few days, and having sent a gentleman of his to inform His Majesty, he has had him ask for
assistance for its defence, saying that five hundred [?horse] […] had come close to those [?frontiers] and that other
troops were gathering there, insinuating that the French mean to [797] those passes for the design of making war in
Germany; and from [?here] he has been promised assistance as he asked.

[?But] greater commotion has been caused by the news brought by an express courier who came from [525] three days ago with
letters of the twenty-sixth of last month: that the secretary, back from England, where […] went when called to negotiate
peace [?with] that King, had reported that peace could not be concluded between the said crowns unless the Palatine is
entirely restored to his state and to the electoral vote, and that this was the first article of the peace made with
France, whose King has promised to help to this end with troops and money. All this the Lord [?Prince of Eggenberg] told
me the day before yesterday, weighing the ill will of France and how badly the Elector of Bavaria would take it. It is
many weeks since I saw in a letter written by the ambassador in England to this [649] that the said secretary had
arrived there and was offering very rich terms, in particular to have restored to the Palatine not only the Lower
Palatinate held by them but also the part held by Bavaria. To which he added afterwards, in further letters, that the
Palatine, to whom this had been written, had not lent his ears, believing it all to be idle tales and that no reliance
could be placed on the promises of the Spaniards. So one may suspect that the news now sent is a device to commit the
Emperor further and force him to carry the war into Italy against France so as not to have it in Germany, and to divide
Bavaria and make it suspicious and force it likewise to stay all the more closely united with the House of Austria.

The ambassador of Savoy has also let it out that the [973] of the four cities of Languedoc have again begun to riot and
that matters there are more broken than ever, though there is no confirmation of this from elsewhere, only of something
at Montauban. Some take occasion to believe that these are [?mere] devices to give the imperials all the more courage to
[?force] their entry into Italy, into the states of Montferrat and Mantua; and to the same end tends the saying that the
French in Montferrat are reduced to seven or [?eight] hundred at most, which has likewise come from the said
[ambassador] of Savoy.

It is the opinion of a sensible person that, once the discontents of the Empire and the fears of a general rising cease
with the dispatch of the troops to Poland and Flanders and with their march towards Italy, and the Empire being by the
same means left as it were disarmed, and consequently in a state to hold the election of the King of the Romans - if it
takes place, as is believed, at the beginning of winter - the settlement of the affairs of Italy will become much easier;
which for the purposes mentioned must now be kept in suspense.

The said election, however, is believed likely to suffer some delay from the accident of the new election of the
Archbishop of Mainz, especially if he does not go about it with good legs; because with the pretext of the despatch he
must await from Rome, of the regalia or investiture he must then obtain here, and of the need to settle the affairs of
his church and gain enough knowledge and information of the affairs of the Empire before engaging in this very great
function, he can put it off not only this coming winter but another one too; and even if he goes with very good legs, it
remains very difficult for all this to be done by the beginning of winter.

The Elector of Saxony is today much changed and estranged in will, not only over the church of Magdeburg taken from him
and the others that it seems are to be taken from him under the imperial edict, but also over the suspicion he has
formed that the aim is the destruction of the Lutheran religion and perhaps the humbling of all the princes of Germany
and the removal of every counterweight to the House of Austria. He has declared himself much displeased with Count
Trauttmansdorff and quite unwilling to come in person to the electoral diet, although they think he will not fail to
send his ambassador.

The Elector of Brandenburg shows himself just as well disposed, and from several sides there are excellent reports that
he is about to declare himself a Catholic; and I am told as a thing almost certain, by a knight who has familiar
correspondence with confidential ministers of His Highness, that when Saxony was speaking with Trauttmansdorff to the
effect that he could not go to the diet because he would be alone, all the others being Roman Catholics, and
Trauttmansdorff said that the Elector of Brandenburg would be there, Saxony replied in anger: I do not know; God grant he
is not a Catholic too.

Some are persuaded that he waits only for the return from Holland of Count Adam of Schwarzenberg, director of his council,
a great favourite, who is a Catholic and a Knight of Malta, so that it may be seen to be his work, and also so that he may
bring back the settlement for freeing his fortresses in the states of Jülich from the Dutch garrisons, since it would be
impossible to recover them once he had declared himself a Catholic. May it please the divine goodness that it be true,
and that this great increase be made to the Catholic religion and to the glory of Our Lord's pontificate.

I do not fail, when the occasion offers, to encourage the said knight to further these inclinations, assuring him of His
Holiness's fatherly disposition towards the person and house of that prince, and to make known with every fitting
demonstration the esteem in which he would hold such a gain.

Of the same Elector's sister, the wife of Gabor: some months ago (as I think I wrote) a very intimate servant of hers told
Monsignor the Archbishop of Esztergom that she was much inclined to the Catholic religion. Now I know that the said
[?princess] has written in her own hand to the Palatine of [966], announcing to him the great grace [?done her] by God in
her conversion to the Catholic religion, in which by God's grace she is utterly resolved to live and die; and the
Palatine's own letter has been sent to the Emperor.

I trust that Your Most Illustrious Lordship is persuaded that I have not failed to represent at suitable moments with how
much care and zeal Our Lord's supreme vigilance has been employed from the beginning for the settlement of the present
troubles of Italy and of Christendom, and to give the reasons for Our Lord's most prudent and most justified actions,
particularly concerning the [?fortification] of his frontiers and the prevention of what [might come] from [233] […] and
from raids of the neighbouring soldiery; [690] [630] I shall make use […] effectively, when the occasion offers, of what
Your Most Illustrious Lordship is pleased to tell me on this subject.

I have spoken to the Lord Prince of Eggenberg about the disposition which Our Lord [?has decided] to make of the
ecclesiastical property to be recovered in the Empire, for the greater service of God and the benefit of [?Germany]. His
