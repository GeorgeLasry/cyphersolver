# R287 — Pallotto to Barberini, Vienna, 4 August 1629: English translation

Translation of `R287_reading.md` (22 Oct 2026, Claude Opus 5.5), with the same holes. The text follows the Roman
decipherment printed by Kiewning (1897), Nr. 153, corrected to the figures of this sheet where they differ (see the
reading's header and the log). The opening words, in angle brackets, are on the preceding sheet R286.

---

‹Nevertheless, after a long discussion, it was drawn from what he said that he would sound out the King's mind in the
manner indicated, provided that the civil and unarmed possession of the states were declared to mean that the Emperor
would acquire it› through a commissioner of his who was not distrusted by the Duke of Mantua, and that no change or
alteration whatever would be made in the civil, political and military government of the two states, nor in the
treatment, pre-eminences and prerogatives of the person and family of the Duke. Provided that justice were declared to
be done by the ordinary way and in conformity with the imperial laws and constitutions, and that the succession to the
said states were judged according to the rights that belonged to each of the parties at the time of the death of the
last Duke Vincenzo. And finally, as to the Most Christian King's withdrawing his arms from Susa, he would propose to the
King one of three courses, namely: that if the Emperor remained in the Rhaetia (the Grisons), the King would remain at
Susa for the same time; or else that, if the King withdrew from the one, the Emperor would at the same time withdraw from
the other pass; or else that, if the Emperor remained in the said passes for the dignity of the administration of justice
and the execution of his sentence, the King would withdraw from Susa to the nearest places of his own state until the
case was finished and the Emperor was in a position to withdraw from the Grisons; but on condition that meanwhile all
the fortifications and defences that could hinder or delay the passage of the Most Christian King be demolished, both
at Susa and at Avigliana, and that it should not be lawful in the meantime to renew them, or in any other way to give
the King cause to suspect that he was being hindered or obstructed in crossing, in case the Spaniards attempted new
violence against the Duke's states; and that Savoy should at the same time leave Moncalvo and the other places agreed
upon to the Duke of Mantua; and that he would send to the King, provided he were assured that, once such proposals were
accepted, they would carry them out on the Emperor's side as well, and that it would not go as with the other courses
proposed in the past to the Duke of Mantua. It was settled in the end that Father Magni would put in writing the points
on which he had remained with Eggenberg, and that Sabran would put in writing the considerations and modifications that
occurred to him.

In accordance with this, this morning Father Magni sent Sabran, Monsignor of Mantua and me the sheet, a copy of which I
send attached. But Sabran would not give any further answer, because of the uncertainty with which it speaks at the end
in the words "this and that", but much more because he has found out, through a person in Father Magni's closest
confidence, that this negotiation is being conducted by the said Father at the instance of the Spanish ambassadors and
of Prince Eggenberg, not to bring it to any conclusion but purely to drag things on and lull the French to sleep, and to
have the courier or gentleman held back whom Sabran is on the point of sending to the King with the report of his
negotiation; or at least so that, if he does send him, the news that a treaty of agreement is pending may hold back or
cool the preparations for war, while they for their part do not cease, with every most exquisite diligence, to press
their army and the Duke of Friedland on with the greatest haste.

And Sabran and the ministers of the allies are the more convinced of this report as they know the said Father, a subject
of the Spaniards by birth, by his residence of more than thirty years in these parts and by having married a brother of
his here, to be entirely imperial.

So today Sabran is sending a French gentleman with the despatches and reports of his negotiation to the King and to
[?Cardinal Richelieu], urging as far as possible the preparations for war and the advance of troops into Italy.

But for all that I shall not [?give up] promoting and facilitating the said negotiation as far as I can, although I
have little hope of a good conclusion, having no slight indications that the point or pretext of reputation is what
moves them least, and that the motive which drives them to take this occasion for war is to quiet the Empire and free
themselves from the danger of a general uprising by sending these troops out, and their commander, the Duke of
Mecklenburg; and what entices them is the hope of setting the imperial reputation and dignity in Italy on a high footing,
of recovering many occupied fiefs (with one of which, I know, they have given Prince Savelli to understand that they
will reward the services rendered and expenses incurred for the Emperor), and of mortifying and weakening the Republic of
Venice in such a way that it can no longer plot against the growth of the House of Austria.
