# R311 — Pallotto to Barberini, Vienna, 29 September 1629 (despatch "A", first part): English translation

Translation of `R311_reading.md` (22 Oct 2026, Claude Opus 5.5). The text follows the Roman decipherment printed by
Kiewning (1897), Nr. 184, which the figures on the sheet match; the despatch continues on R312.

---

On Wednesday I dealt with the Lord Prince of Eggenberg about the affair of Italy, taking occasion of the letter written by
His Majesty to Our Lord, and I said how His Holiness, having made most pressing representations to the Most Christian
King through the nuncio resident there and through Monsieur de Béthune, that Majesty's ambassador to His Holiness, in
accordance with the Emperor's wish, had found various difficulties, which he had communicated to Prince Savelli so that
some way might be found to overcome them; and that for the same purpose new public and general prayers had been ordered
in Rome; and that the zeal and ardent desire His Beatitude has for the peace of Catholic Christendom had led him not only
to suggest to the said Prince that, when he is certain of obtaining it, he will move in person and come to Bologna or
Ferrara to give His Majesty a motive to bend his pious will to the quiet of Italy, but had also written it to me, so that
I should represent it to His Majesty himself, giving him every most lively expression of how highly His Holiness will
value all that His Majesty judges he can do to that end, and how he will consider well spent all the inconvenience and
toil he may suffer in his own person for it. His Excellency declared that His Majesty had valued Our Lord's great zeal
most highly. In short, however, I drew nothing more from him than that he persists in the ideas indicated at other
times: that the King of France should be content with having achieved what he declared from the start he aimed at by the
movement of his arms, that is, the liberation of Casale, should withdraw his arms from the imperial fiefs, and if he
will intercede for the Duke of Nevers amicably and without threats, he will see that His Imperial Majesty values as is
fitting the good offices of so great a king and his nephew.

That day I could not speak to His Majesty, because he had fixed a hunt, but he had me told that, having a very fine one
for the next day, he invited me to take part and would also hear me. So I went the next day, and having gone through with
His Majesty in substance the written representation as best I could, after His Majesty had shown great esteem for Our
Lord's will and disposition, he declared his own to be, as usual, most desirous of peace and most ready for everything he
can agree to without offence to the dignity and authority, not his own, but of the Empire, which he is bound by oath to
preserve. I answered that the preservation of His Majesty's reputation and dignity is not only just but in the interest
of the Catholic religion and of the Apostolic See, and that Our Lord desired from His Majesty only what he judges he can
agree to without loss of his reputation.

His Majesty broke out in complaints of the King of France: that he had tried to throw the imperial dignity to the ground
in Italy, had sent to make representations with threats, had plotted everywhere by treaties against the Empire and the
House of Austria, and had now again sent Monsieur de Marcheville, on the pretext of congratulating the Archbishop of
Mainz on his election, to urge him with offers of help to free them from His Imperial Majesty's oppressions and to sound
his will whether he would agree that the arms of the Catholic faith should assist His Imperial Majesty in the affairs of
Italy; and that the same man was going to the other electors as well. I answered that, against any failing that might be
alleged against the King of France and his ministers, the merit and interest of the Catholic religion and of public quiet
ought to weigh more with His Majesty's singular piety; and I suggested that these same things could be a motive for His
Majesty to settle the affairs of Italy all the more willingly, since this would remove any spur the Most Christian King
may feel to make a diversion, and His Majesty, remaining free with his own arms and with those of the Catholic King,
could not only resist but crush anyone who wished to trouble him. I added, with a reverent and most affectionate
entreaty, that he be pleased to consider the present opportunity of ending these troubles with very great reputation
and glory, no less for his arms and power than for his singular piety, since to his finding himself so powerful in Italy
with his own arms and those of the Catholic King, and the French arms on the contrary so weak, would be added the journey
of a Pope from Rome to the borders of his state, almost three hundred miles away.

I had clear signs, both from the words and from the manner of uttering them, that this and other similar ideas made some
impression on His Majesty's mind; he told me that the next day he would deal with it and take a resolution in council;
so I spoke of it also with some of the privy councillors who were out there, and in the evening, as soon as I returned, I
spoke of it at length with Baron Verda.

Today Baron Questenberg came to me to tell me in His Majesty's name that His Majesty's disposition towards peace has grown
greatly through Our Lord's fatherly and zealous offices, and particularly through the affectionate offer he has made,
which His Majesty values and welcomes with filial affection and deference; and that only the obligation to preserve the
imperial dignity and jurisdiction could keep him from agreeing to all that is asked of him; that he has shown how much he
wishes to stop the fighting, having first issued the decree of sequestration for this purpose, then given room to
negotiations on many compromises, and finally proposed one himself; to propose others, especially not knowing whether
they would be accepted, does not seem fitting, and therefore when they are proposed to him he will listen to them with a
disposition to admit them, provided they are not, as has been said, prejudicial to the imperial dignity and jurisdiction,
it seeming improper either to set that aside or to take a partner in it. I answered in substance, besides the ideas
already explained to His Majesty, that Our Lord has made with the greatest efficacy the representations His Majesty
desired, and has made known the answers he received and the difficulties he found, so that His Majesty might be pleased
to consider them and kindly yield as far as he judges fitting; that if His Majesty does not think fit, for the reason
mentioned, to declare himself to the parties on any compromise, although he did so once before, he might consider
whether that objection ceases when he confidentially communicates his mind to Our Lord, trusting to the affection and
prudence of His Holiness to carry it with due regard; and how far His Holiness's intention is from the drawbacks
mentioned, of diminishing the imperial dignity and jurisdiction or taking a partner in it, would be shown, if so many
other evident testimonies were lacking, by the offer of so great a journey and by his not qualifying the claims of the
interested parties as justified [continued on R312]
