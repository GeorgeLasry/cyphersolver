Bruneau has left on the journey I wrote of; and beyond the purposes reported, it is no great matter that he will have
[to] diminish for him what the extraordinary courier from Spain [227]a[693] in the past days about the
[?capitulations] of the [922] with the [?King of England] on the recovery of the […] and the electoral vote in favour of
Frederick. When the accommodation of the affairs of Italy was raised with Bruneau again, he showed himself rather cold
than otherwise; […] that person has told me that he wanted to speak of it to the Marquis of Aytona. But I place no
reliance on it, because, although His Excellency shows himself desirous of peace, and says there will be time all this
winter to negotiate […] that meanwhile the Queen will come and the wedding will be held, and the imperial troops will
only hold the passes and not advance towards Italy, since the Duke of Friedland in Italy does not suit them, and that
there is [652] little hope of holding Bois-le-Duc; nevertheless it is known on the other side that all their designs
are [?turned] to the affairs of Italy, to driving out Nevers and with him every jealousy the French could cause, and to
recovering […] all they have lost or are about to lose in Flanders, where they think of making a very long [?truce],
whether Bois-le-Duc is kept or lost, and in every worse way.

And the camp master Aldringen has left for Italy, and with him several [?colonels], among them Count Strozzi, as is
written in letters of the twentieth from [?Halberstadt], where the General was still staying.

And I am told that a letter of the Catholic King has been intercepted, addressed there to the Count of Monterrey, in
which he wrote to him to sound out Our Lord about some settlement that had reason in it, so
that if France did not accept it the Catholic King's cause would stand all the more justified, and if it accepted, he
would have it rejected afterwards by the Emperor. [Which], if true, will show all the more clearly their aim of
advancing their p[…], a tacit confession of what they intend, and of a want of justice on the part of the Apostolic
See.

In answer to Your Most Illustrious Lordship's [369] of the eleventh of this month, I will not fail to point out to Your
Most Illustrious Lordship that in mine of the twenty-first of last month I reported [250] weighty reasons that had made
me […] letter that […] to send the packet […] the Duke of Poli […] his [?son], of which there were two principal ones:
the one, the opinion that […] that the aim was not only to keep […] but […] other people's states, and he did not doubt
they would infer this as much from the summoning of the said Duke as from the [?conferring] of the title of lieutenant
general once he had left the Catholic King's state; the [736] that […] the same [359] would not accept the post, being
at present employed [555] in [405] with an immediate increase of post and emolument, and with […] and with […] so
great and so great and so well founded that [537] it could be worth leaving them. But I wrote that [?for] the said
reasons I had resolved [to] send the said packet by a discreet and confidential person; but it could happen that in
the circumstances of the march […] the said Duke, and that therefore on the arrival [of] Your Most Illustrious
Lordship's answer the packet [?might] be found in my hands. I have wished to report all this so as to be able to take
new care [over] its delivery, with the certainty of meeting the will of Our Lord and of Your Most Illustrious Lordship.
By other letters of mine Your Most Illustrious Lordship will have seen that, having learnt that the Duke had stopped
ill at Hamburg, and therefore fearing that the report of his departure might have led the messenger astray, I resolved
to write to Signor T[orquato] by the opportunity of a paymaster which I judged safe, on the thirtieth of last month; to
which I have not yet had an answer.
