# Seure no. 41, Lisbon 1558: English translation

Angus Robinson's English, copied 23 Oct 2026 from his edition (letter 41); `>` = enciphered in the manuscript.

## Translation

Another letter from the same Commander de Seure to the said king. 1558.

Sire, by my last letters of 25 October, which I gave to a Portuguese courier named Antoine Galuan, I wrote to you
of the death of Queen Mary and of the distress of our neighbours at the little hope left to them that the fleet
they were expecting from Peru would come this year, because Don Álvaro de Bazán, who had waited for it in the
Azores for two months, had come back without any news of it. Since then the fleet has arrived at Seville, on the
12th of last month. I could not write this to Your Majesty sooner, because I had no means of sending letters, and
also because I was waiting to learn exactly the whole sum it has brought. It amounts to two million in gold, in
silver: namely eight hundred thousand écus for King Philip, a million for private persons, and another one hundred
and eighty thousand for the deceased whose heirs are here. Besides this there is three hundred thousand écus' worth
of cochineal and aliofre, which are seed pearls.

Spain has made a marvellous celebration of it, as of a thing they were already beginning to despair of for this
year, and also because all King Philip's hope and resource, as I have written to you before, now rests on it. There
were twenty-two ships in all, four of them warships, which brought with them two ships of your subjects that they
had taken in the port of Havana, which is on the island of Cuba opposite Florida, where they were lying in wait for
the fleet. If luck had willed that the fleet had not come escorted by warships, as they expected, or if the said
subjects (who were from Dieppe and La Rochelle) had had three more ships with them, there is no doubt at all, as I
have heard from some twenty of the very men who were there, that they would have carried it all off to France and
done you, at one stroke, a very great and notable service.

> From this, Sire, Your Majesty can clearly see how—following what I have previously written to you—I would be glad
> to deprive your enemy of this great advantage he draws from the New World: or, more precisely, all the sinews and
> resources he now has to sustain the war against you.

> Besides the sum already mentioned which it brought to Seville, that fleet had also unloaded in the Azores a large
> quantity of silver belonging to several private individuals. It amounts to a good eight or nine hundred thousand
> écus. Most of it belongs to the man who is now [viceroy] of Peru. He had it unloaded in those islands for fear
> that, if it reached Seville, King Philip’s officers would seize it, as they had been accustomed to do in previous
> years.

> But, as far as I can see, this will not help him much. The Queen of Portugal has agreed and consented with King
> Philip’s officers and ministers that the silver should be removed and carried to Spain. She has therefore sent very
> stringent orders to the islands to have it handed over to them, refusing to allow any sum of silver coming from
> Peru, however small, to be unloaded in this kingdom.

> Many respectable people here have found this very strange. The late King of Portugal would never have allowed it,
> whatever alliance existed between them. But it seems to me, Sire, that you can well forgive her this one, since it
> is not the first matter in which she has shown such strong attachment to the King of Spain. I have heard that,
> through the last fleet of [hulks] which left here in mid-October for Flanders, she advanced him a substantial sum
> of money which she had drawn from the treasury of the young king, her nephew.

Queen Mary left more than four hundred thousand écus in ready money, mostly in [Portuguese gold coins], which she
is said to have given to King Philip by her will. But she gave her patrimonial property to a bastard son whom the
late emperor acknowledged at the end of his life as a son he had in the city of Augsburg; the said lord also left
him many other possessions. I had written to you that King Philip was sending Don Diego d’[Azevedo] as Viceroy of
Peru, a Castilian gentleman of very good understanding. But he died during the past month, and the princess has
already named another in his place, said to be a man of good understanding. The fleet which will carry him over
there will leave Cádiz at the end of January.

> Shortly before that, another fleet of twenty hulks loaded with spices will leave here for Flanders. It would be
> well worth the while of the men of Dieppe or Saint-Malo to concern themselves with it, for it will be worth an
> immense amount. There will be only two wretched warships to accompany it, and those are poorly armed and equipped
> in every respect.

> The fleet which left here in October is still at present in Galicia, waiting for better weather than it has had.
> Some ships of great value have been lost. The Turks have not besieged Oran, where our neighbours had sent two
> thousand men, as they thought the Turks would do.

Which is all I can write for the present to Your Majesty, to whom, Sire, I pray that Our Lord may grant, in perfect
health, a very happy and very long life. Lisbon, 12 December 1558.

> They owed eight hundred thousand écus at the fairs of Medina. They had delayed payment because they had no means of
> providing the money until after the fleet’s arrival. They have already paid those sums and have since obtained
> fresh credit.

> A few days ago a Norman ship captured a small warship of the queen which, it is said, had attacked it in order to
> take it. She was very angry at this and had the ships of your subjects then in her kingdom detained. But in the end
> I explained so clearly to her the wrong committed by her own ship that she let them go.

Your most humble and most obedient subject and servant, the Chevalier de Seure.

To the king, my sovereign lord. Chevalier de Seure, [27] December 1558. […].
