# R1067, d'Affry to Rouillé, The Hague, 1 April 1757 (no. 161): English translation

Yesterday I went to the Grand Pensionary's to give him an account of the orders with which His Majesty has honoured me,
and which you were so kind as to send me in your despatch of the 28th. I found the Pensionary very cold and
embarrassed. It is certain that everything going on here today is set in motion only to ruin him. He told me that it
was impossible to answer me on the matter today, and that in the ferment in which minds are, he did not think he could
convene an extraordinary assembly for tomorrow; but that he expected to be able to obtain my answer for me on Monday or
Tuesday at the latest. I told him that I would have the honour of informing you of this today, and in the rest of the
conversation I tried not to depart from what you prescribe to me. As the Grand Pensionary spoke to me a good deal about
Madame the Governess, I asked him whether it suited him that I should see her. This question relieved him and pleased
him. On leaving him I went to Her Royal Highness, to whom I read the Declaration I enclose. After this reading I spoke
to her much as to the Grand Pensionary, but with all the reserve of which I am capable in the terms I had to use. When
I had finished, and saw that she did not answer me, I looked at her, and I saw her eyes in tears, and she said these
very words to me: "Mr d'Affry, I beg your pardon; but for a moment do not be a minister. Must it be I who make easier
the means of doing harm to my father?" I answered her: "Your Royal Highness knows how far the King my master has sought
to avoid and to shorten the war. Besides, I do not know the destination of his forces in Germany; but I beg you, Madam,
as a man very touched by and very grateful for the kindness with which Your Royal Highness honours him, to be pleased to
think only of the extreme importance for the Republic of winning the King's friendship and keeping it. I ask Your Royal
Highness to employ not only your good offices but your credit and your power to bring about a prompt answer, such as
the King has the right to hope for." I left her on that. This morning I went to the President of the week, who happens
to be Mr van Borssele; he told me, like the Grand Pensionary, that it would not be possible to answer me before Monday
or Tuesday. Accordingly I am writing to Mr Lesseps so that he may warn Mr Cobenzl and our commissaries who are at
Brussels, because if the navigation of our convoys were far enough advanced to be stopped at Maastricht, a courier could
be sent to me, on whose arrival I would press harder. From what I have reported to you of my conversation with Madame
the Governess, you see that it is to her that we owe the difficulty being made for us. The Stadholderians are taking
advantage of it to ruin the Grand Pensionary and to destroy, if possible, the Republican party. That party is neither
as firm nor as united as could be wished. Count Wassenaer has not yet been willing to come back here, and his younger
son at present has a post in Friesland whose appointment depends on Madame the Governess. The passport granted to
Mr von Reischach is the argument I use most. It is unanswerable, and I hope that at the beginning of next week I may be
able to send your courier back to you with an answer such as we must demand, and to which Their High Mightinesses, or
at least part of those who represent them, will have had the skill to add ill grace, revealing a fund of ill will. At
four in the afternoon I was in conference, and I handed over the ( ). It was taken at once to the States General. I
know that it was not badly received, and I have reason to believe that Holland will give an opinion such as it should
be. I believe that this very evening I shall even have Mr de Byemont, who is to bring me the consent of Their High
Mightinesses for the passage of Namur. As there is as yet no news that our boats have arrived there, I do not doubt that
we shall have time to get our answer about Maastricht before our boats are under the walls of that place. I am all the
more pleased to have sent the warning that had been communicated to me, as it appears that the disguised Prussian party
has completely failed in its enterprise. I would even believe that some of these gentlemen have been arrested, who will
probably be hanged, and who well deserve it. I have just received a report, and from a good source, which says that the
town and citadel of Wesel have been entirely abandoned by the Prussians, and that they have even begun to demolish the
fortifications. This morning there was a conference at Mr Yorke's, attended by the commissioners of the posts, to
establish the correspondence from here to Hanover and to Berlin, since they can no longer let it go by Emmerich; but if
we wish, the East Frisian route will not withdraw the correspondence from our inspections. I enclose a recommendation of
the States General on behalf of some of those interested in the prize taken by the privateer Lalley. I am persuaded
that this recommendation will not be heeded, and I believe that never was a prize more legitimate. I shall send to
Mr de Moras in the next few days a memorandum that I believe no better founded than this one. It concerns some
Englishmen established at Rotterdam who carry on an immense trade in our produce, and above all our wines, with England.
If their ships meet some French privateer, they show them certificates of domicile in Holland. If they meet English
privateers, they produce baptismal certificates of that nation and letters that open the ports of England to them,
in accordance with the Treaty of 1652; which is truly an intolerable abuse.
