## Translation

Lwów, the twenty-seventh of June 1711. Faithful, sincerely beloved to us. We suppose that our letters in answer to
[…] […] your letters, already […], have been delivered to you, and what they [?contain], and from the annexes [?of
them] […] fully […] in what hinge our affairs will turn. To these we therefore add what was lacking then, namely a
copy of the treaty of the so-called Peace of Charles [Szatmár], which the Viennese court now calls Points of
[?Submission], together with [?remarks] which will be able to show you and the allies the [?hidden] poison, because of
which we did not consider it consistent with our obligation to our country to accept them. Nor were we mistaken in
our opinion: for what a certain minister writes [?in Vienna] we [?have heard] by these, and from all this we hope that
the allied Powers too will recognise the just and fair reasons for which we refused to put the last hand to [?concluding]
that fraudulent negotiation, subject to the mind of the Emperor. And just as in our said letters we sent you [?…] the
letters sent to [?General] Pálffy before the [?capitulation] of the [?armistice], so now we also communicate a copy of
those written [?last], which were indeed handed to him into his own hands; but the courier came back with an answer
refused. Meanwhile it is certain that the Palatine has proclaimed Archduke Charles king in all the counties of
Hungary, against the manifest rights and liberties of the Kingdom, which prescribe that such a proclamation must be
made, minds [?first] being pacified, in a general Diet of the Kingdom. And the very article of the Diet of Pozsony of
the year 1687, annexed here in copy, will show them fully what it prescribes must be done before the proclamation and
recognition of a King.

Those who will judge our affairs without prejudice and with a sound conscience will recognise, we hope, that we cannot
recognise a King proclaimed outside a diet, who has not first taken his oath in the due manner, without the [?total]
ruin of our liberties; which indeed would not be harmed or annihilated so much by the claim of hereditary succession,
and lest perhaps even future ones [?in] [?recognition], as by this act, by which the nation would yield its right of
prescribing conditions to its kings and of binding them by oath to them. And therefore, the Tsar of Muscovy and the
King of Poland being here, we have pressed the Polish Republic, assembled in a Senate council in its own way, to
recognise our declaration as just, its [?mediation] being resumed; by means of which [declaration] we affirmed that we
are willing to desist from hostilities until such time as it is established by public acts that our liberties and
laws have been [?restored] anew. We promised moreover that, given sufficient security, we would appear at the
proclamation of a free diet either in person or through our plenipotentiaries, and would recognise as King him who
has been lawfully recognised and crowned, and would swear the oath to him. We therefore ask that, this declaration of
ours being made, all who persevere in our confederation be restored in full, and our goods, and that meanwhile for
the security of our persons we, living in Poland, may [?hold (them)]. We have pressed, as was said, that the Powers
mentioned above will bring this about, which they have also entrusted to their ministers to work on; and the Republic
has sanctioned our security and the reconciliation of peace by an express statute of the Senate council.

From the copy, added to your letters, of the letter of the general estates written to the widowed reigning Empress,
we see indeed with great sorrow the precipitate recognition of the Archduke as King of Hungary, where we would rather
have hoped that all the aforesaid, from the acts of the mediation of the negotiations [?at Nagyszombat] […], ought to
have been fully [?informed] that […] they could not, for the even-handedness of their mediation, recognise a King
proclaimed in Hungary without observing the premises. For we hoped [?that by this] […] [?they] [?were bound] to the
restoration of our liberties, in which they often promised they wished to maintain us, and that no stronger means
could be found to help us than the delay of this recognition until the execution of those things which they
acknowledged ought justly to be done for the common good and for the much-desired security of the religions in
Hungary. Meanwhile, moreover, we believe we may suspect that the so often repeated assurances of the allies about
restoring and consolidating our liberties were not sincere enough, but that they only aimed at this: that, Hungarian
affairs being pacified in any way, they would [?bring] a diversion of the arms of the House of Austria. We therefore
judge it necessary that you show them fully that those who believe we have no means left can easily be wrong in their
opinion. But [as to] the continuation of the war [?which will follow] in case of the oppression of the nation, and
those who imagine that the nation is so estranged from us that […] of thousands of faithless men could thus better be
wished for, had we not with our consent soothed them, who [?believe] they have now been deceived.

And indeed at this time they will take up again the spirit of claiming their liberty, unless it is so secured that it
makes both our minds and theirs tranquil. The designs of the Viennese court have been largely revealed by the
continuation of Count Pálffy's hostility against our castle of Munkács. Our declaration, and the one made by our order
to the garrison, which we add to the present, being disregarded, notwithstanding which he continued his hostilities
below the castle, brought cannon against it and, forcing it to surrender against all laws, occupied that castle by
force from us. Let the allies therefore not wonder that in all things we [?have judged] fit for preserving our
liberties [we] wish to proceed securely, and they would keep us in [our] state […] also maintain, [?we can], unless
they have supplied us as soon as possible with the means of securing them through a firm and stable security, [?than]
which a better can hardly be supplied than through the restoration of the liberty of Transylvania, which in the
present conjuncture the Archduke ought not to make difficulties over, so that, the minds of the Hungarians being won
and bound entirely, he could apply his army according to the necessity and exigency of his affairs.

[?If] [?therefore] you yourself, because of your state of health, could not continue your journey to Holland, you are
to communicate this without delay, as the need of our interest requires, to those who are well disposed and to whom
it shall be necessary. You are to send [?the same] views to the King of Poland's Commissary General, a man named
Spiegel, who is here in Poland, and to write them out as soon as possible in [?letters] directed through Saxony, so
that we can adapt the present conjuncture, whether tending to peace (if a suitable one can be made) or to war, to our
affairs and the interest of our country. Further we announce what [?happier] news our correspondents have brought of
the progress of the Muscovite arms, from which it can firmly be hoped that that war will be finished with easy labour,
as the Tsar desires. As for your expenses, we believe it is fully clear to you what we have so far disapproved; [you
are] to adapt yourself in future too to the state of affairs and of the times, whose straits restrict us as well.
Trust least of all [?letters] and information from private persons, unless they come directly from us; for in the
present circumstances it can hardly be decided whom one should trust. Ráday ought long ago, by our order, to have
[?written] to Lord Jabłonowski […] about the indiscreet way of proceeding of the Royal Grand Chancellor, assuming that
you had continued your journey to Holland; but at the same time we received letters of the said Grand Chancellor
written from Dukla, in which he notifies that he does not want to keep the wines once [?accepted] still at vintage
time, the original receipts having been issued about them and being held by us, but to leave them in the care of a
certain Jew, to be handed over to our deputed man when he comes.

But since we believe that we have satisfied [the matter] entirely by [the decision] of our Economic Council issued on
it […], the wines having been consigned to the custody of his own man from the time the receipts were issued, carried
at our expense to the appointed place and left in his custody, if any damage has befallen the royal cellar through
the carelessness of the keepers, we do not consider ourselves answerable for it. For the wines, the money advanced to
Hegyi of the [?Council] of the Realm's Economy [is to be claimed] from him to whom he can claim [it] as owed beyond our
orders; for he, [?having committed] a breach of faith with the others, has turned the money to his own uses, and is
not at all subject to our authority. For the rest we remain benignly inclined to you with our princely favour. Lwów,
27 June, year 1711. Francis, Prince.

We are astonished that no mention is made in your letters of the mathematical instruments and other things ordered
from Mangold, for whose payment your assignation too has been dispatched; we wish to enjoin you seriously that all
those things which we ordered to be made there be carried out without delay and sent here.
