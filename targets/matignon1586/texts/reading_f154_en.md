# f. 154r–v, Forget to Villeroy, after Easter 1586: English translation

Translated from the reading (`reading_f154.md`), which follows the contemporary clear copy on f. 157r. `(?)` marks a
word read with doubt.

## f. 154r

[You saw] what difficulty we were then in over the payment of the two musters that had been held for our soldiers,
and it has since grown much greater. Because the payments could not follow the musters as quickly as usual, some
captains put it into the soldiers' heads that they were to be cheated, and a mutiny in the Spanish manner nearly
broke out among them, with language and oaths(?) as outrageous as can be, to the point of wanting to abandon their
guard posts and threatening to furl the colours. Monsieur du Mayne [Mayenne] is in such distress that I assure you
it is pitiful, in constant fear of seeing some great disorder break out in this army, and with no means of providing
against it as quickly as the occasion requires(?). And since those who had promised to lend us money here, to be
repaid in Paris, when we sent the Treasurer's clerk to them with our bonds and promises, have raised new difficulties
and declared that they would not lend a single écu unless I bound myself alone in my own name, and unless I had it
promised to them by word of mouth that the money would be repaid to them in Paris on sight, I am forced to stay here
for this reason; and I see well that I shall not get away without being bound for more than forty thousand écus in
my own name. That will be taken for pure folly by anyone who considers the times we live in, in which there may

## f. 154v

befall such a mishap as would ruin me from top to bottom. But the fear of a still greater misfortune makes it count
for less with me. I have been obliged to draw bills of exchange for all these sums on Monsieur Castille. I beg you,
Monsieur, to give orders that they be paid, and I can assure you I would not have done it so readily but for what is
in one of your despatches, where you tell us we should try to raise as much money on exchange as we can.

Monsieur, it is no longer a matter of making predictions, but I cannot hide from you that unless this army is
otherwise provided for, by the end of next month there will not be two thousand men left together. For they say
openly that the army of Dauphiné is paid every month and that this one is the out-of-favour army, with talk so ugly
that I cannot repeat it to you. And I can tell you that those held to be the wisest are, in this, almost less so
than the others.
