# R163, the Secretariat of State to the nuncio in Madrid, Rome, 2 April 1718, second copy (Spagna 364D/10, ff. 181r-183v): English translation

## Translation

If the King persisted in the design of disturbing the peace of Christendom and of diverting the Christian arms engaged
against the Turk, and much more if the Emperor justified with authentic documents the accusation made against Spain
and Cardinal Alberoni, as His Majesty for that matter intends to do, Your Most Illustrious Lordship sees well that the
Pope could not limit himself to the mere prayers, complaints and threats made in the letters written in clear. His own
duty would oblige him to pass to deeds, and first of all to suspend the effect of those graces which the Supreme
Pontiffs his predecessors granted to the Catholic Kings for the sole and only purpose of supplying them with means
sufficient to keep the war against the Infidels alive at sea. And in fact the concessions of the Cruzada, of the
subsidy, of the millones openly declare the end the Holy See had in granting them, binding them also to the
fulfilment of various conditions, such as keeping fifty [4171] etc. in service against the Infidels. Now, if Spain,
instead of making war on the Infidels, were to contribute to their advantage, either indirectly by hindering the
progress of the Christian princes against them, or even directly by acting in concert with the Infidels themselves to
the Emperor's harm, how could Our Lord, without betraying his own conscience and without making an indelible stain on
his Pontificate, not put into action all his authority so that the use and proceeds of the said graces, given to
keep a powerful squadron at sea to the harm of the common enemy, should not be turned to the fitting out and
maintenance of a maritime [?armament] destined to harm the Emperor, diverting him from pursuing his victories against
the Turk? The Emperor, who [...] the Pope to fulfil what he promised him in the Brief of 16 February 1716; how then
can the Pope withdraw from doing for so great an object at least what, even apart from the engagement contracted, he
ought to do at the prompting of his own duty? Our Lord knows that this threat will seem very harsh there; it is fitting,
however, that you handle it with delicacy, but in such a way as to make the King, and the Cardinal principally,
understand that the Pope will not be able to excuse himself from carrying it out if that Court does not yield to Our
Lord's loving insinuations, prayers and instances. [4322] much harsher for the Pope the failure done to him last year;
and the fact that not the least account was made of it there, with an [?intolerable] contempt, and that its fatal
consequences for the good of Christendom and for the reputation of the Vicar of Christ are seen growing every day, put
Our Lord under the necessity either of exacting a prom[pt] and immediate reparation for it in the form indicated, or of
making an effect of it [...] [4312] by Your Most Illustrious Lordship that a good success of this despatch of ours
would put an end to all the complaints that have been made about you, and would fully restore you to the possession
of that particular esteem and love with which Our Lord has regarded you, who would let you feel its effects even in
the face of any contradiction.

The principal aim of this despatch of ours, and the object of all Our Lord's concern, tend to turning against the
Infidels that powerful armament which Spain is preparing against Italy. If this is obtained, Your Most Illustrious
Lordship may well be persuaded that every possible facility will be found for the settlement of the other pending
matters; and the merit that Cardinal Alberoni would gain from it, to whom the principal glory would justly be
attributed, would destroy in an instant any sinister impression formed against him, and would remove all the
obstacles that keep His Holiness from continuing towards him the demonstrations of his love. It is certain that
without this Our Lord will never be able to bring himself to grant new graces to a person who, for being at the head of
the affairs of that monarchy, is considered the author of all the present upheaval and of the harm that comes from it
to Christendom and to the public pea[ce] ...
