# R167, the Secretariat of State to the nuncio in Madrid, Rome, 31 May 1718 (Spagna 364D/14, ff. 302r-312r): English translation

## Translation

There were two principal commissions which our despatch of the 2nd of April last, sent to Your Most Illustrious
Lordship by the extraordinary courier, brought you. The first, the more essential, and for every reason closest to
Our Lord's heart, concerned acting so that that Court should not only not divert, directly or indirectly, the progress
of the Christian arms against the Turk, but should moreover dispose itself to turn against him those forces which
seemed destined to harm the Emperor, I declaring to Your Most Illustrious Lordship expressly what the Pope's resolute
determination would be if the strong remonstrances you were to make on this subject had not had the desired effect.
The other concerned both the difficulties that still delayed the proposal of the Church of Seville in favour of
Cardinal Alberoni and the means proportioned to cutting them short. I wish to believe, and must well believe, that
Your Most Illustrious Lordship, driven by your zeal for Our Lord's good service, and giving with your spirit all its
force to the most powerful reasons I then set out to you at length, will have omitted no step to carry out the said
commissions fruitfully. Nevertheless I have not been able to find in your despatch of the 12th of this month, with
the return of the same extraordinary courier, with what replies it was sought there to give at least some apparent
vent to our most convincing remonstrances. I have said "to give vent to our remonstrances", and not indeed to justify
the strange and perhaps unheard-of conduct which that Court has held towards the Pope on this occasion, because it does
not truly seem that any colour of justice can be given to it.

His Holiness deigns to write a letter in his own hand to the King, not stinging or harsh, but full of fatherly
expressions, and directed solely to procuring the good of Christendom and His Majesty's true glory, exhorting and
beseeching him not to divert, but rather to second and promote the progress of the Christian arms against the common
enemy; and yet it is refused, and that regard which is commonly had for similar letters of princes, even among those
actually at war with each other, is denied by a Catholic King to a letter of the Pope, who not only is not actually at
war with him, but, after having been gravely offended by him, receives his letters with kindness and freely admits the
royal minister to audience. Nay, as if such ill usage were not enough, the Apostolic Nuncio is forbidden access to the
royal apartments, and terrible threats are added of new outrages against the Church and new insults to the Holy See,
spread abroad here too by Cardinal Acquaviva with unbearable contempt of the Pontifical majesty. And for a treatment so
little conformable to the laws of all propriety, let alone to the King's piety, no other cause is brought than that of
wishing by it to show resentment and indignation against the Pope because he does not refuse, but only delays, the
translation of a bishop from one Church to another; to which, besides the princes having no right at all, the Pope
himself, according to the canonical dispositions, must not consent without strong and just reasons. I confess the
truth that such a manner of proceeding has no less surprised than embittered the Pontifical mind; for, however strong
the King's engagement for obtaining the grace may be, Our Lord could never suppose that he would insist on it in ways so
improper and diametrically opposed to the grace requested. For if one will weigh its nature with the scales of the
Sanctuary, it is easy to understand that violence and ill usage constitute no degree of merit for obtaining it, either
in the one who requests it or in the one who is to enjoy its fruit; unless one wishes to do the Pope this further
enormous wrong of believing him so forgetful of his duty as not to have courage to resist every violence so as not to
betray his conscience and not leave an indelible stain on his Pontificate. Do not wonder at the force with which I
write to you on this incident, for it is still much less than that with which Our Lord expressed himself to me, who
wishes moreover that Your Most Illustrious Lordship convey to the King, by whatever ways seem most proper to you, his
bitter complaints, hoping that the royal piety has not grown so lukewarm that His Majesty [...] hear with indifference
what so much concerns his filial respect for the Vicar of Christ, and also the strictest and most due proprieties of one
prince towards another.

Returning then, after this painful but alas necessary digression, to the examination of the said two commissions, I
have little to tell you of the first, on which I fully informed you of the Pontifical sentiments with my same despatch
of the second of April. It will suffice that you be content to recall its contents to mind, so as to have the means to
make known there ever more the obligation the Pope is under, both by reason of his ministry and by the special
engagement he contracted with the Emperor through the Brief of 16 February 1716, of which a copy is sent you, to make
use of every most effective means to remove any diversion that might be made to his victories against the Turk. And
because this is a most important point, on which you must never cease to insist, also so as to remove, if possible,
every pretext for the Emperor to cut short the course of his progress in Hungary with some untimely peace or truce,
which, as you well see, would be nothing but a deplorable effect of the fatal move of the Spanish arms against
Sardinia, and could [...] horrible consequences: besides what I wrote to you under the 2nd of April, special
consideration is deserved by the express word given by the King to the Pope with his letter of the 25th of November
1715, that he would exactly observe the neutrality of Italy and not disturb the states possessed by the Emperor during
the war His Imperial Majesty might have undertaken against the Turk. From the copy of that same letter, which I send
you annexed, Your Most Illustrious Lordship will recognise the truth of what I put forward to you; and if perchance it
should be said that the King is to be held free of the word given because the Emperor did not expressly reciprocate
the engagement, reply, Your Most Illustrious Lordship, that this is not enough to release that Court from it; since
that of Vienna on its side, having observed everything that His Catholic Majesty claimed from it in return, has by deeds
exactly fulfilled the condition literally required in the said royal letter, and fully satisfied what could have been
claimed from it if it had reciprocated even in the most solemn form. Nay, let Your Most Illustrious Lordship have it
well reflected that not only was the engagement taken by the King with the Pope later than all those alleged
infractions by the Court of Vienna of the Treaty of the neutrality of Italy which the Spanish ministers have set out in
public manifestos to colour Spain's move, excepting only the arrest of Monsignor Molines (which, for the reflections so
many [?times] made, could never give any just reason to kindle so great a fire), which would abundantly suffice to
oblige the King not to fail in the said engagement; but moreover, in his own letter, His Majesty expressly declared
that he contracted it and wished to observe it notwithstanding the justified reasons to the contrary which he might
have had because of the many failures that had occurred up to that time on the Emperor's part.

The other commission consisted in making well understood there the difficulty by which His Holiness was obliged to
defer Cardinal Alberoni's translation to the Church of Seville. To this I do not find, either in your despatch of the
2nd of this month or in the long letter written me by Father Daubenton, that any other answer was given than a
consulta made by the Council of Castile to the King, and approved by His Majesty, to proceed to every greater extremity
against the Holy See if the said Church is not proposed at once. And the title on which so strange a resolution is
founded lies in the claim (these are the words of a letter of Your Most Illustrious Lordship of the 2nd of May) that
the refusal is an offence done to the royal dignity and to the right it holds of presenting to vacant bishoprics persons
who cannot be rejected except when there is a stain in matters of religion and morals. What I have written to Your
Most Illustrious Lordship these five months manifestly convicts this whole foundation of being baseless and most false.
In the first place it is false that the Pope refuses to make the translation; he only suspends it until he is in a
position to second the royal wishes with honour and in conscience; and both in writing to you and in speaking here to
Cardinal Acquaviva, nay in the very act of the counter-protest made by Monsignor Auditor, the same language has always
been held. It is false besides that in the present case the royal right of presentation can in any way be said to be
injured and prejudiced, because it is a matter of a translation, for which, as has been said so many [?times], and it
does not seem possible that it can be contested there, no right belongs to that Court; since perhaps no Catholic author
will be found who asserts that a prince can have the right to nominate or present an incapable person, such as one who
is bound to another Church, nor to oblige the Pope to transfer him from one Church to another. Hence it is that at
[?times] the Popes have [?had] so great a repugnance to translations that they have rarely been induced to them, and
then not without examining well whether the requisites prescribed by the sacred Canons concurred; and Innocent XI of
holy memory, otherwise so inclined to favour Spain, as everyone knows, had for a long time a very grave scruple about
admitting them in Spain; and yet no protests or threats were heard then, as if the royal prerogatives were being
violated; nay, that Court refrained for almost three years from making any instance about it. It is false, then, in
our case, that the Pope can reject only persons who have a stain in matters of religion or morals, since every bishop,
however excellent, can freely be rejected by the Pope when he is nominated or presented for another Church. If then
the fact on which the said consulta rests holds in no part, how can the Pope ever believe that His Majesty, well
instructed of its truth, can and must conform to it? If the thing were doubtful, or at least claimed by those very
Spanish authors who most enlarge the royal prerogatives, it would in truth be condemnable that the King should uphold it
by violent ways; what then will it be when no doubt falls upon it, nor has anyone ever claimed that the right princes
have to present or nominate extends to translations? If, then, one wishes to place reason in pure violence, and to
pursue the request for a mere grace by those ways which should not be taken with the Vicar of Christ even where all
justice were on one's side, there is no remedy. Let His Majesty only consider, besides the grave stain that will result
from it to his conscience, the blame he will incur in the Catholic world for a rupture with the Holy See that has for
its basis only resentment at the mere deferment of a simple grace. And let Cardinal Alberoni consider whether that same
rupture will make it easier, or rather harder, for him, in this and in other Pontificates, to be Archbishop of Seville.

His Holiness has not, however, let himself be so carried away by the indignation conceived at the harsh answer given
to Your Most Illustrious Lordship by that ministry, as not to have weighed at the same time certain expressions with
which both Your Most Illustrious Lordship and Father Daubenton assure me that the decree made by the Governor of
Catalonia for the expulsion of the Bishop of Vich had been partly disapproved there, and that Cardinal Alberoni did not
care for the administration of the mensa of Tarragona. In fact Our Lord hoped that, with the express that reached
Cardinal Acquaviva on the very day on which the one you sent back to me returned, the appropriate instructions had come
to His Eminence to satisfy us on those same points. His Holiness was [?however] surprised when, the Cardinal having been
kindly admitted to audience, he heard his discourse restricted to new and ever stronger insistences on the proposal of
Seville, without a word of any prior satisfaction; nay, when His Eminence was questioned about it by Our Lord on the
basis of the hint given us by you and Father Daubenton, he answered frankly that there was no substance in it, since
the Governor of Catalonia had declared the Church of Vich vacant according to the express royal orders he held about
it; and as to Tarragona, that Cardinal Alberoni was ready to leave its administration after his translation to the
Church of Seville had taken place. These same sentiments the same Cardinal has since confirmed to me several times,
and has declared moreover that they were entirely in conformity with the orders he had received from there,
notwithstanding that I judged it expedient, in order to come to some discussion on the matter, to put before him the
very letters written to me by you and Father Daubenton. So manifest a contradiction has cut off for us every way of
agreeing on those means which must necessarily precede the longed-for grace. I said precede, because, Cardinal
Acquaviva having let slip that the Pope should propose the Church of Seville and the satisfactions would be discussed
afterwards, it has in substance been recognised that the whole aim is to engage the Pope to do what is desired of him,
without taking much trouble about what is owed to him. Now let Your Most Illustrious Lordship see whether His Holiness,
well warned by last year's failure, can and must, as the rules of all prudence [require], trust positive words, let
alone vague intentions, and not demand beforehand that the words and intentions be carried out. In the end the Cardinal
proposed that the satisfactions we claim about the affairs of Vich and Tarragona be [?set out] in writing; nor would
this have been a remedy, even if the difference and contradiction between his orders and what Your Most Illustrious
Lordship and Father Daubenton write to us had not advised us to have a pending matter handled there, through you and
the said Father Daubenton, whose good success they already have so much in hand. For the rest, I say again that His
Holiness can ask no less than the full reinstatement of the Bishop of Vich, with the revocation of all the acts done
on the occasion of his expulsion, and that the administration of the mensa of Tarragona be restored to him to whom it
had been committed by Apostolic authority. Nor should such a demand seem harsh, since, if Your Most Illustrious
Lordship has Cardinal Alberoni weigh well what I wrote to you at length on this matter in the mentioned despatch of the
2nd of April, he will see that the circumstances accompanying these two outrages put Our Lord under [?sheer] necessity
of exacting an entire reparation for them, and that the facilities used at other times [...] the affairs of Tarragona
and Solsona cannot be adduced as an example, because in substance these have served only to multiply the offences
against us. Taking up again, then, our despatch of the 2nd of April, and considering the present one, it seems that
Your Most Illustrious Lordship is well instructed of the mind of Our Lord, who, notwithstanding the great ill usage and
insults, desires nothing else but that the King, by helping, or at least not diverting, the continuation of the war in
Hungary against the Turk, and by giving beforehand the due reparation for the affairs of Vich and Tarragona, should put
Our Lord in a position to be able, without burdening his own conscience and without prejudice to the Pontifical
dignity, to propose the Church of Seville promptly for Cardinal Alberoni. As to the news Your Most Illustrious Lordship
has given us about the Treaty of peace, I assure you that it has been most welcome to Our Lord, who desires its
continuation.

Our Lord can never persuade himself that the King could carry out the most unjust consulta made to him by the Council
of Castile; but if ever God, as a punishment to us and to Spain, should permit so great an excess, and consequently
proceeding were made to the expulsion of Your Most Illustrious Lordship from those kingdoms, you may set out in this
direction, so as to be in a position to enter Rome once the danger of the air is past.
