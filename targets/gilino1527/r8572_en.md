## Translation

I am assured by many that [?His] Lordship stands in little favour with the Emperor for one single thing that is
imputed to him, and on account of two letters of his, intercepted, to the Viceroy, that he should send the galleys of
Naples to the relief of Genoa and order Lope de Soria to use the rest of the hundred thousand ducats of the exchange
for the benefit of that city, both in provision of cash and of victuals, since otherwise the whole would be lost.
[?His] detractors, who openly are the confessor and [?the German] secretary, will have it that Lautrec, on such
information, sent part of the troops [?…] to the siege of Genoa which were destined for another place, and that for
this the said city [?…] was no longer defended, [?for] the great need that I see His Majesty has of him. I cannot
think but that he will keep himself in the Emperor's good favour, and for this I shall not cease to urge «Your
Excellency» to order the Bilia by your letters to keep on good terms with him. Now we cannot hope for anything but
good offi[c]es from His Lordship, above all with the death of Bourbon. I have resolved to go to meet him as far as
three days' journey, and I know that for this I shall not be held suspect by the confederate ambassadors.

The ambassador of the King of Portugal, newly come in the Pope's name, is not sent principally by His Holiness,
[?…], as the Datary writes to the Lord Nuncio; but as he was about to go on to [?his] King, he begged Our Lord that it
might please him [to honour him] with the honour of a brief in [?his] [?…] person to the Emperor, with authority to
negotiate his settlement, promising to have him released at once and restore him to his former friendship with His
Majesty and to the ancient state of the Church; which has not come about for him as he had promised, and [as] said not
only in [?Italy] but also in Spain. Here he has been known for a light man; and with this good opinion of himself he
leaves the Imperial court tomorrow for Portugal. This ambassador has brought a letter in the [?Pope]'s own hand to the
Nuncio, as credence for [?Saporito], chamberlain of His Holiness, [?who] died in Genoa, «so wisely and prudently
written that hardly anything more could be wished», exhorting him not to fail in his due service; likewise two briefs
to the Emperor, full of many fine sentences, and with fine reasons it persuades His Majesty to take up the patronage
of the Church and not to oppress it, above all sub fide, although unlearnedly written.

The Abbatino returns despatched as he desires; the Emperor has granted him all he knew how to desire and ask. It is
true that on many heads, since His Majesty does not know of what moment they are, he writes to the Viceroy of Naples,
as better informed of the affairs of Italy, that he should satisfy the [?aforesaid] Marquis, as far as he can, for such
is his mind. «No particular have I been able to learn beyond what is already written; [?…] at the first opportunity I
shall advise Your Excellency of it; hold this for certain» that the settlement has followed with this Majesty,
although he had come to terms with [?…] [?…], as we have news here that he was negotiating it through[h] [?…] the
Cavalier Casale. The Emperor has given his mind to this settlement through the reminders of the Duke of Ferrara and
the work of the Nuncio. I believe, however, that the Marquis, being obliged to the Florentines, will not show himself
wholly in favour of the Emperor, but will see to it that Pagolo Luzzasco with three hundred light horse passes into
the service of His Majesty, as [?yesterday] the aforesaid Abbatino, where [?…] to leave for your parts in all haste,
and by him I have written to Your Excellency under cover of the Lady Marchioness of Mantua.

Both have also sworn to me that the Most Christian [King] lately writes to them that he absolutely wants Your
Excellency to remain in the state of Milan in the settlement, which does not agree with what he wrote to them lately;
none[the]less [?I] leave it to the truth. The Lord Venetian ambassador and I alone, the day before yesterday, were
with the Lord A[po]stolic Nuncio at [?Usiglios], and begged him to be willing to go to Palencia and stay there while
the Fre[n]ch ambassadors stay, and to use diligence to learn the truth of what they will negotiate, and above all
concerning that matter. He promised us to do it willingly, and so we understand that His Lordship has gone there; and
I am almost certain that he will serve us, both out of respect for Your Excellency, and for what he owes to Italy
[?…], and for the friendship he holds with the said Venetian ambassador.

I understand also that the Emperor, as soon as he heard of the surrender of Genoa, sent a [?new] despatch to
Monseigneur de [?Miliao] [?…], who until that time had not embarked for Rome; and it is hinted to me that His Majesty
is revoking the liberation of the Pope because of the new events in Lombardy. What leads [?me] to believe this are
the bad offices which the Ferrarese ambassadors and the agents of the Colonna publicly do against His Holiness with
the Emperor; of whom, when they speak of him, they cannot name him except as traitor, false pope, bastard, maker
[?of] poisons, and with a thousand other insults which it would be long to write. With him, I am told, they will
despatch Troilo Negrono, and that His Majesty [?…] now says he wants to grant many favours to the Lords Colonna; and
this is the present difficulty of the affairs of Lombardy, and has caused it; therefore they fear that after the one
ruin the other, of the kingdom of Naples, may follow.

There are here letters of Messer Andrea Borgo [?to] the Emperor and Valdes, [?…] formerly his [?servant], full of
counsels pro opprimenda Italia [for crushing Italy] and long discourses for keeping the state of Milan in the
devotion of His Majesty. [?…] At the end they conclude that the Infante [?is] the one who can hold it and defend it
from any violence that [?…] [?might come] to occupy it. After the taking of Genoa it is said that the Emperor has sent
word to have the Infante come down, and [?to give] all [?Italy] as a prey to the Germans; and His Majesty shows greater
heart than ever, and that the conf[ess]or [?…] has come out [with it]: that the loss of Genoa has been a strong spur to
the Emperor; therefore he should show himself wholly bent on war; and how [?powerful] [?…] [?forces] are [?…]. [?…]
here by several ways [?…] I have been advised [that] the Emperor h[a]s sent by three couriers the investiture of the
state of Milan to the Infante, [?…] for the defence of that state, and that at present he is providing him with a
hundred thousand ducats. The loss of Genoa had been [?confirmed] by way of the Lord of Monaco, whom His Majesty is
providing with money so that he may keep himself in his devotion.
