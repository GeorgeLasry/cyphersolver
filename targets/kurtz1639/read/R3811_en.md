# R3811, Kurz von Senftenau to Trauttmansdorff, Hamburg, 17 Jan 1639

Most high-born Lord Count, gracious Lord,

In Duke Georg's affairs I cannot quite find my way. The enemy is across without resistance; against him [there are]
so many troops, though with rotten muskets, such as [...] did at that time, because one [hears] of neutralities here;
and here the failure to resist gives all the more suspicion. Whether it happens for lack of strength, and will be used
at its time and place hereabouts, remains to be seen. To defend the Elbe and the Weser is impossible for the Duke, and
they also take our subsidies for little friendship. I would wish Salis had better regiments with him, that could show
themselves to friend and foe. [...] whether among them some are not still reformed, which yet will not come up; the
part of the Lower Saxon Circle we must not let out of our possession, [and must] forbid the enemy to cross; to make
the quarters in Silesia easy is [done] with dexterity, having collected a couple of thousand thalers from the
Lüneburgers, and the unmounted [men] make a raid into Farther Pomerania. It will happen, but too late.

The gazettes say recruits are destined for Austria. With little money one can recruit here, and they would gladly run
to Bohemia. Double [...] is the [...], because what one recruits there is lost. The dukes who go over to the enemy
compete with ours in suffering hunger, and are already brought down; no one denounces himself to stand by all the
plans. All money is lost that does not come at the right time, and entirely: a thaler of grain is worth hardly
anything in six months. NB. From Count Franzen I still have no answer. If I meet His Majesty, perhaps he will be
commissioned to deal with me in Polish affairs. I make little progress all the same with the dukes, only what [I]
saw at the King's, [...] and to forward the Holstein [...] envoys' journey to Lüneburg directly. The chief cause is
that France, Sweden and Holland are about to send the King a considerable embassy and dissuade him from pressing so
hard for the congress in Sweden and thereby weakening the alliances of all three; no doubt threats go with it.
Therefore [...] shall have been demolished at the King's instance, and the toll stopped.

They add: Duke August of Saxe-Lauenburg wants to put himself under Danish protection, and the [?Stecknitz]
[?position] be garrisoned with Danish troops; thus Holstein is better and more easily defended on another's ground.
The rest remains to be seen.

Arnim was with me yesterday, [and] reported [...] that he grieves that he should be suspected by His Imperial Majesty,
as if he had been [part] of the Friedland treason, and had cooperated in it. [He] knew officers who pursued him, but
with orders from above. When at [?…] in the greatest danger, and so also not assured of the Emperor's grace. With all
this it was not the intention of his lord the Elector at the past [...] understanding to ruin a single house, least of
all the Archducal House of Austria, but that he, Arnim, himself was put into his instruction when he was with
Friedland, in case [...] treason was [...] by him, [and] to imagine the like. If [?Friedland] were disloyal to His
Imperial Majesty, the Elector could expect much less from him; that may be negotiated with him, [and with it] the
exceedingly great plenipotentiary power [...]. He asked that I should ask the [?Elector] for information on it; and if
His Imperial Majesty, informed of it, changed his protocols: at the late Emperor's [time] the Elector had resolved
that he, Arnim, should keep quiet, and against peace and war the Elector had cause [and] would let slip the occasion
of negotiations, machinate nothing against the Peace of Prague, [and] he should be assured of imperial protection
here.

I have assured him of the same with [...] His Imperial Majesty against the performance of his duty, and sent him away
content. On my advice one gives him a protectorium in the best form and binds him; he is after all within the
amnesty, unless Friedland matters are proved. And how will one raise this, and with what convenience? He will get
arms again, either for us or against us. England and France court him here, the Swedes too; he says he wants to go
away from here to avoid all suspicions. He asked me for a pass to Prussia. How much better a protectorium from His
Imperial [...] Majesty? Whether he behaves according to it remains to be seen.

Well invested. For the rest I gladly do my part with Denmark. [?Arnim] [...]: Denmark could not stay out of the game.
We have rather watched fortune and misfortune through the window. Stockholm would be in his hands if he only wanted
to make war; Sweden would lose Pomerania [...] for it. He thinks that by way of the Circle's arrangement [...], so
that the enemy is pursued and driven [...] imperial soil, [it] could best be brought into play. The Swedes do not
distinguish between the [?Duke] of Holstein and the King of Denmark, and the royal answer to the Swedes said so when
he wrote back lately: he, as a Duke of Holstein and a prince of the Empire, could not look on at the business any
longer; to defend the contrary; calamities [...], and whoever could have peace, as the examples of others deter him.
But he holds to his opinion, and the discussion rests there.

Hamburg, 17 January 1639.
