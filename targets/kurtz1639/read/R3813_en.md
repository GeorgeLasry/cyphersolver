# R3813, Kurz von Senftenau to Trauttmansdorff, Hamburg, 27 January 1639

High-born Lord Count, gracious lord.

I am still waiting for Herr Pfenz, who is hiding here; Wol[...] and my people are at Lüneburg; with the English king's [envoy] there is nothing [to be done]; the following of the Danes and Holsteiners was no bridge and no security. The enemy is coming over here quite unhindered and is moving up towards Salzwedel, so that the [?troops] under Salis and the artillery in the [?assigned] [?quarters] will not stay safe, or at least can [?withdraw]. Apart from Meirer's regiment nothing more has arrived here. Note: at the first crossing Winckel was in command, at the other Meirer.

On that, the offers we have made him have not moved him either, for the danger he may rightly fear under Swedish domination. If the [...] of Mainz should be [able] in its time to remedy [things] more surely than now, when fortune is with us. Duke Georg of the [Lower Saxon] lands lets himself be harried beyond all royal warning, [?willing] or unwilling; cum post oblatas suppetias, a Caesare, a rege [after help was offered by the Emperor and by the King], he can and will not have the directorate of the [?Circle's] [?constitution] given to him either. We know whether he [...] him rightly at all times. I lately saw in a letter in which he reproaches him that his warnings were communicated to Salvius. Magdeburg will not agree to the matter; Halberstadt is in our hands. In sum, it remains an imperfect work for those too, and each for himself and God for all. The main conclusion, therefore, is: Banér should be left his fortune in [the] lands; the [?intended] blow between the two crowns and the most praiseworthy Archhouse of Austria hitherto prepared, he should nevertheless, and with the victor, then capitulate.

3o. On granting the Roman Months [Römerzug], [...] the shadow of a real Circle decision, I hold too [...] in sight of the [?enemy], above, further [...] a [?brave] resolution should cause more, still more with a practicable consultation, the [...]: 3. dated the 8th of this [month] report, as far as this Circle and [?consequently] almost the universal [peace] concerns. Firstly, one has made no reckoning of any real [?existence] and conjunction, no result; 2ndly, as little on the princes' and [...] [?estates'] own constitution for the defence of their own lands. Whether now nothing but [?scattered] [?brooms] [i.e. no cohesion], when in the direction [?of raising] the means in the manner [...]. His Imperial Majesty should and could be pleased with nothing less than this: Denmark [...] to act as [?mediator] and at the same time to [?exclude] itself while the war lasts. At the same time they keep [them] indirectly confined in Mecklenburg and Pomerania: the Swedes are not so subtle as to distinguish between the King of Denmark and the Duke of Holstein, and the king himself often does not distinguish, as [you know] from my report, the help from Gadebusch [...]. On the other hand [...] the King seems to wait for the present, not to be so that Denmark, and yet to hasten a resolution, the struck single hostility, [...] wounded [...], [have] not [?been committed] against each other this time; and the greatest worry is that Banér wants to go to Meissen, His Highness [...] and further wherever his luck carries him.

NB. Without any bridges, without any [...], the enemy came over here with five or six regiments to [...] and went back with nothing done, so that he could neither have withdrawn nor received any relief because of the ice floes drifting there and more than that. Neither the four thousand men asked through the soldier, nor the four-and-twenty thousand thalers offered, nor the conjunction with the Imperial [?guards], nor the taking of Stadthagen, nor the defence on the Elbe, nor the redoubt at Stillhorn; and in sum nothing has followed with Duke Georg. Of [?warnings] to Denmark itself there was no lack, but with the Swedes watchful here, and even what [was] here, I had no answer to my letters. The outcome of the Circle diet I [?doubt], [?of] a decision among the fearful and wavering, if not something worse.

[Margin:] Of the letter one takes care [of] a Circle [...], but the answer only of a convoy is to His Imperial Majesty [...]; to the others I have still had no answer.

[...] with Hildesheim. At the Danish court the ministers are being tried by the English, French and Swedes; the king is [?deterred] by our misfortune at Bremen. It is the height of ingratitude; no one is well inclined, and all are against the Peace of Prague, not so happily defended by arms as well concluded for the Emperor and the Empire.

After what had happened I was further about to write my simple thoughts to Your Excellency, chiefly whether the enemy could have been [?contained] on this side of the Elbe, or at least some resistance from Duke Georg had appeared afterwards; in such a way that I thought His Imperial Majesty should go merely on the defensive here and never hazard anything, and keep the army in place in such a way that a whole army would not be ruined to the ground every year even without operations; by which way alone [peace could come] from the Crown of Sweden, which has no means to continue the war from its own, where the French [means] too are not effective in the matter, and peace might be raised sooner or later; and were everything still, God be praised, [as it was], chiefly the standing of the estates and [?towns] had been in good order, [and] the enemy had had to stay in a future posture, [things] would meanwhile have come to the goal and to treaties, and been understood, if only the Crown itself had felt the war [...] or still had to feel it.

Of fairness no one [...] on Duke Georg's [side]; [?above all], though, that the King of Denmark should abandon his own word given to him. But now that the whole state of things changes [?totally], it will not be so easy to do at the coming campaign as much more must be thought of, because the further incursion into the Empire, or even the conjunction with Duke Bernhard and Banér, remains [to be] prevented; so a stroke must be ventured, come what may as God wills. On the other hand, unventured [...] Banér [...] would then remain; half his army ran away and would still run away if he wanted to lead it back.

But in my judgement His Imperial Majesty's and his most praiseworthy Archhouse's affairs were never in greater danger than this time, so that the basis of a universal peace hardly [...], and also the [?total] oppression of the most praiseworthy Archhouse of Austria, at least [...] Germany, then [...] they are the party against Spain, to the same end will not only alienate the estates with force and power from His Imperial Majesty, but by the universality of the peace bar the way to peace altogether.

So I hold that, without losing any time, one should see how His Imperial Majesty [...] the utmost force and a great real [...] negotiated, because to that end [it] brings together that we mean to raise the work by force or to end the war at once, but only and solely because one might be forced to everything that suits [the enemy] by being made to fight, so that nothing but [what] remains in reserve in case of unexpected misfortune, so that one could then capitulate to the enemy. To raise this I found no other [...] than that [...] the other hereditary lands of Her Imperial Majesty [...] no [...] taken [...] such a corps remains on its legs in [...], so that nevertheless [...] are not lost at once, the courage of the loyal estates [does not] fall [...], and with others the [?insolence] [...] money had to come from elsewhere. The hereditary lands could give quarters better than money, to be for His Imperial Majesty [...] than at an untimely time [?paid] old money, and as [?all] is done only to those through whom such subjects [are], and that [...] administered without self-interest. How now, after what has happened, the [reform] is now to be put to work in our state, I leave aside; extremes we must in my opinion take once, because to [?continue] the war in the Empire is still impossible [...] nothing.

The 30 men of Colonel Meirer, who lately let themselves be [?killed] and captured, deceived me, as I was assured of the enemy's necessity, and from that encounter at more resistance, and so [?argued] a real break: the estates not wanting to offend the Swedes for this time, and all the less because they are not over there in full strength, and threaten with burning and destruction [...] all, they will push things through. Magdeburg with its directorate is too weak for the work. Would be outvoted by the majority; but should something be granted sooner or later, [it would be] with infinite conditions and clauses, which in effect make every grant void. What remedy, how to wage the war, how to reach peace? The Circle diets have come to an end this year, from the poverty of some and the resistance of others; without quarters one cannot be. The upper estates will meanwhile stir [...] estate against, will want to but in the end desert us; the fewest [will] stake everything beside His Imperial Majesty. NB. At all the courts here there may be [?zeal] for religion; ministers so obliged to us. At the Brunswick lives which one [...] about the restitution of goods,

but still at this time and this time sees Banér's progress, nothing is to be advised here so long as the Circle diet is without the Imperial arms and Banér dominates there [?totally]. Since no Circle diet [is held], and even if an assembly is raised, nothing but disrepute for His Imperial Majesty is to be gained. Yes, because an unconcluded Circle diet, broken up by the enemy, is better than one concluded with an express neutrality, and [better than] that such a decision would then be maintained with arms among the estates. Whether to [?requisition] separately at home and from others? With that it rightly has, in my opinion too, its delay until one knows where Banér wants to go. Next, nothing separately to raise either; but then the demand of the Roman Months remains, and, God willing, the quarters [...] Banér is sooner to be found a country in Brunswick than in Pomerania. These people will draw the war on to their own necks, for the Imperial army, as Count Puchheim has said, was no more than the shadow of an army rather than an army, the fewest of whom could do unanimous service, and would have had to hold back because of hunger and want; so there would never be, with better reason and without offence to Denmark, quarters as well in the land of Brunswick, but to keep Austria, Bohemia and Silesia, and to make peace [?in whatever] way at the [...] last.

If a further misfortune happens, they will not let us [...] to recollect ourselves; the enemy knows well that he means his victory and our ruin in this. All the more, though, I must advise, since the recruitment here wants to be raised with the enemy's crossing, even if Duke Franz Carl or Herr von Hofkirchen openly have the particular offers [?from] officers and a following. Therefore [...] accepts. But I do not see how to bring the [?troops] well to the regiments [...], still less how to keep them here, not at all, since nearby no single running place, things standing as they stand, could be given, how they could remain. Against that it looks hard too that the Count Palatine would gather his [?troops] in the land of Saxe-Lauenburg or Mecklenburg and procure the means of maintenance for them out of Hamburg and Lübeck. Meanwhile [he lets] his troops operate [?elsewhere] for Sweden. There would still be people at hand, but they are not to be held off without zeal and ready money from the English and French ministers; on our side no one is at hand at the right time. [...], and still the conjuncture changes so that Banér's distress lessens and the hope of plunder grows [?from] day to day. But if they will take an officer besides, it is a venture and one cannot rely on it, for the [?performance] of what they might promise does not stand in sum, but [in] fortune's hands. Such then, that one should have been watchful, because the enemy, having crossed the Elbe, [put] a [?corps] [...] in Further Pomerania, and from there into Mecklenburg and Saxe-Lauenburg with the help of Brandenburg's [people]: to occupy, de facto and without any regard, residence or no residence, to maintain the recruited soldiers with provisions from here, and to open the pass to Silesia, since the time of the best counsels in these parts has slipped away; and hoping that nothing but ourselves is to be relied on. The lands are ruined, respect lost; Brunswick, Bremen, Holstein the town[s] armed, the Imperial army away, not with reputation.

Though as concerns the completion of the [...] Circle diet here, one might think it could be summoned for another long time, or perhaps this means taken, as is [?observed] in such times of war, and one estate or another be requisitioned separately [...]. It is power [...], perhaps Banér shows the way to that too. It could be that the Circle diet still continued, but I do not hold its continuation to be in His Imperial Majesty's service either. If the war stays in those parts, one still holds that no contribution is transferred, [to] arrange for oneself other ways and with pasture in Pomerania; but we [get] nothing. In sum, as is broadly meant, Banér would get to go back here, where it came from [...]; a granted and an ungranted Roman Months all one thing, especially with the danger still that others might [?break off] their resolution. How it hurts one [?to watch] how easily the enemy could be ruined and how little is done, Your Lordship will not believe. Should the like happen [?elsewhere] too, everything remains on the Elector's opinion until its time.

As concerns the peace, I am still in doubt whether to talk with Salvius separately or not; I see no particular fruit from it, and his inclination is not to be trusted at all. He is bad, and worse than the French, towards His Imperial Majesty and the Empire, though he professes the contrary; and if one gave him 1,000, the French [give] 2,000. He is full of poison against the House of Austria, and if he inclined, the work must still in the end get into Sweden, and against Oxenstierna's will raises [it], and consequently be negotiated through those councillors of the realm who sincerely want peace in exchange for a piece [?of] Pomerania, which was Salvius's demand. Supervision is at hand; with that he will now stay more than ever.

It now depends on the Imperial resolution; this resolution must be obtained either with the [?knowledge] of Electoral Brandenburg or without its [?foreknowledge]. Should it remain directed to the first case, it rightly waits until the Elector's [?intention] is known. But here it is not so easy to keep an eye on Brandenburg as well as Denmark as regards the ports, for though Denmark is passed over in this, it is still not hidden from him at the Swedish [...]. So the question is whether Brandenburg could be moved to negotiate an agreement [?with] Sweden for himself and as if without the Emperor's knowledge, with the remark that if he had the Swedish Crown's real intention, he would also want to strive to keep the need [met] with Imperial Majesty; or whether, after obtaining the Elector of Brandenburg's consent, the Emperor's [?intention] is to be trusted really to the King of Denmark, and to negotiate in Sweden through him. Their [...] against Denmark are indeed great, but he still has regard, and [lets] the crown be treated by a third hand, as to found the particular treaties unanimously and only on Salvius's report to Stockholm, who alone is more easily corrupted by the French than all the other senators; hence they are now more [?insistent] than ever.

Secondly, as to resolving without the Elector's knowledge, when the estates want to desert a Roman emperor, push the war on his and his house's neck alone, accept no [?subsidies], not resist the enemy themselves, there I hesitate greatly what to do in such a case. His Electoral Highness is indeed quite innocent of this, but [...] they will also hold, if through the neutralities of other estates both the Elector and His Imperial Majesty have to lose, to the point that [...] contributes to the matter, that will come because of it. Whereas if he wanted this Circle, as Your Lordship lately reported, the Swedes would long since have been defeated; [to come] to these extremities, I hold, is all the less to be reached, because it is not customary to deal so with loyal estates, and it would unfailingly be spread by the enemy himself in hatred of the Emperor, and this would be the real main rift of the Prague peace, and others too [would gain] access, and inform the other minister through his people, and great confidence with several councillors of the realm. He enjoys the toll anyway and for nothing, but will not oblige anyone else so highly or spend so much on others' offices.

Though it could be said that, since keeping the ports runs against his state, this work could be trusted to him so much [?less], it is still to be considered against this that Denmark will think it is also [...] his state [...] better to let Sweden [have] one and two or more ports than to stand in danger that Sweden prospers in the Roman Empire so that it makes itself master [?of] Pomerania and Mecklenburg. Perhaps it could be arranged that the King of Denmark sent Count Penz to Stockholm under the pretext of pushing the universal treaties: [...] not so much information of the matter, that they [...]: and that through him, as if this secret dealing were undermined and penetrated with one councillor or another, whether the Crown is really inclined to enter into an agreement, particular and secret treaties. In my judgement it is safer [to do] this work directly with the [...] principally, and after everything has been pacified and removed by the Peace of Prague, let them treat and retreat: that means to resign the empire. If the court of Rome will not take to heart the confederation between Sweden and France, it must care nothing for whatever is attempted by foreigners to the ruin of the Catholic religion in the Roman Empire; and it is a shame that Cardinal Ginetti [...] the like less [...] that the King of Denmark.

The longer the door to universal treaties is open, the less one comes to peace; they glue the crowns together through the very [?assent] of the Emperor more and more, draw the estates [...] away from accommodation and by the very name of treaty bar the way to treaties. As long as we proceed towards peace on the roads [?prescribed] [...] by our enemies, so long they raise their intention of no treaty; but we are [?totally] without hope of peace. I also hold that they had better have been broken off at Cologne than now, when many count us lost; it will be taken for a still more firmly taken resolution for war, and then we will come out of the labyrinth of universality and [?impossibility] of treaties at last with reputation. Here the work will still give cause, with the particular treaties and the [inte]rested estates, to [?adapt] themselves as well as possible with their interest to the foreign crowns.

So I stay with the first way proposed through the Elector or Count Penz, and hold that it is to be practised only with His Imperial Majesty's use, and if he is caught in it, that I am to be commended to God. But above all I hold that the very name of universal treaties is to be [?abolished], not to speak of dissolving the congress at Cologne. It is a devilish invention, this universality, and serves to exclude all treaties, and impossible, if His Papal Holiness, the intrinsic nature of the safe-conducts is shown, the manner how and to what end the [...] crowns at the beginning engaged themselves in Germany, that they should not understand the supreme danger to the Catholic religion if they made themselves obliged to the most praiseworthy Archhouse of Austria; so because of it, in this war and the extremities are chiefly lodged. Before universal treaties we see the difficulties that universality brings; in the treaties, if it should come to that, it will be seen still more; after the treaties and in the very execution of the transacted universality more than ever; and impossible that the Pope and the College of Cardinals should be able to include the French and Swedes [...]. What more absurd could be asked than to have conceded by His Imperial Majesty before the congress that the estates of the Empire, from the alliances of Heilbronn, of the Wetterau and of Worms, [be] allied with the Crown of Sweden and France against the Em[peror] by the above-[...] methods; so long as one leaves [?Salvius] in his place or must let him remain, one could then also press [the case] so much better, also the removal of the French and English envoys, if they want to negotiate [?hostilities] publicly.

As concerns France, I see no way to reach peace; they must [?continue] the war there and also to defend themselves, come what may, as God wills, they want to have Lorraine, [?Savoy], Burgundy, Alsace and even the empire. That is too much; with Sweden it is easier to come to terms, but certainly not through [?Salvius], and there is no way to make a partial peace other than to detest [...] universality. Forgive [me]: with Sweden it is concluded particularly, and still the universal treaties remain in being; they will push some steps there, accept what is useful for the Crown, and still seek such improvement with the universality. The King of Denmark's letter, sent off, went off in good zeal, and in the hope that the treaties might go on in this way; these fall consequently too, that the Count Palatine's obligation and that he keep out of foreign alliances, [...] with those of his good intention, but that the King of England will not oblige himself in return to any crown to perform anything [?substantial], [to] him, the Count Palatine's [?potentates] will not help in return, and through that so much does not [?grow] to other crowns, so that they should create for him at present the way to an advantageous peace for himself, or at least [something].

Since residents are so [?sufficient] in common, it would have been better to have sent someone here and to Lübeck long ago; one has to win these people here over the long term and meet the [?artifices] of foreign ministers little by little. As long as [?our] arms prosper again, so much is not to be raised or prevented, but still no time to lose to send someone to both these places, and one who is [?sufficient], and the sooner the better. If there is a place where His Imperial Majesty needs people, it is here [...].

The English envoy is courting Arnim. He is a man who, because of his great following, [...] has done much harm and much [...]. I do not know whether it would be advisable to help after him, whether it would not be a way and useful to oblige him in that way, or at least keep him in good [?hope]. The protectorium of which I once made mention for him, I think, should not be refused but furthered.
