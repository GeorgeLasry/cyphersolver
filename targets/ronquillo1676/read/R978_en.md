# R978, Ronquillo to Fuenmayor, Nijmegen, 10 Feb 1677 (signed for him by Bernardo Navarro)

⟨... in which to discharge my obligation.⟩ The settling of this devil of a draft of full powers keeps us more
embarrassed every day by the disputes and objections raised at every moment; and although on our side, and the
Dutch side too, and I believe on that of the Lord Emperor, this point could have been finished, since the drafts we
have presented to the mediators agree and there is nothing to catch hold of, nevertheless a gasconade of the Danish
plenipotentiary keeps this dispute alive. It consists in his maintaining and stubbornly insisting that, if the French
hand him their full powers in Latin, he will hand over his in the same language; but if it is in French, he will
draw up his master's in Danish; in which dispute it is plain that much of it is obstinacy.

The Dutch have already handed in their signed power and are trying to do the same with their claims; and to this is
added the uproar raised among the plenipotentiaries of Holland because Count Kinsky let slip to the mediators that
all the drafts handed in were to be brought back to the conference of the allies so that they might be signed and the
objections found be examined; which, in the Dutch hurry, seems to them a great deal of delay; and this morning they
were with me hurling thunderbolts and sparks, and finally burst out saying that there was to be no more conference;
that although it is true that in all business it was well to proceed by common accord of the confederates, they did
not wish to be bound so as to be prevented from making their own particular moves. And I am minded to make ours at
the same time, even if the others do not join, not only so as to keep step with these people, but also because, if
it contains a clause on the satisfaction of the allies, the French cannot answer that we have asked for nothing.

I see these men very eager for peace; and although I would not dare affirm that they will make it separately, given
the assurances they give me every day that they will not abandon us and will assist us in the war, nevertheless I
much fear they may break out into forcing us to sign a treaty out of season, laying down for us whatever law they
please. I try to calm them, and they have promised me that, even if it delays the presenting of their claims by four
or five more days, they will wait for ours to come in at the same time; and I try not to let them out of my hand for
a moment, lest they throw themselves into greater despair.

I repeat to Your Lordship my thanks for the news you impart of what happens there and for the papers you send me. The
declaration Count Windischgrätz has made on behalf of the Lord Emperor is very good in general, for it gives to
understand that he wants a share in the conquests, or that the allies should come to guarantee his dominions in
Alsace; but this matter must be handled with great care and skill, so that it does not harm us. The Dutch will
approve the first, because they are of the opinion that the share of the conquests against Sweden should serve as an
exchange for our losses in Flanders; but the second they will not face, and the Lord Emperor will regain the
reputation he has had of not wanting peace and of looking only to his own particular interests; and this will be the
more recognised the more he marches along the upper Moselle and is employed in working for the common cause.

And Your Lordship will already have heard that the Prince of Orange has declared that he will not move as long as
the Lord Emperor's army does not carry out what was planned at The Hague. Your Lordship may consider how I shall find
myself with all these things, being alone, and so alone that I assure Your Lordship that neither from Madrid, nor
from Flanders, nor from any other quarter do they help me, not even with news; and in the midst of this Don Manuel de
Lira, who was the only one who could help me from The Hague, is staying in Brussels; but I also recognise that the
same reason that justifies his leaving Holland equally justifies his not returning, there is no help for it; and in
truth his presence at that court would be of great advantage. ⟨God help us, and keep Your Lordship many years as I
wish. Nijmegen, 10 February 1677.⟩
