# R975, Ronquillo to Fuenmayor, Nijmegen, 28 Dec 1676

⟨... and I have already visited the second and sought out the first.⟩ The Dutch are pressing fiercely for peace, and
hide nothing that leads to this end, and no less do they make plain their feeling against our negligence and
unpreparedness. One must confess that they are good [?Swedes], in which the English concur no less. The excuses
Count Kinsky gives of a sciatica that has come upon him in Cologne they scorn. Nevertheless they have agreed that we
should not hand over our full powers until a minister of the Lord Emperor arrives; and it is no small thing that
they have understood that it would cause more trouble to present those of the allies one by one than all together;
but it is certain that, by putting forward all the objections together, the course that is taken will be more
effective.

They have handed their full powers to the French and Dutch mediators. In that of Holland there was a small
objection, which the States have already ordered removed. In those of Sweden the [?haughtiness] cannot be tolerated,
nor the declaring that they were provoked into the war by the breach of the Peace of Westphalia; the French have
thrown in similar clauses of arrogance. But the two that seem intolerable in their full power are: the one, the King
of France saying that he has condescended to send his ambassadors to the congress through the expedients the King of
England has supplied him, and hopes he will obtain the release of Prince Wilhelm of Fürstenberg, which the Lord
Emperor will in no way consent to; the other is raised by the Dutch and all the Protestant princes, namely the one
containing the mediation of the Pope, saying that the instrument of the full powers is the foundation of the
firmness of the contract of peace to be made, and that it must not contain a clause contradicting religion, which is
the basis of the public law of the State. And indeed, when the deputies at The Hague agreed to remove that word
"separate" in their full powers, they resolved at the same time that the mention of the Pope's mediation should not
be allowed. The French answered that they will not alter the said text, which has their master's approval; but they
also say they expect within three or four days an answer to another letter they have written; and if, as they say,
they mean to deal straightforwardly and remove the obstacles to the peace treaty, middle ways will not fail to be
found for this.

The night before last the Dutch told me that the mediators were pressing hard for the ordinary posts to Sweden to
be allowed; and although they made me the excuse that His Danish Majesty rightly opposed it for not having been
willing to grant this very thing in his passport, nevertheless they raised an outcry over their trade, which was
hampered by the merchants being unable to keep correspondence for bills of exchange and news. To overcome this point
the mediators offer us that France will allow us the free dispatch of the posts, though by a roundabout way; an
offer I have not wished to answer positively, but it will always suit us to have the passage free, although they
also believe that if they attach conditions, they will be the same ones we grant them.

From the withdrawal of the minister of Münster, and the excuse of the Wolfenbüttel one for not wishing to negotiate
on that account, one infers what Your Lordship reasons, that they act in concert; and I believe they will aim at
having this matter brought before the general congress, because they must understand that they are to be helped by
the Dutch more than others, and that the Dutch have no great wish that Denmark and Brandenburg should keep advantages
in the duchy of Bremen, since they would sooner compensate Sweden from the spoils of these princes than from those of
Celle and Münster.

No minister of these princes, nor of Lorraine, appears here, because the King of France makes it well understood
that he is not minded to grant them the jus legationis. And although he treats Lorraine with more decency, and agrees
that, there being a precedent of some ambassador of the Duke having been covered, he will grant the passports in the
same form; and two having been presented to him, he now says they should justify them, when the King of France has
carried off to the last item the archives of Nancy; to the princes of the Empire he says he wishes to know what the
Emperor and Spain will do, but he does not defer to what they may do. I should wish some course were taken in this,
for I foresee that on the very day the text of the full powers is settled we shall have to enter on the main points
of making proposals, and it would be of great harm if by then ministers of Lorraine, Celle and Münster were not here;
and on this matter I dare not speak, because in Madrid they have not answered on this particular, and Your Lordship
knows that at our court no one is covered unless he be of a crowned head, or of the republics of Venice or Holland.
⟨Your Lordship forgive the length of this letter ...⟩
