# R972, Salinas to Fuenmayor, London, 26 Jan 1677

⟨... and that all my obedience to your service wishes to deserve it.⟩ I do not wonder how awkward that residence must
be for Your Lordship, having to deal with such different humours and interests, and particularly with the Bishop of
Münster, whom I take for a quibbler and, as they say, something of a cheat; but let Your Lordship console himself that
there is trouble everywhere, and let him turn round to the north and he will see how [?our] lyre plays, what is
happening at Nijmegen and what is happening here, the disorder proceeding from the pilot who steers the helm, for
which, the voices of the people say, there is another who alone can remedy it; on which the letters from Madrid will
undeceive Your Lordship.

Don Pedro Ronquillo has been anxious these days, with motives to spare; and although my reports will not have freed
him of it, he will nevertheless proceed with more light, for since his fears came from the journey of Ambassador
Temple to The Hague, which gave him and everyone cause for suspicion, I have tried to discover all the ground I could,
and I have found that everything was aimed at bringing the Prince of Orange round to lend a hand to the peace, and that
it has succeeded; for since this last post from Holland this King is better pleased and satisfied with the said Prince,
and Ambassador Van Beuningen no less, with his inclination so barefaced to this end.

But even this is not the whole evil, for if those offices were directed along the straight path of honour, we could
hope for a good settlement for all; but I fear, and not without great grounds, that between this King and the Dutch
they have [?planned] for us the peace they mean to give us, and that it has been necessary to win over the [Prince] of
Orange and settle his interests; with which negotiation, and the haste these men are making at Nijmegen to come to the
substance, passing over so many difficulties and formalities displeasing to the parties, they are running to the
conclusion, and they promise themselves here to obtain it before the campaign, because of what it matters to this King
and because the Dutch desire it. In which project I always fear they take Burgundy from us, because, great discussions
having passed with me on this matter, in which they saw me firm and laying down as the first foundation of the peace
the restitution of this province, they have kept themselves guarded from me; and lately there are great demands and
answers going from here to Paris.

On which I spoke two days ago to the King, and he told me that the peace had to be made, that he was neither in a
state to enter a war nor wanted it, that Flanders had to remain with frontiers, that we should not flatter ourselves
with the Parliament, because it would not oblige him to this engagement. On which I will tell Your Lordship that the
dispositions we have towards this tribunal could not be better, but for the same reason I fear there must be so much
confusion that it will oblige this King to dissolve or prorogue it, so that we shall remain as we are. Nevertheless I
do not lose sight of any offices that may foster our hopes; and on the main point, which touches us so closely, that
of the levies and recruits for France, I have pursued my complaints without yielding to the fear they tried to put in
me with the absurd answer I was given, in which nevertheless it was achieved at least that the recruiting ceased in the
kingdom of England; but as it continued in Scotland, I wrote the paper of which a copy goes enclosed to the Secretary
of State, whose answer Your Lordship will see, together with the letter His Britannic Majesty ordered written to the
Estates of Scotland.

I saw what Your Lordship tells me of the discourse Monsieur d'Amerongen held with you, and it is a pity that so discreet
and prudent an answer as Your Lordship's should be spent on that person, for he is not one of the most classic men of
Holland, though he is one of those of ill intention. His opinion does not agree with that of Van Beuningen, who is
here, for the latter is not of the opinion for the truce, because he is more [?thrifty], and he thinks that maintaining
the allies might cost them some expense, so that he is heading with all haste towards the peace; and, as Your Lordship
says, any [peace] will be better than a truce. But what I believe is that, if time were lacking here at the opening of
the campaign to conclude the peace, this King, urged by this minister, might propose some suspension of arms for two
or three months, but no more. What I can assure Your Lordship is that within a month the abscess must burst, and that
of everything that occurs I will give you a more punctual account, always seeking [Your Lordship's] orders for your
service.
