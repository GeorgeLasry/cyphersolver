# R980, Ronquillo to Fuenmayor, Nijmegen, 27 Jan 1677

⟨I thank Your Lordship with all the gratitude I owe for the news you are pleased to give me of what is happening
there,⟩ and from it I see that the Princes of Brunswick and the Bishop of Münster are already thinking of withdrawing
the negotiation from that assembly, judging by the delays their ministers put in the way of meeting. I come to believe
that this will not only be on account of the proposal of the Imperialists, whose sting, Your Lordship tells me, they
keep very much in mind, but even more particularly because they must have heard something of the Dutch treating to
make a separate peace with Sweden, which must touch them to the quick, for they will see how harmful this novelty will
be to them. And I hold to this view all the more as I see the openness with which the matter is already spoken of,
which I do not doubt will have come to Your Lordship's notice, since even from England they write it to me.

This talk has much reduced the rumours that went about of a suspension of arms, and the argument Your Lordship made on
this point to Monsieur Amerongen is so prudent that I can say with much pride that I have used the same reasons Your
Lordship gives on the occasions that have arisen to speak of the matter. It now carries less weight, as I told Your
Lordship; but I would think that, in the state we are in in Flanders, so wretched that I spare myself repeating to you
the gloom that the letters from Brussels will cause you (if it is with Your Lordship as with me, we may count their
brevity well spent, so long as they do not add to our distress at greater length), it would be of great benefit to us
to agree a suspension of arms until the end of June, and on the contrary of the greatest harm if it were to be for four
years, as Amerongen argued, for the reasons Your Lordship weighs and I understand. But for the very reason that we
shall contradict what our enemies want, they would use it to refuse it; and so it seems to me that this point cannot
be settled, and that we shall have to think only of the war.

And to return to the separate peace the Dutch wish to make with Sweden: although it has not yet come to be spoken of
openly in the congress, it is certain that at The Hague it will be pursued with great insistence. I will tell Your
Lordship that this proposal was made by the Swedes themselves, adding that if the King of France would not agree to
it they would settle it by themselves and declare against the Most Christian King. It is certain that the Dutch long,
as always, for peace with Sweden, saying that there is no other remedy for a general peace than to offset our losses
in the Low Countries with Sweden's losses; but while this settlement could be very convenient for us if made within a
general peace, we expose ourselves to the precipice if Sweden's is made separately, for it is certain that we shall
lose the allies of the North, that the King of Sweden will not be able to help us, and that, having them so close to
our dominions, they can play us a nasty trick by taking the side of France, particularly at a juncture when the sums we
owe them are so great. So if this business does not go along with the universal peace, I find many drawbacks in
opening it now; and so I must beg Your Lordship to be pleased to let me know what you hear there, so that I may be
prepared when this matter comes to be discussed in the congress.

Everything that has been dealt with in it so far has been foolishness and drunken squabbling, the Count Kinsky having
taken up our time with his German ways, very proud of coming with Italian maxims and reflections, so that most of the
time has been spent in settling his quarrels. The point of the draft of the common full power is resolved, and in the
conference we allies shall hold tomorrow I believe it will come approved by the enemy; and I do not tell Your Lordship
of the objections that have been raised because I shall send you a copy of it when it is fully settled. I shall only
tell Your Lordship that, while on our side we try to ease the arrangement of the preliminaries (in which I cannot deny
I employ my greatest care), the French show how little wish they have for peace, an attitude they have already
revealed; and I assure Your Lordship that all of it was needed to dispel the rumours our enemies had spread, and had
made the whole world believe, that we did not want peace and were delaying the means that could lead to it. We shall
see what comes of tomorrow's conference, and I shall report everything to Your Lordship.
