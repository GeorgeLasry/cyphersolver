# R983, Ronquillo to Fuenmayor, Nijmegen, 22 May 1677

⟨... I go on to beg you not to put down to neglect or want of friendship my not having answered with the punctuality
that Your Lordship's continued favours deserve,⟩ for here we are so hampered [...] in doing nothing, as Your Lordship can
[...] temporise with the [...], for here they follow the same maxims as in Bremen [...] of Pomerania, to the jealousy
[...] power of Brandenburg and princes of the [...] Swedes and [...]; and the worst is that in the end the treaties make
no progress, and that premature declarations [...] which are the cause of the [?losses] [...] take upon their [...].
[...] I value, as I ought, the papers Your Lordship is pleased to send me, and I shall always be glad that the plan that
has been formed on the separation of that conference should go ahead. And to tell Your Lordship the truth, it [?grieves]
us little that Bremen and Verden should remain Swedish [...], so long as the present [...] is pursued with warmth;
which depends solely on our [...] with diligence the Baltic squadron, whose importance [...] Don Manuel de Lira and I do
not cease to represent to Spain every day with the most urgent pleas. As far as I see, the Swedes stand firm in refusing
to give up any of the conquests, and say that if they make any reparation it will be to Brandenburg, but that it is
not to be in lands or fortresses. I believe Your Lordship knows the view I have always held on the northern alliances
since the time the treaties were being [?negotiated], and I would wish us to have an army of our own in Flanders, which
without doubt would set in motion the troops of [...] and of the Prince of Orange; for although we have nothing
particular to [?expect] from either, after the battle of Saint-Omer, it is my opinion that things would be done
differently if the Lord Duke of Villahermosa could take the field with mules that were not hired; but in the present
state of things God grant we have even these, and above all the means to feed them. And since God willed that we should
find ourselves embarked in the war with Sweden, we must make the last effort to carry it forward under full sail and
keep those allies, not so much for the profit we should expect from them as for the harm that would come to us if
France won them over, especially now that they find themselves with forces and men, and need money to keep them up or
to get rid of them on good terms.

⟨At last, notwithstanding the reluctance of the adversaries, we presented on the 18th of this month our answers to the
first proposals ... publishing us as infringers of the Peace of Aachen,⟩ using, to bring the English round to their
view, the [?pretext] that, if they had to answer in writing, their justification on the point of the infringement
[would rest] on the declaration that [?the ambassador of Great Britain] made against us after the siege the Prince of
Orange laid to Charleroi; a commitment which they well knew the English would try to avoid, since this recourse would
not serve the King of England well with his Parliament, which is full of the misgivings that the monstrous progress of
the Most Christian King and our weakness must give it. And even after our proposals had been handed in, the English
suggested that the treating should begin in the form that I myself had proposed to them, namely that, to avoid the
harshness the parties' proposals may carry when handed over sealed, which would serve rather to embitter minds than to
soften them, the mediators should take the verbal answers of both sides and put them in writing themselves; and for
this they sent me Monsieur Jenkins, third plenipotentiary of England, who, being a devout heretic, came to preach to me
what I would not receive as gospel, telling me that they still held our answers sealed and that handing them over
could be dispensed with. But seeing that I held to it [...], and finding that he was making our allies and even the
Imperialists waver, he asked me, convinced, with very weighty words, whether I thought peace would be made by handing
over these answers; to which I replied again that [...] the King of France [...] so that he should withdraw them. In the
end Ratman and I, who alone supported me, carried the point, so that they are now obliged to put our answers into the
hands of the French; and it matters little to me whether they speak in writing or not, nor the cleverness of trying to
involve the King of England in the blame they lay on us for having broken the peace treaties, for I trust that God will
not fail us in answering them decently without offending the mediator. Now we are discussing the form in which we are
to proceed from here on, so that we remain covered with our masters.
