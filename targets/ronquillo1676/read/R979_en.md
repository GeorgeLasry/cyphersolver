# R979, Ronquillo to Fuenmayor, Nijmegen, 9 Jan 1677

⟨... in which to exercise my obedience.⟩ I see from what Your Lordship is pleased to impart to me in your said letter
the difficulties that also arise in that conference; that if, in the present state of our affairs, those which in
this congress prevent us from coming to treat of the essential points of the peace before spring begins are harmful,
those are no less so for the arrangement of the operations of the coming campaign. And so, to avoid the irresolution,
discord and animosities likely to arise from it, I judge that no course more to the purpose can be found than the one
Your Lordship has devised; for, if it can be obtained, we shall rid ourselves of all these embarrassments, we shall
leave the affairs [?undecided], and with the demolitions Your Lordship proposes we shall leave unfortified a country
which, in whatever hands it may be, will never be in good ones for us.

In short, from what passes there and what I can see here, I see more clearly every day that all will aim solely at
their own advantage. And when, in the bad posture of our affairs, caused by the lack of everything needful in
Flanders, by the progress the French are making at Messina, by the open impatience with which the Dutch desire and
seek peace, and by the dejection of the peoples, increased by the unfortunate encounter of the Danes, whose recovery I
fear must cost us dear, the greatest ground of our hopes should have been the forces of the Lord Emperor and the
diversion his troops could make by an entry into France, to draw off part of those the Most Christian King is
gathering on the frontier of Flanders and turn aside the fatal blow that threatens these defenceless provinces, we see
that his whole aim is the recovery of Breisach and Upper Alsace; and whatever colours those imperial ministers may
wish to give to erase the just impressions their ill-judged declaration has made, the bottom of their policy always
shows through, which is to draw the northern troops to help them carry out their own particular enterprises, and to
make war without a thought for Flanders or its approaching ruin. The pretext of sounding the mind of the princes is
not satisfactory, because in my understanding they will be good as long as they are paid, particularly those of
Brunswick. But what I fear is that this untimely declaration of the imperialists may be very harmful to us even for
the acceptance of that course, however imperceptibly one tries to induce them to accept the [934]. ⟨Of the affairs of
this congress I will tell Your Lordship ...⟩

⟨... the form of each be followed.⟩ It is true that the English, when they communicated it to the Danes and the
Dutch, did not say it was from the French and Swedes; but when I took them to task with some strong arguments, they
frankly confessed the truth to me, saying that as mediators they could not avoid receiving the papers one of the
parties presented and communicating them to the others in the state in which the preliminaries [?stand] today. It
causes me great concern to see the eagerness with which the Dutch desire peace, which makes me fear some rash move on
their part, and it would be no rash judgement to think so, given their longing and our bad posture everywhere, which
is the argument they throw at us; and no less perplexing to me is the narrow brief Count Kinsky brings, who arrived in
this town three days ago, for having been together more than two hours I have seen that in Vienna they cannot grasp
our dreadful condition; so I try to soften him and to make him understand little by little what is most fitting,
having devised the means to slow down as far as possible the speed with which the Dutch wish to move in these
affairs, on the occasion of the second draft of [?full powers] drawn up by the French and Swedes; and at the same time
I do not neglect whatever may lead to the surest and speediest repose of Christendom, since at the least the French
may very possibly wish to push their progress in that quarter, to make up with what they conquer for the advantage we
could gain in the Low Countries. ⟨God keep Your Lordship many years as I wish. Nijmegen, 9 January 1677.⟩
