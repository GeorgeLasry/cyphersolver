# R981, Ronquillo to Fuenmayor, Nijmegen, 4 March 1677

⟨... and I only miss being employed in Your Lordship's service, to exercise my obedience.⟩ In both letters Your Lordship
writes at great length on the separate peace with Sweden that is being negotiated; and that it was promoted by the Dutch
is certain, and God knows what will come of it. Freedom of trade is so much so that it will be a relief to the three
provinces that the Swedes should have no exemption in the North; it is that too, but I do not know whether it will be
worse for Denmark to have it, as will happen if she wins Scania, for she is a good [?manager], and much more so being
master of both shores of the Sound; and I do not know whether the provinces would put up better with the Swedes'
exemption than with the absolute dominion of the Dane.

God grant us good success in the conference of [?Wesel], for if in it Stettin weighs more with the Elector of
Brandenburg than the persuasions of the others, we must be on the alert. And in friendship I will tell Your Lordship
that the Elector fears that the Lord Emperor will not allow him to keep Pomerania, and rightly, because it matters more
to the Lord Emperor that the Swedes should hold those provinces than the Elector of Brandenburg, and with reason it is
beyond doubt. And over this I have had some hours not as pleasant as a comedy's, because on Sunday night the
Brandenburgers were resolved to ask for it outright in their proposals, and the Imperialists obliged to oppose it,
because the Empire would be in uproar if this did not happen, and the Emperor could not allow it; for, whether by right
of conquest or of lapsed fief, he has an interest: if the first, it is plain that he has helped with an army corps, and
if the second, which is the more regular, whenever it leaves the house of Sweden it returns to the body of the Empire
and it falls to the Emperor to give the investiture to whom he pleases. With this worry the Imperial ministers came to
me; and having learned that our whole party rested on keeping the Elector firm, I told them so, and so I took the course
of going myself to offer mountains and valleys to the Brandenburgers, and that the Emperor was on their master's side,
pointing out to them at the same time that they would ruin themselves if they asked for Pomerania in their proposals;
and the expedient was agreed that the Imperialists and they should make them in general terms, and the Imperial
ambassadors did very good offices to this end. And so many are the tangles that have had to be unravelled that I would
have much to tell Your Lordship, and for this reason I am not at ease. It is true that there have been letters, in good
ink and with good doubloons, from the minister the Elector keeps in Madrid, with which they must have made a start with
him; the Brandenburgers showed me the copy today, and they declared themselves to me with more sincerity in good form;
but God above all.

Three days ago [?Mulle], minister of Celle who resides at The Hague, left here, and although he was incognito he made
each of us a bow with a proposal in two parts: the one contains the request for their passports with the rank of
ambassadors, and the second that the treating of the peace be suspended until they had obtained them; which, besides
being shameless under the beard of such great kings and such powerful princes, is a step that already makes the
neutrality stink. I answered him that he should speak to the others, that our master would not be the difficult one;
and besides this he did not speak of subsidies, which to me is a more fatal sign. But if Brandenburg holds firm, it may
be that Brunswick and Münster will put on a better air, and in that case I shall be at ease that there is no question of
a separate peace with Sweden.

Although I have made the exchanges of the new full power with France, with the Goths we are still where we were,
because Sweden claims that, as ours is in Spanish, hers must be in Swedish; but I believe they will now get off their
donkey, seeing that we allies have already handed in our proposals, which at last today at noon were left in the hands
of the mediators, and soon we shall have the handing over of those of our enemies, so that the first volume of this
negotiation is now on the market square. Ours are the enclosed, accompanied by the letter that goes with them; and
after the incense that had to be burned in the preamble, the case is made for asking that we be restored to the state
of the year '65. In justice they could not be more right, but by the law of arms and in our weakness a flick of the
finger will knock them down. I did not think fit to frame them by asking separately for each of the things included
from the year '65 until now, so as not to cause greater horror by asking for the Peace of the Pyrenees, which would also
run against the wish to improve, if possible, the Treaty of Westphalia in favour of the Burgundian Circle, and so that
the Lord Emperor should find no difficulty in assisting us; and so as not to touch that key, and to leave the door open
in case God wills some good success to enter by it, the sixth article was put in with that preamble on the damage that
experience had shown. The second is as much a matter of course to put in the proposals as not to be granted in the
treaties; I say the same of the fourth and fifth, but this bluster will serve for our Flemings. As the case of Messina
is of a different nature, it is put in a separate article. The last is the allies', which is "here peace and afterwards
glory": God give us the first as soon as possible and the second afterwards.
