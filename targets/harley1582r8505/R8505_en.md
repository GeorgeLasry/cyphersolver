### f. 263r

The State of the King of Navarre and of his Party in France.

The King of Navarre must be considered first in the quality in which by the grace of God he was born; secondly in
that to which it has since pleased God to call him, namely as first Prince of the Blood and head of the house of
Bourbon, and as head of the Reformed Churches of France.

In the first quality, his authority [?can] only grow, the King being, as people think, quite without hope, and
Monseigneur far from having children, the one having been married so long without having any and the other not yet
married. That is why all good Frenchmen begin to cast their eyes on him and will try more and more to win his favour
in advance. Besides, the harm they suffer from the present government of [81] and what they expect of [21] in the
future, [?through] the examples and samples he gives of it, lends great lustre to [51], of whom no one has cause to
complain and whom no one can so far reproach with any perfidy or oppression whatever.

[…] also, of which we have been [?modestly] [informed], has made the people forget the wounds of the civil war, the
hatred of which was always laid on us while [?it lasted]. It has suffered so much, either from the new taxes of [81]
or from the depredations of the troops of [21], that they have [?served] [?its] hatred, and the King of Navarre in
some way their goodwill, being […] that one has to complain of all [?…] rather than of him.

What would greatly increase his credit, if it could safely be done: that the King of Navarre should come a little
nearer the centre of France, being a handsome, agreeable, adroit prince, endowed with all the parts needed to win the
heart of the nobility. And it is likely that he [?will have], given even the disdain of the others, the greater part
seeking him out and taking a liking to him.

would have changed [?…] of the [?tavern], but only to drink there [?…] even more.

The Religion of which the King of Navarre makes open profession is indeed an obstacle to [?his entering] so far and so
soon into the heart of the people. But the wisest do not cease to say that a prince who holds one religion firmly is
worth far more than one who holds none at all; that things in France are brought to the point that it cannot be at
peace without the two religions. And there is no doubt that if one saw the King of Navarre as well allied, and [?as
well regarded] abroad [?as] he is […] at home, many notable persons of every rank, who are extremely weary of the
abuses of the Roman Church and the corruptions of the present state, would [?come over] to the Religion and to his
