# BnF fr. 3091 no. 23, ff. 45r-47v: Gabriel de Gramont, bishop of Tarbes, Rome, to Montmorency, 11 October [1529]: English translation

Translation (Claude Opus 5.5, 23 Oct 2026) of the reading in `fr3091_no23_gramont.md` (section "Reading"), the
decryption with Lasry's reconstructed key of the cipher, which fills the whole letter apart from the clear opening
and close. Same holes and marks: `[...]` unread or a page join, `(?)` and `[word?]` doubtful (here `[?word]`),
`[!]` a sign that does not fit, code signs in brackets with their probable meaning, `[word]` a word supplied.

## Translation

**f. 45r.** Monseigneur, I see that, both by my letters of the thirteenth and of the seventh of September [and by
the] despatch of the bull of the four tenths and other things which the King of Navarre's maître d'hôtel brings you,
you will have been made sure that, if I have heretofore written to the [King] and to you of the good will of Our Holy
Father, I have done it more with clear knowledge of it by deeds than by light belief or any other extraordinary
wish that moved me to it. And I can tell you that, the further I go, the more I confirm my opinion, inasmuch as I
see ordinarily and clearly that the greatest wish our said Holy Father has is to remain in true friendship and union
with the [King]; and that whatever good word or show he may have from [?the Emperor], this only increases in him a
distrust he has conceived, so that he cannot persuade himself that anything but harm and mischief can come to him
from that side, although he shows that it is all the contrary. And to make him trust, nothing [?served] in any thing
[like] what the [King] commanded me to tell him of the general council, which he feared above all things in this
world, although he showed and shows to everyone that he greatly wishes it and is pursuing it. And at the beginning,
before he gave me entire faith, he would have liked to make me believe as much. The whole being debated and my
reasons heard, he was forced to declare himself [... f. 45v]

**f. 45v.** [... the Pope ... to] confess that he knew very well that what was being pursued, the said council, was
to ruin him; and that he had resolved, while granting it in [?principle], nevertheless to stop on some particulars to
put it off, and that the delay should end for him in a breakdown. And that, since I assured him of the [King]'s good
will and of the friendship he bore him, he wished to throw himself entirely into his arms and trust in him alone.
And because the time does not allow showing distrust, he is content (provided that the goodwill always remains
whole) that, as often as there is need, and [that it] will be to his profit, the said lord [the King], both himself
and through his ministers, should say and have it said that he has little reason to trust him, and that he should
proceed boldly onward as he sees fit to do; for he sees that the temper of the people one has to deal with requires
that and a greater thing. You know, as I have often written to you, that with good words you will have from him what
you wish; and do not fear that he will want to make any foundation or profit of it with the said [sign: ?the
Emperor], for he aims at nothing but to send him out of Italy, whatever may befall him. And believe that Monsieur the
Admiral will not have much to do, given the disposition in which I have put him, to make him consent to what might be
done in favour of the [King] for the allies and confederates. And although for [... f. 46r]

**f. 46r.** [... f. 45v] ... [to] be [!] bound by his articles, as you know, and that he were forced, to keep them
[?], to make the said allies come to full restitution or else make war on them. He puts a good face on it at the
beginning [to see] whether he will come; he has what I tell you. Of which I have advised my said lord the Admiral, so
that he may look well ahead to conducting his charge, and that he not fail to come as far as he can with the said
[!] in the other matters, thinking to find difficulty in this one. And moreover I have written to him that, if he
found it good, what our said Holy Father has told me [?], in case he delayed his journey, or else wholly played the
sick man in some town [where] he was, [he is] ready to do everything; and he has already begun to complain, so that,
if there were need, the thing should not seem so sudden. He does everything possible, both by word and through his
ministers, few as they are, in whom he trusts, to keep in with me, begging me to keep him in the good grace of the
[♡ = ?the King] [sign]. And if I saw that the said lord had to negotiate something with him in which there could be
profit or damage for the one or the other, I would keep on my guard; but seeing that he asks for nothing, and that
the said lord has no quarrel to settle with him [... f. 46v]

**f. 46v.** [... from 46r] and that in all things I ask of him on the said lord's behalf, whether there be no profit
in them and sometimes there be damage, he gratifies me in them. I must tell you that he goes about it in good faith;
or otherwise he would be the greatest fool I ever saw, seeing that he would give at every hour without hope of
having anything. And although he is pope, and ought not to give credit (as I believe he does not) to the sayings of
a great many people who meddle with divining, yet I can assure you that, when they say something of him, if it is
ill, he fears it greatly. And there are several who have said that he must be deposed, or must die in the coming
year, and that the whole must come to him from the side of the said [sign: ?the Emperor]. And believe that he has a
wondrous fancy about it; and to keep him in it all the more, the night before he left this city he had a dream,
which I send you, which has put him into a wondrously great fancy, inasmuch as he says that he dreamed it at the
[same] hour at which at other times he has dreamed several things that have befallen him. And he has not spoken of
the said dream except with great mistrust, telling nothing of it except to one man alone. For this cause I beg you
that it [... f. 47r]

**f. 47r.** [... that the thing] be not published, and that you be content that the King, Madame and those whom it
pleases them should alone know it. I shall leave tomorrow to go to Perugia, where I am to find him, and on the way I
expect to have news of the said lord the Admiral. I have made your commendations to our said Holy Father, and showed
him your letters, because they were good for these ends; and believe that he had as great contentment from them as
he ever had from anything in his life, and it will only be up to you if you do not have him do entirely all that will
be for the King's service, the conduct and the furtherance of his affairs. And he was also greatly satisfied with
what I told him on behalf of the said lord [the Admiral], by the command he was pleased to give me of it in his last
letters. Do not be astonished, if you please, if for some time from now you do not have news of me more often, for
the road will be the cause of it, besides which all must be advised to the said lord the Admiral, who will let you
know it. I have been informed that the Prince of Orange wished to act as go-between with the [⊞ = ?the Florentines] to
restore the [▯ = ?the Pope / the Medici] to the state of Florence as he used [to be], and that they should hand over
Pisa and Livorno and [?put them] into the hands of [Ꝭ = ?the Emperor], which is a thing greatly contrary to what the
said [Ꝭ] has agreed by treaty with the [▯].

**f. 47v.** And I believe he has learned of it, for yesterday evening he let me know how he had begun to play the
sick man while waiting for news of Monsieur the Admiral, and that if, at his departure from this city, he was in good
will, he was at this hour in [?] good will.

**Clear close.** Monseigneur, I commend myself very humbly to your good grace, and pray God to give you [in good]
health a long life. At Rome, the 11th day of October. Your humble servant, G. de Gramont, b[ishop] of Tarbes.
