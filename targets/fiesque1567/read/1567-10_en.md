# Fiesque to Catherine de' Medici, Vienna, 11 October 1567 (cipher passages)

⟨Madam, my last letter was of the [25th?] of last month, and since then I have not been able to speak to the Emperor until last Wednesday, for he does not leave his chamber and cannot stand on his feet.⟩

[v006r] I told him that when I took leave of you to come here, you [?commanded] me to find out whether what he had told you was true: that His Majesty, complaining of the disorders in Christendom on account of religion, [...] well that it was necessary for the Christian princes to put good order to it, and that if the others would take heed, he [?would not spare himself] on his side to take pains to bring about a good union; that if it were so, you [?would not spare yourself] in this good work, but [?would put] your hand to it on this side, and [?would bring it about] that the King agreed to it, although his kingdom is [Dz] in good peace and well obedient to his commands, because it would be very easy to see the whole of Christendom in good peace and union. I begged His Majesty to do me the favour of telling me his will, so that I could inform you. He told me it was true that, seeing so many disorders, he could do no less than complain [?bitterly] of the condition of the times, and that he put this [?misfortune] down only to the disunion of the Christian princes, who are more inclined to their private interest than to the public good,

[v007l] and blinded by their own interest, have [?made] the evil known so far that for his part he will spare nothing in his power to restore Christendom to good peace and union; and [?he greatly praises] this good will, of which he has always been well assured, saying that you have governed yourself so wisely that the King and his kingdom remained in good peace and quiet; and he thanked God for this good inspiration He has given you, to be willing to [?apply yourself] to so good a work. And because the matter is of great consequence and there will be many difficulties, he wishes to think about it and [?take counsel], and afterwards he will give me an answer.

I told him I am sure that if he would set about it [?in earnest], on this side you will show that it will not be for want of you that good order is put [?into the union], and that I hope it could come to a good conclusion, given that the greatest forces of Christendom [?are] his, the King's and the King of Spain's; that there is no doubt the rest will fall in with whatever they [?agree], and that His Majesty [...] it would profit him more than the others, for if by his means the said [v007r] [?were] helped in their need [...].

He told me he had well understood all that, and that God knows how he has always wished to see Christendom in good union, and told me again that he would think about it and soon give me an answer. It seemed to me that he has taken a liking to the proposal; still we can say nothing until his answer has come, and so I have kept back the courier to send you the decision, and have written you this letter so that you are not troubled by so long a delay.

Since then he has told me that he would have cause to complain of the King, of the bad offices his ambassador does against him at Constantinople, but that he does not complain of it [...] he will not believe it. I told him that what he had just said to me, the other time I was here, people were spreading the same rumour, that the King's ambassador was [?urging] the Turk at Constantinople to come and do harm to His Majesty, [?yet] that the thing was very far from the truth; and that I begged him to [...] that he [?refrain] from believing the ill-disposed, and that there are [?more] people in the world who seek nothing but disunion between him and the King, [...], showing by several reasons that [v008l] [...] that he could well know that you have always shown him good affection.

And he told me it might well be that this ambassador had done these bad offices of his own accord without [?having] known more; that he had been informed that an ambassador whom you are sending to Constantinople had passed through Venice. I told him I did not know that you were sending one there, but that I was quite sure you would negotiate nothing to his prejudice, and that if that were done it would be contrary to the good will you have to work for the said union; and I begged him to [?believe] that you proceed in it with the best and most sincere friendship [...] that is possible. Please let me know what I am to say and answer him on this matter, in case he speaks to me of it.

[?Further] I have not been able to learn anything about the marriage of the daughters of [...], except that in making the marriage of the eldest he would wish [?to be elected] King of the Romans, and that [...] jewels nor anything else touching this marriage; but that he [...] the second [?with] the King of Portugal; of which things [...] a courier has been dispatched to Spain, about which I have been able to learn nothing. [v008r] I [?will try] [?hard] to inquire into it, but I must go about it very discreetly.
