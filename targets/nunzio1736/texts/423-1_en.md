# Spagna 423/1 (DECODE R26), Rome, 14 July 1736. English translation of 423-1_reading.md

Same marks as the reading. N.S. = Nostro Signore, the Pope (Clement XII); S. Sede = the Holy See.

To what I write at length in clear, I think it necessary to add, in answer to your ciphers of the eighth of last month,
that His Holiness has been most deeply embittered to hear how that Mons. Molina, forgetting the obligations of a
bishop and a regular, should join, in this juncture too, in the riots against the Holy See, and, to excuse conduct so
irregular in everyone's judgement, should put forward there the affair of Donghi and the satisfactions granted to the
Emperor.

I will leave aside that the violence of others should never serve as an example, and that it is a great scandal that
princes, against every principle of piety and justice and far from the footsteps of their predecessors, should now
seem to make it a point of honour who most insults and oppresses the Holy See.

But in the very affair of Donghi, although His Holiness yielded under pressure to the satisfactions requested, the
Emperor's demands were in the end limited to those against Mons. the Governor and his officers, whom His Caesarean
Majesty claimed to have failed; nor did it [the Imperial court] ever press for the achievement of its other particular
claims on the Holy See; and yet we were dealing with a prince who had long reproached us with being too partial to the
Crown of Spain.

That Court, on the other hand, which His Holiness was always to hope would be more benevolent to him, not content with
the excessive indulgence His Holiness has shown in granting what had been negotiated in Naples in satisfaction of the
affair of the riot, after all those [satisfactions] it had taken with such violence, now of its own accord unexpectedly
goes on to claim things that have no connection whatever with the riots, and that strike directly at His Holiness, as
if he had stirred up the populace and had not rather used every means to defend the Spanish nation, even with the
lives of others of his subjects, when not a single Spaniard was wounded or killed.

Then, to justify the manner of the enlistments that took place, when the recruiting itself was forbidden by old and new
edicts: if it will not believe us and the trials conducted scrupulously by public authority, Rome is full of foreign,
neutral nations and ministers to whom this undeniable truth is well known; and it is a great misfortune and an
injustice that the ear has been kept open only to the reports of those whose interest it is, to excuse their own
conduct, to deny facts so plain.

It is likewise known to everyone how many officers of the troops of Naples were in this city, with houses taken on
lease to keep the enlisted men in, and that eight days before the first riot Cardinal Acquaviva himself, to whom we
had made repeated representations, rebuked in his public antechamber some of those officers who were thought the most
guilty.

This was not the due and sufficient remedy, but it proves all the more the existence of the disorder that it is now
claimed to deny.

It seems, lastly, that to confirm ever more this evident truth about what the recruiters did in Rome, the Lord God has
allowed the same practice to begin lately in Florence; but the Duke of Montemar, according to the reports received
from that city, cut out the root of the evil at the very start and applied the proper remedy, which was not done in
Rome by those who would have had an equal, if not a stricter, obligation to do so.
