# Alfonso de Exea, archbishop of Seville, to Francesc Climent, Perpignan, 23 Aug 1408: English

Translation of `exea1408_reading.md` (Puig y Puig 1920, app. LXIV, pp. 500-503). Puig's deciphered passages are the
whole letter after the opening formula. Names in modern form; Puig's identifications in brackets.

## Translation

Reverend father and my dearest lord, know that the Cardinal of Spain [Pedro de Frías] has played a Trebellian trick
on us, as I suspected from the start, Your Holiness. He feigned illness when our lord left Portovenere, and several
days after our lord's departure he came to Genoa. His Holiness the Patriarch [Simon de Cramaud] was with him at once
and presented letters of the King of France, on whose behalf he urged and begged him, and also persuaded him, to be
willing to go to Paris and be with them in the matter of the union, for the King wanted to see and deliberate certain
things with him. The traitor who presented the letters drew him to it with many speeches, promising him much, and that
he need not care for what he held in Spain: if the Pope took it from him, as he assured him, as it is said, he gave
him an undertaking on the King's behalf that in France he would be given the like twice over, in benefices as well as
in vacant churches, and in particular promised him the church of Rouen, and that the King of France would never fail
him and would do wonders for him. Led on by this, the said Cardinal at once agreed and announced his departure for
Paris, and sent one of his household to our lord about it, and sent him the summons the Patriarch had made to him with
a public instrument; and the squire he sent boasted that once the Cardinal was in Paris he would go on from there into
Castile, which is very likely, since he wants nothing but to set foot in the kingdom, above all at the time of this
discord between the Queen and the Infante [Fernando], to do his usual work. Therefore our lord has decided that hands
be laid at once, in the name of the Camera, on all his revenues, of the bishopric as well as of the benefices; and
since a bull on this cannot be drawn up at once and addressed to you in due form, it was decided that he would signify
his intention to you by a closed letter sent to you quickly, because there is danger in delay, and afterwards bulls in
due form will be sent to you, especially as the general powers you hold in the acts of the Camera are enough for
carrying this out by the Pope's mandate, of which you can give proof with the letter sent to you. So it is needful in
this case that you work quickly, beyond your strength, to have the aforesaid carried out at once. And since the
greater difficulty will be over the castles, because on the face of it I believe the alcaides will not hand the
castles over to you so easily, before all else you must speak secretly with the Queen; she may give some help or
provisions for that execution, since you can show her the Pope's intention and will by the letter that will be sent
to you. See also whether it would be expedient or safe to have recourse to the Infante over this, since on the face of
it it is doubtful whether he would acquiesce or make trouble; on the other hand it seems it cannot be done without him
together with the Queen, which seems difficult because of the discord between them; yet do what you can and what you
see to be expedient according to the state of affairs there. As for the other things, which are plain, that is the
revenues of the church as well as of the benefices, where there is no such difficulty, send at once, especially to
the church of Osma, a sure and reliable person with your powers. The arrest must be made most firmly and generally,
taking in fact into the hands of the Camera corn, wine and whatever else is found, ordering the debtors, farmers and
all others in no way to answer to the said Cardinal, but at your order; let the church farms and everything else of
the revenues of the said bishopric be made by you in the name of the Camera. Send to the subcollectors of every diocese
in which he holds benefices, that in the name of the Camera they arrest the said Cardinal's benefices and receive
their fruits in the name of the Camera; let the farmers and debtors be ordered not to answer to the said Cardinal but
to the said subcollectors, in the name of the Camera. In this matter it is superfluous to write to you at length, for
it would be teaching Minerva; and our lord ordered me that in this article you and the lord of Toledo [Pedro de Luna],
but chiefly you, whose office it concerns, apply every diligence, even beyond your strength, that the aforesaid be
carried out at once, because once this is done I think a provision must be made to others, the axe laid to the roots,
and the deprivation follow together with the provision to others, so that the possessions of the church as well as of
the benefices are taken over at once by the others whom the Pope provides, so that everything is already occupied by
others and the roots of this bad weed are torn wholly out of the kingdom; otherwise, in my judgement, we do nothing.
On which our lord must fully decide which persons to provide, especially to the church; and first there is the doubt
or difficulty of the provision of the church of Osma, for which on the face of it it seems it should be a man who is
noble and powerful, who could easily take possession of the said church and could also have the favour of some, of
the King, or in the Queen, or in the Infante, or in both.

Four occur to me. First, the archdeacon of Toledo; Alfonso Carrillo [later cardinal of S. Eustachio]; Gutierre Gómez
[archdeacon of Guadalajara]; and the abbot of Salas, a kinsman of Diego López, which Diego is a neighbour of those
parts, or near, and powerful over them. Of all of them I would choose the archdeacon of Toledo, who is noble by birth
and has enough kin and friends, is himself prudent, industrious and diligent in business. For him the Queen lately
pleaded earnestly with Pedro Fernández, the King's secretary, saying that the supplication she had made earlier for
the bishop of Plasencia she had made for the occasion; and yet I believe he is now on good terms with the Infante,
especially after what he brought him; he is the Pope's creature and most faithful to him above other clerics to be
promoted; before leaving he begged the Pope very earnestly about it, and our lord had told him plainly that he wanted
to promote him. But this objection is easily answered: since it serves God, the Church and our lord, once the
provision was made he would have to be enjoined to accept it altogether, under precept of obedience and even sentence
of excommunication, and so the unwilling would become willing. Of the second, Alfonso Carrillo: for him speak the
nobility of his family and his kin, though they are far from those parts; he himself is, I will not say good, but
excellent, capable and worthy of every honour, our lord's own man, and one who owes so much to the lord of Toledo. I
believe he would be acceptable to the Queen because of the supplications made for him at other times, and the son of
Leonor López would have his archdeaconry, as was asked. As to the Infante, I have been given to understand by one of
the lord Infante's men that Gómez Carrillo, his father, is not in the Infante's anger but rather more in his favour
than you would believe; I think this comes from the dissolution of his son's marriage with the daughter of the said
Leonor López, of which, it is said, he complains and thinks himself wronged. Against him: in the absence of the said
Alfonso, who is here, the business could not so easily be carried out there by another, nor could his father and
other kinsmen bring the remedy so well; and even if he were present he is not as experienced in these things as the
archdeacon of Toledo, unless the lord of Toledo had his place filled in this. Of the third, Gutierre Gómez, who is the
Cardinal's enemy: in him are nobility of family and the capacity of the person, and I believe he would know how to
carry it out diligently by himself and through his people, and that it would please the Infante, to whom he adheres.
Two serious things stand against him: first, I believe it would be most displeasing to the Queen, who would be
scandalised; second, which burns no less, I believe he will never be faithful to the Pope, nor has he, I believe, a
good intention towards his service, unless he were changed by a benefice received; he is presumptuous, I will not say
proud and malicious, and we might be planting bad wheat in our vineyard that could harm in the future. Yet I name him
because I once saw our lord inclined to provide him with some church when the case arose, and our lord's intention was
to satisfy past ambition and the ratification of the lord of Toledo's affairs; the said Gutierre would not despair,
that is. Of the fourth, the kinsman of Diego López: though in his own person there is not so much capacity, yet out of
regard for Diego López and his faithfulness I believe he would carry it out powerfully, and I believe the said abbot
is faithful to the Pope; but it stands strongly against him that perhaps the Queen or the Infante or both would think
themselves wronged by it, and in the present state of things it could harm the affairs of the Pope and the Church. Of
them all, in my judgement, I prefer the archdeacon of Toledo, with whose benefices one great person could be gratified,
either on the Queen's side, since she pleads for the son of Leonor López, or on the Infante's, or the said Alfonso
Carrillo, should his promotion be otherwise blocked, which I would much regret. The man of Plasencia [Vicente Arias
de Balboa] also seems reasonable, as the Queen made known; yet it stands against him that he is not powerful in kin,
unless Juan de Velasco and Diego López, his friends, helped him; also he is hateful to the Infante, who wrote
expressly that he should in no way be provided with Salamanca, unless perhaps the Infante were placated by providing
Salamanca to his liking, and Carrillo would be left frustrated on every side, which would be a pity. Of all this I have
said nothing yet to our lord, but I write to you on my own initiative so that, after deliberating with the lord of
Toledo and also with the said archdeacon, all three of you there take counsel on it and write your view of which
person, all this considered, it would serve our lord to provide to the said church of Osma, according to the state of
affairs there at present, since our lord might decide something here that is reasonable at first sight which, given
the state of things there, would perhaps not be expedient. So I ask you to write me by the bearer of these your
opinions and the lord of Toledo's and also the lord archdeacon's, whom you will tell from me that where it seems to
serve our lord concerning his person he should not commit himself elsewhere, for it must be so.

Know further that five or six days ago a cleric of the bishop of Cartagena [Pablo de Santa María] arrived here, who
told by word of mouth that on the fourth or fifth day of the month after the feast of St James the Infante sent for
all the prelates who were at court and told them hard words, that they were the cause of the discord between the Queen
and himself, rebuking them very sharply and speaking many hard words; among other things he said he was on the point
of imprisoning them, but in the end he ordered them to leave the court at once and go to their homes. To the lord of
Toledo in particular he said that though he was not as much at fault as the others, he could not deny that he had
openly taken the Queen's side against him, and that they all were the cause of the scandals that could follow in the
kingdom from this discord; and that the lord of Toledo should beware from now on not to keep such ways, or he would
put him in a place he would never come out of. And at once the bishops of Sigüenza [Juan], Plasencia, Cuenca [Diego
de Anaya Maldonado] and Mondoñedo [Álvaro de Isorna] left the court, and the bishop of Sigüenza was expressly ordered
not to enter Hita, which lay on his road and where Juan de Velasco and Diego López were, nor to speak with them. Then
the said cleric told that on the same day the Infante sent doctor Juan Rodríguez to the Queen's palace with two
notaries, and in the said Queen's presence ordered Leonor López to leave the court at once; and the Queen began to
weep, scratch herself and cry out, but in the end it had to be done, and the said cleric said he had seen her leave;
moreover, Gómez Carrillo had been ordered on the Infante's behalf, on pain of treason, not to take the King out of the
said palace or alcázar; the Infante's men-at-arms guarded the Queen's palace from outside night and day and held the
town gates, so that no one could go in or out without his knowledge; and the Infante had written about this to all the
cities of the kingdom, and a new assembly was to be held on it, for his intention was good and for the King's service
and the good of the kingdom. Some others who came afterwards told the same or similar things, and some of them say
that the lord of Toledo shut himself in the castle with the Queen and has not come out. Hearing this our lord was
troubled, and all the rest of us, and not without cause, because of the scandals that may follow from it in the
affairs of the kingdom as well as of the Church. And the Pope can hardly believe it, because neither the lord of
Toledo nor you wrote anything, though it is almost a month since this happened; and if perhaps you had no way or
opportunity to write, he says you should at least have sent some discreet person without letters through whom the Pope
could be informed of everything; and if it is so, he reproves you and the said lord of Toledo strongly for such
negligence, unless perhaps the messenger you sent has been taken or died on the road. And though I believe that before
this messenger reaches you we shall already have sure news of you on this matter, in doubt our lord orders that by him
you write at length everything that has happened in this matter and the state of affairs, because our lord will
decide finally on what doctor Gonzalo Sánchez and Pedro Fernández, the King's secretary, brought, once full certainty
of the aforesaid is had. After the above was written there is a presumption that the Cardinal is pretending to go to
Paris in order to reassure the Pope about his journey to Castile, and that, being this side of Avignon, through which
he will not pass, he will keep his road straight to Carcassonne and Toulouse and from there through Gascony and
Navarre put himself into Castile; or, supposing he goes to Paris, without doubt he will negotiate there to be sent to
Castile on the King of France's behalf. In either case the danger is great, but more in the first because of the
shortness of time, since he is already on the road; in the second there is more delay, and before then, with God's
help, his affairs will be settled. So our lord's intention is that meanwhile the lord of Toledo think and provide for
remedies both in Aragon and in Castile, for his intention is that on the person's entry it be dealt with; as to
conscience, the Pope has fully provided. Meanwhile think, the lord of Toledo and you and the said archdeacon, about
these things, and meanwhile make what provision you can for the execution when the case arises; and a sure person
will shortly be sent to you who will tell the lord of Toledo and you by word of mouth our lord's mind and the practice
to be kept in this business. Also, the Pope's letter addressed to you bears the date of a past time, so that it
cannot be said that the Pope ordered what it contains only after he knew the Cardinal was going to France, moved by
that alone. Also, since the archdeacon of Toledo left here I have received all your letters, sent first by certain
monks of Galicia elected abbots, second by the courier I sent you, whom I free [?], third by Gonzalo Sánchez, the
Infante's doctor, and Pedro Fernández, the King's secretary. Last I received your short letter dated St James's day,
which Guillem de Fonollet gave me, who left here lately. The Pope also received a long letter from the lord of Toledo
and I a short one, by his own messenger, by whom I reply; let an answer be given both to him and to you on everything
in the said letters, for at present there is neither time nor place; meanwhile put diligence again into what this
letter contains. Written at Perpignan, the twenty-third of August. Alfonso, archbishop of Seville.
