## Translation

### f. 13r

At Genoa, besides what has been said was discussed with the Duke of Castro concerning the meeting with the Pope, he
had it said and proposed to us through the Marqués del Vasto that, if we would give the investiture of the State of
Milan to the Duke of Camerino, His Holiness would give us two million in gold in cash for the needs and matters that
arise for us, and a census every year; indicating that, this being done, Parma and Piacenza could also be joined to the
said State, and that, the forces remaining in my power and with other conditions, a good settlement could be made, as
would suit our service and the public good of Italy. And having understood the offer, and thought and looked well into
it, and with the opinion of the said Marqués del Vasto.

First, those [considerations] that could incline [us] to treat of the disposal of the said State, which are the
burden, labour and expense that we bear and must bear continually for the preservation of this State, and the
condition and hazard in which it is at present, and that some great harm could follow hereafter from not being able to
provide fully what would be needed for it, because of the needs we have.

The sum of money that is offered, which, for the matters we have in hand, you can well judge whether it would be
necessary to us, and the reputation it would give us with all our enemies, who with this regard, and [?seeing] the
State disposed of, might perhaps be brought to peace; and if that were had and we could keep some part of the money,
we could redeem a good part of the things sold from my patrimony; and if we were forced into war, spending it we could
with the help of Our Lord obtain our due, and perhaps achieve other effects of no less importance than the State of
Milan, and also bring those of Germany to reason.

### f. 13v

That the State would be given according to the nature of the fief, and, giving it to the Duke of Camerino and our
daughter, the King of the Romans our brother and everyone would hold it good; and, there being children of the
Duchess our daughter, it would pass to our grandchildren.

That it would also be to the satisfaction of the potentates of Italy, since it has always been understood that they
wished it to be given to an Italian person, and one not powerful.

And placing this State in the Duke, he being our son, with the fortresses remaining in our hand under the order that
would be given, it would always be as if we held the State ourselves.

To join the cities of Parma and Piacenza [to it] would be a great strengthening and security of the State, and a means
for it to sustain itself better, because they are lands with large revenues; and the league that could be made with
the potentates of Italy for its defence, with the conditions that would be seen to be suitable, [would be] of great
importance for its security and for the affairs of Italy; which one could hope would last at least during the life of
the Pope, and perhaps afterwards; and meanwhile matters would settle, and with time many things could be done that
cannot be achieved now; and we would find ourselves relieved of the great burden and expense we bear now, and could
better attend to and provide for the other parts.

The regard that must be had to the negotiations that there are between the Pope and the French, to divert and break
them off, and to remove the suspicions and distrust that have been sown, and to confirm him by showing goodwill to the
advancement of his house.

On the other side one considers the quality, greatness and importance of this State; the authority it brings us, being
in our hand together with the imperial dignity, for the affairs of the Empire itself and of Italy and with its
potentates; and also the usefulness for the security and preservation of our Kingdom of Naples, because

### f. 13 bis r

if we did not hold this State, not without great difficulty, leaving it at their backs, enemies could pass to invade
that kingdom, and great forces would be needed for it; and resistance could be made with more ease and better in the
said State than in the kingdom itself.

What has many times, as you know, been considered: that by means of the disposal of this State peace could be
established with the King of France, and that, disposing of it otherwise, we do not [?see] how it could well be done;
though in this there is, on the other side, that only with difficulty could such securities be taken from him as would
be needed for the keeping of the peace; and if it were made by the said means, besides our losing the authority and
usefulness we have with the said State and passing all of it to the said King of France, one may doubt, and judge for
certain, that holding the said State and finding himself with more forces and convenience by it, he would wish, and
would not fail to try, to extend himself and occupy and make himself master of whatever else of Italy he wished, which
could hardly be defended against him; so that for the said State to remain with the said King of France, or at his
discretion and will, could not be without prejudice to our authority and affairs; and though this would be so at any
time, at present, [?he] having broken the truce and made war and continuing it as he does, it would also be with great
loss of reputation.

That he would more easily think of having the said State if it were in another's hand than in ours, because that one
could not defend it as we can; and that by nature the Pope cannot live many years, and he failing, there could be no
great security in the league that would be made, and the burden would always remain on us.

### f. 13 bis v

The bad name that could be had [from] selling the said State and receiving the money of His Holiness, which ought to
be spent in the defence and benefit of Christendom.

The suspicion that the King of England and the Protestants of Germany could take from this negotiation and closer
friendship between His Holiness and us.

The said considerations.

And [by this] means we could be served, helped and succoured by the Duke with a good sum of money, up to one hundred
and fifty or two hundred thousand ducats, of which we are in extreme need, to be able to leave the Marqués del Vasto
some good provision of money for the affairs of Piedmont and of this State, and to resist the enemies if they wished
to make some [?movement] of troops and enterprise, and to do what according to time and occasion shall be seen fitting
and possible; which provision is very necessary and important not only for keeping the forces we have in Piedmont and
the State of Milan, but also to divert the enemies on the side of Flanders and Luxembourg. And having the said help of
the said Duke of Florence, we reckon that another one hundred and fifty or two hundred thousand ducats would be had
from the Kingdom of Naples, asking the barons to serve us with this amount for the needs that arise; and otherwise we
have no means of making any provision to the said Marqués del Vasto, and not making it, Piedmont and Milan and our
affairs in these parts would be left with the loss of reputation, danger and harm that you can consider, nor could we
achieve anything elsewhere, for these reasons. It seems […].

### f. 14r

We answer the said Prince that we think it well to change what had been agreed, since it is right to succour the
greatest need, and that thus with all the galleys they should go round towards Sicily and Naples with the greatest
speed possible, leaving it to him to do everything that can be done and that he sees most fitting to hinder and prevent
the damage that the said fleet could do in our kingdoms and states; holding it certain that in any case, whether it
pass to Naples or Sicily, or to La Goleta, Bugia and the coast of Africa, or to that of Spain, he with our galleys
should follow it and keep close to it, so as not to give it room to scatter or to supply itself with victuals or other
things, and to take away and hinder the conveniences they could have.

La Goleta, says the Viceroy of Sicily, is provided, and he has received letters that in these days a ship loaded with
provisions had gone there. We wrote to the said Prince that, once arrived in Sicily, he should have Don Francisco de
Tovar told what he learns the said fleet is doing, and whatever else he thinks fit; and in case it should go against La
Goleta or Bugia, since with its fleet in some port or place together, help could be given with ships in company with
the galleys, and doing so as he by his experience will best understand, we charge him to see to doing and providing all
that he thinks and sees fit; for which, when such need arises, we leave it to him to have the ships that are needed
taken in Sicily and Naples or wherever he finds them, and to provide all other necessary things, taking on exchange
what is needed for it; and that, as when it is needed it must be found and provided in any way that it can be done, so
we trust that otherwise he will take care not to enter into expense, knowing the needs there are; and when the enemies
should not come to our coasts, or coming should return to their seas, and

### f. 14v

he saw that the galleys of Spain could and should go back there, that he see that they would not go safely alone, and
that some of the others should go with them, as had been agreed, so that the French galleys should not be superior to
them nor any harm follow; and we do not doubt that in time he will see to it and provide so that they go with the
security needed; and that he should see how the Marqués de Aguilar and these despatches can pass, and that, if he
thought a pair of galleys could and should be left for these, since it would not be right to risk the person of the
Marqués on a voyage that was not safe, he should order it as he sees it can best be done.

Likewise, that he provide some ships in which the hundred light horse we are sending there may go, since, if the
galleys do not go to tow the vessels in which they were to pass, they cannot be taken otherwise.

That he see how the said two thousand Spanish infantry whom the said galleys were to bring can come, and whether they
should come to land at Genoa to serve in the State of Milan and Piedmont, or whether, considering the need there could
be in Sicily on account of the Turk's fleet, it would be better for them to go from Spain to land there in the ships
that would be found for their passage, and making their voyage by the islands of Majorca, Minorca and Ibiza [and]
Sardinia, if they had news that the said fleet had passed to the coast of Africa or of these kingdoms, the said two
thousand infantry could stay in the said islands where the need most required it; charging him to write there with
these despatches what he thinks about this, with the reasons and considerations that move him; and so we ask you that,
having seen what the said Prince writes, it be looked into, resolved and done as best and with most speed and security
it can be done, having the care that we trust you will of the provision of
