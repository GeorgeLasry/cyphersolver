# BnF fr. 3984 f. 186r-v (no. 88): Baudouin-Desportes to Pietro Aldobrandini, Paris, 22 July 1593: English translation

⟨Clear opening, summarised only: "Most Illustrious Monseigneur, if I have not written to His Holiness and to Your Most
Illustrious Lordship as often as duty commands me, two things have prevented me ..." (uncertain events, unsafe roads);
"... In short, unless God sets His hand to it, I see the security of the Catholic religion in this kingdom in a bad way,
for several reasons I could tell Your Most Illustrious Lordship ..."; what the ministers "here for the Holy See" report
has nothing to do with the truth, "which will now be found such as I have always told it, at the"⟩ cost of my head.

Beginning with Monsieur Matteucci, down to the one who remains here, they alone have brought more ruin to our affairs
than the enemy, so full have they been of I know not what that does not suit us, or of small knowledge of our humours
and passions. And if I put anything forward in the discourse I am giving to His Holiness on the conduct of the said
Matteucci, the least letter that anyone could write me about it will always let me show it, wherever one wishes, to
make it good. As for Monsieur de Malvasia, I was not there when he and Monsieur du Maine fell out; yet the nephew of the
Legate complained to me of it; and it is hard to contradict a rumour […]. And of yourself you can consider that the
army of the King of Spain did nothing but stay on the frontier, a reproach to so great a monarch, and withdrew on a mere
rumour that the King of Navarre was coming to seek it. Moreover the Legate, with no consideration at all, wants us to
proceed to the election of a king just when the enemy is beginning to win the voices of many who were his enemies, by the
show he makes of wishing to become Catholic; and he is to go to mass on Sunday. To have nothing to resist him with but
promises, by which we have been so often deceived! It would take many [?deeds] before we could be made to reject the
appearance of a peace; and it will be no small thing if we temper it by a truce, everyone being tired of being the talk
of all Christendom and of seeing ourselves abandoned, only to be brought to bay in the end. For as to promises, our
enemies do not lack them, and they offer to show letters of Your Most Illustrious Lordship [saying] that, if he becomes
Catholic, he will be received. The Holy See is tired of continuing its expense, which may be said to have been something
during the pontificate of [?Pope] Gregory; but since then it would have been better for it if we had been denied all hope
of help outright, so as to make us take a firm resolution. And let Your Lordship remember how urgently I asked for one of
the house of Bourbon, and the refusal I was given; then the marriage of the Infanta with one of our princes: I was
refused outright, and referred to the Legate and the Duke of Feria, to whom I think I made it plain enough that we would
never hazard our religion, honours and lives without other assurance; and that unless His Holiness spoke more [?clearly]
(for we want no assurances but his, he being the most concerned), no one would do better in future than in the past; and
I would have been most imprudent to act otherwise, I who know the Spaniards. That is what made me reject the candidate
His Holiness proposed to me, who in truth would have been the best; but one needed to see a [?sure] light in it. And even
if my master had been willing to let himself go that way, no one would have resisted him more than I. And where would he
be now? The enemies offer, further, to show a letter of His Holiness addressed to the Legate, by which he orders him to do
nothing to the prejudice of the house of Bourbon, against the fundamental laws of the kingdom, nor against the Catholics
who assist the King of Navarre: a thing quite contrary to so many protestations made to me. I have said it and will say
it until death: if harm comes of it, it was willed so; for we did not fail to remonstrate everything that could be said,
with no passion but religion. But amid these uncertainties we remain; and if the others use the occasion, do they not do
well? And for reward of all our pains I expect a world of reproaches, all falling on Monsieur du Maine, who does more than
he should, without any fear; for honour is worth more than all [?these] appearances sought by most men; and besides, the
end will crown the work. For at the time when […] is bent on tearing our state apart, which would bring after it the ruin
of Christendom, abandoned by everyone, he neglects all their [?warnings], and on them […] of persons who, but for their
quality of ministers of a great prince, would be neither out of rank nor of too good a house to serve as servants, he
takes heart against every kind of storm, to [?make] the sovereign and only remedy, his presence, so as to keep the state
and religion from an imminent fall. There is no doubt that there will be some who, as is their custom, will cover their
malice with a world of [?falsehoods]; but if their design has been made vain, there is no lack of persons able to write
good apologies, and they will turn back the shame that one would [?cast] on the face of those on whom there is only the
mark of honour and magnanimity. Would to God His Holiness knew as I do how his ministers have behaved, their interest, and
[that] of the one who makes a show of being nothing but religious, whereas there is nothing but pure ambition, too plainly
uncovered, in spite of a number of protestations to the contrary; and to which there was no means of replying if the
Legate had not put himself in the matter, in which the honour of His Holiness has been greatly engaged. I speak of his
affairs to Your Most Illustrious Lordship at great length; and if I did not fear to weary you, there would be enough to
fill a ream of paper. And not to be too tedious, I will tell you that if you knew as I do the artifice of our friends,
what they have [and] [?do], their weakness, how the Legate has shown himself their partisan to tear our poor state apart,
to the prejudice of the honour of the Holy See, and a world of other things, you would wonder a thousand and a thousand
times at the patience of Monsieur du Maine, who went so far as to be willing to accept Monsieur de Guise as king,
provided he was shown the means of being able to defend him. But they want us to believe in words, as if there were any
reason to trust further people who never showed us that they had any, and whose maxim is to deceive us. Perhaps you will
even find it strange that I have pointed out to you the two points that the enemies [?put about], both from the letter of
His Holiness and from that of Your Most Illustrious Lordship in particular; but it seemed good to me that you should be
told everything, to provide for it. It is true, and I have protested it a [?thousand] times, that if it pleases His
Holiness to let me know his intentions, for Monsieur du Maine, without putting the Legate between them, he will take
nothing for his rule but His commands. And besides, things between those two are on such terms that, but for respect for
His Holiness, he would no longer stay so long at his own cost and at the ruin of all those of his house. He has even been
so disrespectful as to make him fear that he is in the disfavour of [?His Holiness]; which everyone nonetheless thinks a
device to reach his own ends, so as to oblige the Spaniard, which has always been his principal aim.

⟨Most Illustrious Monseigneur, I most humbly pray the Creator to give Your Most Illustrious Lordship a most happy and most
long life. Paris, 22 July 1593. Your Most Illustrious Lordship's most humble and most obedient servant, Desportes.⟩
