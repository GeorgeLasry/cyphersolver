# Battier to Fagel, Madrid, 30 January 1687 (all cipher): English translation

My Lord. Since the last post I have consulted with Cantelmo how best to work for Castañaga, and we have thought it good
to do it underhand, for otherwise it could be harmful to Castañaga, because [?they] are so touchy here and do not want
their neighbours to meddle in their domestic affairs. The Emperor's ambassador has already spoken of it openly
everywhere, and the Master of the Horse of Aureis came to tell me that he had heard, on a visit of consequence, that His
Highness had written in Castañaga's favour, and that it was taken ill. I merely answered that I knew nothing of it, for I
noticed that he had come to sound me out in order to report back to the Emperor's ambassador. I have nevertheless done my
duty wherever I thought it necessary. Monterrey assures me that, to his knowledge, nothing has yet been resolved, and
perhaps will not be resolved so soon either. The King is distrustful and becomes obstinate when he thinks he notices
that people want to give away offices against his will; and the Duke of Medinaceli lost the King's favour chiefly by
wanting to make Don Pedro of Aragon President of Castile by force; and therefore Castañaga's friends do nothing but keep
His Majesty in his distrust. It had been said that he would be sent from Brussels to Peru as viceroy, which would
[?indeed] be better for him. [?Meanwhile], now and by the next post I shall do further duty. Otherwise there is still talk
of Melgar, Montalto and the Marquis of Leganés, but without the least certainty, and above all because there is no money
at hand.

It also seems that the Emperor's ambassador is still flattering himself; but if the Queen, who hates him greatly on
account of the Cantons affair, which he fomented so strongly, has once already been able to [make] him perplexed, she
will now too surely be able to dispose the King, to please her, not to visit him. The reason why the Emperor's
ambassador had the French courier waylaid is thought to be that he [?himself] had been to his commissary several times and
could not learn on what matter; he suspected that it was about consenting to turn the Twenty Years' Truce into a peace,
and that it was to be done behind the Emperor's back. He lately [?asked] me whether I did not know what Their High
Mightinesses thought of it; I said I did not, but that I could easily imagine that Their High Mightinesses had no other
interest than the general peace and the satisfaction of their [?allies], and that if His Imperial Majesty made peace with
the Turks, France would then apparently undertake nothing. He is perplexed and imagines that even the Emperor's friends
begrudge him his victories and would gladly see him forced by whatever [?] it may be to put an end to the Turkish war.
And when an express courier came from Brussels last Saturday, he was at once alarmed; but on Sunday it was learned that
it concerns only the settlement of [?Philippeville].

⟨Right Honourable Sir, Your Honour's humble and obedient servant Battier. Madrid, 30 January 1687⟩
