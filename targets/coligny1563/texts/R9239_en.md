# R9239: Coligny to Elizabeth I, Orléans, 24 January 1563: English translation

To the Queen of England.

Madam, since the capture of the Prince of Condé I have sent three despatches to Your Majesty, to keep you informed of the
state of affairs here, in keeping with the great desire I have always had, with this company, to let you know fully all
our principal actions (as is only too reasonable), if the difficulty of the roads and passages did not too often prevent
us. Now, with the convenience of this bearer, I did not wish to fail to write the present letter to Your Majesty, to
inform you how the said Prince of Condé, although very closely watched and guarded, has found the means of letting us
know his good news so openly that, instead of receiving consolation from us in his captivity, on the contrary he
strengthens our courage, and makes us well aware of the zeal and firm affection he has for the true religion: having
sent us word with assurance that, whatever may happen to him, he will never consent to anything against the service of
God and liberty of conscience, nor anything that offends the justice of our cause; using at the same time an urgent and
affectionate prayer and request to all those who have assisted him in so holy and praiseworthy an enterprise not to
abandon him at this time, nor the cause of God with him. Which it seemed to me I must not fail to make known to Your
Majesty; beseeching you most humbly, with this whole company, to be pleased to pursue the deliverance of the said Prince
of Condé, and to embrace this said cause even during the minority of our young King, which touches not only his liberty
and that of his kingdom and of consciences, but also, and principally, the service of God; employing for so good an
effect, and in so holy an enterprise, the great means that God has put in your hands, according to the true duty of the
kings and princes of the earth (among whom you hold so great a place), which is to maintain religion and help the
oppressed; and according also to the perfect trust this whole company has in your constancy and piety, from which we all
expect, after God, our principal aid and succour: recognising in you a virtue and divine assistance, and that God has
chosen and reserved you in this time, and presents you this occasion, so that by your means His pure service may be
raised up and re-established, and idolatry struck down throughout Christendom, and even in this kingdom; as all your
previous actions bear sufficient witness, and so many effects of your virtue and religion, as great and praiseworthy as
have been seen in any prince or princess in memory; Your Majesty having shown everywhere so evidently that you have no
other aim than the advancement of the glory of God: so that we have all taken this firm assurance that neither the
captivity of the Prince of Condé, nor the faults that might be laid against us, nor the weakness or diminution of our
forces, nor all the efforts of Satan, nor the ruses and artifices of our enemies, will have such power over you as to
lessen or cool in any way the good zeal and affection you have shown for it; rather they would add to it.

Now, to give you a good and full account of the state of our affairs, together with our needs, I shall tell you, Madam,
that, according to the treaty of association that Your Majesty has been able to see — the Prince of Condé having always
named me and given me charge to command this army and company in his absence — since his capture all those of this said
army, foreigners as well as those of this kingdom, have accepted and acknowledged me as chief, as everyone knows well
enough. And because the foreigners asked me after the battle to rest themselves, I put them in three towns on the river
Cher, which I took quite near our enemies. The latter, as they made a show of coming to besiege Orléans, part of their
army having crossed the bridge of Beaugency to put itself into a suburb called the Porte Reau, I moved closer to them:
which made them at once change their plan and recross the bridge. So that, to finish resting our said reiters afterwards,
I have since put them in other garrisons above Orléans, on both sides of the river, to keep it free; and for that purpose
I was forced to take by force, under the nose of our said enemies, some towns, where our said reiters and our cavalry are
now lodged; they number four thousand horse and more, determined to fight well when they are wanted.

All we fear is that the said reiters may become discontented at the delay in their pay for three months, which will be
owed them at the end of this one, amounting each month, for them and for their German foot soldiers, to six score
thousand livres: of which we had felt assured, both on the first offer that Your Majesty was pleased to make so
liberally to the Prince of Condé and to this company, and on the sixty thousand écus besides which the said Prince of
Condé asked of you through Monsieur de Briquemault. Which makes us all beseech Your Majesty most humbly to do us this
favour, to be pleased to put into execution what we have always expected and hoped from your goodness, so that this holy
enterprise may be brought to so happy an end that, according to your intention, the Gospel may have free course in this
kingdom, and that it may be delivered from the violence and tyranny by which it is oppressed. And to this end may it
please you to have the said sums held ready at Le Havre, where we shall go to take them and join your people, to go on
from there to complete, under the trust of this good God and by your good advice, what shall be found fitting: beseeching
you most humbly also to write a letter to the Marshal of Hesse, that he continue to exert himself well in this cause and
for the liberty of the Prince of Condé.

For the rest, Madam, I will not omit to tell you that there is talk of a meeting between the Prince of Condé and the
Constable, put forward by the Queen Mother, to seek means of agreement and pacification: should it take place, I shall
not fail to inform you of it at once, and Your Majesty in particular; assuring you that with my consent nothing will
ever be settled in this matter without including you, and without your being informed first, so as to have your advice
on it. And although matters went quite far before Paris, I can tell you in truth, Madam, that our intention was first to
settle the point of religion (for which we took up arms lawfully), and to make known by what spirit we are led, so as to
inform you afterwards of everything, learn your advice, and put forward what concerns you: a thing quite easy to see from
this, that the Prince of Condé himself made no mention of the rank that belongs to him in this kingdom, nor of other
things which at the same time it was necessary to settle before concluding a good and sure [...].

And as to what I have heard, Madam, that your ambassador Monsieur Throckmorton (in whom I have always known a great zeal
for the service of God and for yours) has written that he was told by the Prince of Condé that he had no treaty with Your
Majesty, I never heard the Prince of Condé say such a thing; rather the said ambassador said several times that you had
no treaty with us, but with the subjects of Normandy (as he himself will be able to say and recall, being now back with
Your Majesty); and he added further that he had no charge or instruction to negotiate with us. Whereupon I always gave
him to understand that I was sure Your Majesty's intention was that, provided the Gospel were preached in this kingdom
and there were liberty of conscience, and your right were well kept and remained entire, you would be very glad to see
these troubles pacified by a good agreement: it appears sufficiently from your protestation. Beseeching you most humbly
to believe, Madam, that we esteem your virtue and greatness, and all your actions so praiseworthy and memorable, so
highly that we should never commit so great a fault as to forget the goodness you have shown us in the defence of this
cause of God, and for the liberty of the king and of this kingdom; as I have asked Monsieur the Vidame and the Sieurs de
Briquemault and de la Haye to make known to you, together with what it seems necessary that Your Majesty should do, if
you please, for the recovery of the liberty of the Prince of Condé. I beg you most humbly to believe them in what they
tell you on my behalf, as myself: and whereupon I shall pray this good God to keep Your Majesty, Madam, in most perfect
health and prosperity, and to bless your actions. From Orléans, this twenty-fourth of January. Your most humble and most
obedient servant.
