To the Secretary, 30 August 1585.

Your letter of the 21st reached me on the 26th. From it I see that {39 = the Master of Gray} has complained that my manner towards him has changed. My answer is that, whatever cause he gave me, I never changed my manner, but always treated him with as much familiarity and courtesy as I did [..]. The truth is that after he had spoken to {10 = the King} for the release of {19 = Arran} without the knowledge of {40 = the Justice Clerk}, [27] or myself, he stopped his usual visits to me for three or four days, so that the change of manner was on his side, not mine. I will now take that proceeding in the best sense, seeing he has corrected himself so well, and [..] again.

As for [27] and {40 = the Justice Clerk}, I have never seen cause to suspect them of being in the [11] course, nor do I think {39 = the Master of Gray} really believes it of them. Still, it cannot be denied, as you write, that [27] was much hers, is a great supporter of the Hamiltons, and is so far no earnest friend of the Lords; [?yet] in every matter of my negotiation I have had to handle with him I have found him fully as forward as either of the other two. Even so, I mean to follow your wise advice and deal warily with him and with everyone. From the little experience I have of the people of this country, I think it dangerous to trust any of them further than suits his own interests, as they call it here.

So in my simple judgement, if the Queen wants to win reliable friends in this country, she must both take account of their interests and so commit them to her course [..] that necessity [..] [..]. Men who are only bound will quickly free themselves for a greater gain. Feeding them with money is good, but serves little purpose unless you commit them so far that they cannot hope to jump ashore without [?drowning].

{19 = Arran} is still at Kinneil, where, under colour of fearing a sudden attack by his [enemies], he is said to be very well guarded. Keeping him from the court beyond the meeting of the commissioners will, I think, be very hard, unless they find him guilty of the [?recent] breach on the Borders. If he regains access to the King, I cannot see how {39 = the Master of Gray}, {40 = the Justice Clerk} and [27] can live safely at court; and if {19 = Arran} is there alone as the man who does everything, we can easily guess how things will go. For my part I am too inexperienced to find a remedy for this mischief, and so leave it to the Queen to consider whether she will listen to {39 = the Master of Gray}'s offer or not. I expect her decision on the proposal made by {39 = the Master of Gray} any day.

This morning I saw a letter written to the Prior of Blantyre by the brother of the Laird of [?Jamington], from which it appears that {20 = Lord Maxwell} is very eager to make his peace with the King through {19 = Arran}, with whom I suspect he already has a secret agreement; so I will do what I can to block his reconciliation with {10 = the King}.

The Earl of Bothwell has, through the Master's efforts, just come to court, to the great dismay of Arran's party. It is quite certain that Hay and Dury are in the country. The King seems very keen to have them caught and has given the task to {39 = the Master of Gray}, who has promised to do it. I can see no change in the King, except that he seems as eager as ever for {9 = English} friendship.

Note for the Earl of L[eicester]. This morning Sir William Stewart told the King that Cuthbert Armorer is now [..], and that his brother had been told that the Queen has decided to expel the distressed Lords from England for a few days, and that her real intention is to send them shortly into this country to shuffle the cards. Whether that is really her intention you know best. Meanwhile this fellow is playing his part very nicely. I would therefore wish some order to be made that no Englishman may come into this country without a special licence. I would wish some order to be made to stop his coming into this country.

[Endorsed:] Copy. To the Secretary, 30 August.
