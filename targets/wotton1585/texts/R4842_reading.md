To Mr Secretary, the 30th of August 1585.

Your Honour's of the 21st came to my handes the 26th of the same, by which I perceive that {39 = the Master of Gray} hath complayned of the alteration of my countenance towarde him. My answeare thereunto is that I never, what cause soever he gave me to the contrary, altered my countenance, but used him alwaies with as great familiaritie and curtesy as I did [..]. Mary, the trouth is that after he had spoken to {10 = the King} for the enlargement of {19 = Arran} without the knowlege of {40 = the Justice Clerk}, [27] or myself, he then for three or four dayes abstained from his wonted manner of visiting me, so that the alteration of countenance was on his parte and not on myne. This proceeding in that matter I wil now interprete in the best parte, seeing he hath so wel taken up himself, and [..] again.

For [27] and {40 = the Justice Clerk}, I never sawe cause to suspect them to be in the [11] course, neither doo I suppose that {39 = the Master of Gray} in his conscience is so perswaded of them. Yet can it not be denied, as your Honour wryteth, [but] [27] was greatly hers, and is a great favorer of the Hameltons, and as yet no ernest frend to the Lords; [?howbeit] in all matters concerning my negotiation wherein I have had to deale with him I have found him fully as forwarde as any of the other two. Neverthelesse I meane to folow your Honour's grave advice in dealing warely with him and all others. For by that litle experience I have of this contry men, I thinke it dangerous to trust any of all their [..] further than it shal stande with his owne particulars, as they terme it heer.

So that in my simple judgement, if her Majesty wil procure herself fast frendes in this contrey, she must both have a consideration to their particulers and so embark them in her course [..] that necessity [..] [..]. [Men that] are bounde wil quickly for greater gain free themselfes. To feed them with money is good, but to no great purpose unles you do so embarke them as [they] may not hope to leape ashore without [?drowning].

{19 = Arran} yet remayneth at Kinneil, where, under a colour [or] pretense of feare to be suddenly assaulted by his [enemies], he is, as I heare, very wel accompanied. To kepe him from the courte longer than the meeting of the commissioners, unles he bee found by them gilty of the [?late] breache on the Borders, will be, as I suppose, very harde. If he recover the King's presence, I cannot see how {39 = the Master of Gray}, {40 = the Justice Clerk} [and] [27] can live safely in the courte; and if {19 = Arran}, being there alone do fac totum, we may easely coniecture what successe matters are like to have. For my owne part, I am to yong to find out a remedy for this mischeef, and therfore leave it to the consideration of her Majesty whether she wil harken to the {39 = the Master of Gray's} offer or no. [I] expect daily her Majesty's resolution touching the overture made by {39 = the Master of Gray}.

This morning I saw a letter written to the Prior of Blantire from the Laird of [?Jamington's] brother, by which it seemeth that {20 = Lord Maxwell} [is] very desirous to make his peace with the King by the means of {19 = Arran}, with whom I suspect he is secretly alreadye contracted; and therfore I wil doo what I can to hinder his reconciliation with {10 = the King}.

Erle Bothwel is by the Master's means newly come to the courte, to the great grief of the Arranistes. It is very certain that Hay and Dury are in this contrey. The King seemeth very desirous of their apprehension, and hath laied that charge on {39 = the Master of Gray}, who hath promised to performe it. I cannot perceive any change in the King, but that he seemeth stil as desirous of the {9 = English} frendshippe as ever he was.

Ticket to my Lord of L[eicester]. This morning Sir William Steward told his Majesty that Cutberd Armorer is now [..], [and] his brother was advertised that her Majesty is resolved to banishe the distressed Lords out of England for a few dayes, [and] that her meaning is to send them shortly into this contrey to shuffle the cardes. Whether there be any suche meaning or no, your Honour best knoweth. But in the meane season this companion plaieth his parte very kindly. I wishe therfore some order might be taken that no Englishman without espetial licence passe into this contrey. I wishe some order might be taken to stay his repaire into this contrey.

[Endorsed:] Copie. To Mr Secretarye, 30 Auguste.
