# R785, ff. 213-215: St Germain, 1 September [1645], in modern English

The Queen is perfectly recovered.

St Germain, [1] September.

I have very little to add to my last, except to tell you that the business of the ships is going forward excellently:
half the money has already been given, and the [?remainder] will be found. A letter from the Duke of Orléans, written
just as we could have wished, is also in the Queen's hands, to be returned tomorrow; so on our side everything that was
asked has been done. I hope they will not fail in [?what] [was] promised on [522]'s [part].

Ned Progers has arrived, by [541] I have received [letters] of yours; I have also talked with him at length. I do not
wholly excuse you, though you seem to have some reason for your discharge in the business of [228]: you ought not to be
open to provocation, so necessary are both the effect and the example of your agreeing.

I hear [148] is angry with me over a letter I wrote to you [541] he was concerned. I shall wonder both how he could be
angry if he saw it and how he came to see it; but I hear this from others, and from him [488] nothing but the
expressions of the kindness that in truth he owes me. So I believe this is rather the doing of those who want to give
him occasion to be displeased than the truth of the matter.

The Duke of Bouillon, being at Rome, has made a proposal to Sir Kenelm Digby to raise and ship into England for the King
his [500] 1,500 horse and [500] dragoons, at his own expense, without expecting repayment until the end of the war; and
he has asked that a gentleman be sent from the King to meet him at Cologne, with powers to treat and deal with him. He
has asked for many things to be done for him here by the Queen, which are too long and not important enough for you to
need to know. The Queen has decided to send Dudley Wyatt back to give his opinion on this business. He intends to give
your Lordship a fuller account by the next post. I remain for [?ever] your Lordship's most obedient servant, A. Cowley.

[Second letter, f. 215, beginning in mid-sentence:] ... him, to deal with him. I am of the opinion that this money,
laid out on well-armed foot, and foot raised [541] they will turn out to be men who had served before, will not only be
of greater use in England but can be shipped over with fewer difficulties; but we should take advice on this. I put it
to you not to fail to send me your opinion as soon as [535], whether horse or foot will be of more use in England now. I
see this as a very hopeful thing. Little by little I have embarked on a longer letter than I intended, and I have still
not finished.

From the Queen I must tell you that she is not yet of the opinion that the marriage treaty with [the Prince of Orange?]
should be made [?public]. Another thing has today become more expected than before: a speedy [507] with [237], a thing
that ought to wake us up in England. It bears on us in several ways. It is true that [543] we [348] falling into
the rebels' hands, [nothing] is so likely as that to deliver us, and in that respect, in the present emergency, the [507]
is to be wished for. But as it is a remedy for this great evil, it might become as great a one, by freeing the hands
that our present disorders might enable to master us. But more of this by the next occasion.

A patent is to be prepared for a baronetcy for Master Wright; you have the particulars from W. Crofts, who will lose
[500] if it is delayed any longer. It must soon, one way or another, be done without returning the money for it, for
that is not to be had: use your ingenuity on it, and no excuses, for I will accept none in this matter. Please dispatch
it and return it by the [344] [334]. I thank you heartily for your care of Campsfield and ask you to continue it; he
deserves it of you by his due acknowledgments. If you sent the letter from [212] to you to clear your [488] of that
foolish scandal, I cannot help taking it unkindly. I am entirely yours.
