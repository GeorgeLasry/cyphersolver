# Jean du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to the Grand Maître (Montmorency), London, last day of June 1529: English translation

Monseigneur, you will see from the King's letters part of what I could tell you of late, except to assure you that if the
Duke of Suffolk had a face of marble, the King had one of alabaster, seeing that you meant to press on; but that was not
without words. Nevertheless I forgive him, in my opinion, for he is somewhat timorous, and not only fears but is dying of
fear that, if the Emperor comes into Italy, the Pope will revoke his commission; whoever said I had done the work, when I
am with him, would be very much inclined to lie. I shall leave this talk; it would serve for nothing at present. I can
indeed tell you that when he does nothing of what you would wish, it will not be the fault of the Cardinal, who is wiser
than he, nor of any servant he has. Consider a little how he leads his people for you: it was by the contrivance of […]
and […] that the Duke of Suffolk had gone to you, thinking a [*] at Amiens when I arrived here […]. The Cardinal wanted to
break off their journey and go at once to Cambrai; he saw that this King took it ill, he gave way, but he made their
commission worthy of Master Bryan, then by good means had them recalled so as not to be at this Assembly; and do not think
that he would be the sorriest man in the world if everything were concluded before the arrival of those who are going
there, whatever face he may put on it, provided nothing is done to the prejudice of this King in returning to the
marriage.

I had written to you that there would be great delay, but I see that since the coming of Stephen and Bryan, bringing, as I
certainly think, [evidence] of the falsity of the brief, Campeggio is half won over to cut it short, for several reasons
too long to explain, unless the Pope should hurry to revoke the commission; and I do not think it will not be done within
a month, unless something else intervenes, and perhaps sooner; and if anything hinders Cardinal Campeggio in it, it will
in my opinion be the fear he will have — without cause, however — that the King and the Emperor may come to terms and
become friends, leaving this one a little behind, and consequently him too. I do good service in this, but without going
too far into it: I have begged on the King's behalf that they attend to it and hasten it, with many good words, saying I
had a letter, and this has already served them. On this subject, the Lord Cardinal most humbly and most affectionately
begs the King to write a word in his own hand to the said Campeggio, asking him to expedite this affair, and assuring him
that wherever he does something for its good he will hold it as done to himself and will remember it in time and place,
with other such or similar words. To tell you the truth, Monseigneur, I see for the present nothing whatever that would
serve their matter better; and its coming to an end can only be marvellously convenient for you, for besides the
friendship the King bears the King his good brother, it will be a means in future of bringing this King to his knees
before you. Perhaps the King need not write so openly: the letter remains, and there would be no need for a date, but to
refer the rest to what I shall lately have told him about it, in accordance with what I had discharged by my letters.

Your ambassadors will leave on Friday and travel by long stages: I hope it will be the return from matins. I did not
want to send you an express courier, so that, when the packet reaches you, the ten days will have expired. When you send
Doctor Benet the Lord Legate's letters, have an excuse made for the delay, that my courier broke a foot on the way and
gave the packet to another; they will believe it, for I have provided for that; otherwise they would see that I had put
in this delay on purpose. Monseigneur, I shall commend myself most humbly to your good grace, and pray our Lord to give
you a good and long life. From London, the last of June. At The Hague I am not writing to Madame, hoping that the King's
letters and yours will serve her; I would only be repeating myself to her.
