# Jean du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to the Grand Maître (Montmorency), London, 22 June 1529: English translation

Letter of Monsieur du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to Monsieur the Grand Maître.

Monseigneur, what has given them the hottest alarm is that the Duke of Suffolk has written to the King his master — I
have seen his very letter — that the King had told him that, when the Ladies came to this parley, everything would be
done and concluded in two days; on that they judge that matters are already agreed, that nothing is needed but a little
ceremony, saying that in this you have dealt too falsely with them, always showing them, saying and writing that you saw
no foundation in the matter. I assure you, Monseigneur, that without pretence the Lord Legate is in terrible distress,
for until this very hour he had always assured his master and everyone, in private and in public, that it would come to
nothing, and that you would do nothing whatever without them. He now strongly suspects the contrary; and I leave you to
imagine whether he speaks to me, and whether he says that I deceived him and was the cause of his deceiving his master,
and consequently that I put him on a very bad road. I help myself at this and defend myself tooth and nail; yet in the end
he will come to seek me out again, and already, as you see, he has wanted me to go and see the King, and to serve him the
best talk in the world, without among other things putting him entirely out of hope that Monsieur Feuguillaume's coming
was not to put some delay into this Assembly; which I well feel I must do, partly to a good end and to keep these things
on a good course, partly so that he may extricate himself, so that, my having my share of the burden towards his said
master, his own may be so much the less. It will be very necessary, Monseigneur, that whoever comes to bring the news of
peace be well primed with fair words.

Also, Monseigneur, I have heard from a good source that Madame Marguerite is pressing you hard to come to a good
conclusion (you know whether it is true), alleging the trouble she had in getting her commission from the Emperor,
although all the ambassadors of his relatives and allies, the greatest lords of Spain, fell on their knees to him for it,
[saying] that he saw Monseigneur d'Orléans despairing of his life, his affairs in the worst state in the world in Italy,
everyone discontented in Spain and minded to mutiny, Germany so troubled, and the Turk's preparations as great as could
be; and that now, some of those causes having ceased and the others being able to cease in time, she feared greatly that
he would revoke the said commission, and that she already had some good diversion from it. I have heard nothing of it;
but it seems to me a good thing to say, and even to the Duke of Suffolk. It is true, Monseigneur, that having the children
is the main thing; but whoever can satisfy everything does well.

I believe Feuguillaume brought Monsieur de Suffolk orders to return; I understand they have letters from the King of the
16th, in which he put quite crudely that he was leaving Paris to go towards Cambrai, as if he were to be there himself,
with which I assure you the Cardinal of York would not be pleased. Monseigneur, the Legate would much like that, when the
King writes to me of his good affection and will to help the King his brother in his affair, he should expressly mention
that he [?comes] to employ himself in it all the more because he is and has continually been asked and pressed to do so by
my said Lord Legate; and it seems to me, Monseigneur, that it would be well to do so. You may perhaps take it ill that I
send you express couriers so often; but, Monseigneur, I would have to not write at all, for in the first place the packets
would not be carried, and besides, they would stay lying on the table, as I am sure they would; at every post there are
men of the Duke of Suffolk who would not miss me in so fair a field. The Cardinal of York has warned me of it closely
enough, and I find that a packet has been lost since I have been here, about which I am troubled.

You will see from the King's letters in what state they are in their divorce; all the help they ask of you with the Pope
is that he should not revoke his commission. As for your affairs, they are going well enough here; they have almost got
over half their grief, and hold the peace as made. Seeing they had gone so far, I preached with the Cardinal of York a
sermon that served the matter; I reckon that, since they have had a good [?July] preparation, it will be all the easier to
make them swallow the medicine one of these days. What greatly vexed the Lord Legate was a report he had had, from Rome as
I believe, that things were long since arranged and agreed through the Duke of Savoy, which I have shown him well is not
true. The King was very pleased with his artichokes, coming from where they come from, and makes much of them among his
friends; it was a small gesture well timed. My said Lord Legate asked me whether the Chancellor would be at this meeting; I
answered that I thought not, unless there were too great a likelihood of matters being concluded. He said he saw clearly
that Madame Marguerite, in wanting the said meeting to be from the 18th, is pressing on for two reasons: one, to prepare
the Emperor's passage; the other, to keep him [the Chancellor] from being there, thinking well that his presence would
not improve the said Emperor's affairs, knowing him to be quite clear-sighted. Therefore he advises that Madame should not
fail to bring my said Lord Chancellor, for if anything is done, he will be able better than another to see that you do not
lose in the bargain. Which will be the end of this letter, after I have commended myself humbly to your good grace and
asked our Lord to give you, Monseigneur, a good and long life. From London, the 22nd of June. Thus signed, your humble
servant J. du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne.

There can be no danger in having Campeggio's packet delivered to Salviati; I beg you, Monseigneur, to be pleased to order
it. On the back of the letter is written: To Monseigneur, Monseigneur the Grand Maître and Marshal of France.
