# Jean du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to the Grand Maître (Montmorency), London, 17 October 1529: English translation

Letter of Monsieur du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to Monsieur the Grand Maître.

Monseigneur, since the letters I thought to send at once, this despatch has been delayed until now, because the letters
I am sending you had to be made and remade a great many times, and for that reason there was much going and coming to
Windsor, both of the Dukes themselves and of others of this Council; and only now have they sent them to me in the form you
will see from the copy of them. They beg me as hard as they can to see that no one takes it ill if in these despatches,
and especially in what touches the main point of the despatch, I am not altogether satisfied as I would wish, nor they
themselves, excusing themselves that their way of negotiating with their master is not yet well settled; but for the
future they are to do wonders, and they give such great assurances of it, and so well sworn, that I cannot keep from
believing them. I have not renewed my letters to the King, for I do not see that there is matter for it.

For the rest, I went to see the Cardinal in his troubles, where I found the greatest example of fortune one could ever
see. He set out his case to me in the worst rhetoric I ever saw, for heart and speech failed him entirely; he wept much
and begged that the King and Madame would be pleased to have pity on him, if they had found that he had kept his promise
to be a good servant to them as far as his honour and power could extend; but in the end he left me without being able to
say anything better than his face, which has indeed fallen by half its just value. And I promise you, Monseigneur, that his
fortune is such that his enemies, although they are English, could not keep from pitying him; nevertheless they will not
fail to pursue him to the end, and he sees no means of his safety, nor do I, unless it please the King and Madame to help
him. Of legation, of the seal, of authority, of credit he asks nothing; he is ready to give up everything down to his
shirt, and to be left to live in a hermitage, provided this King does not hold him in his displeasure. I comforted him as
best I could, but I could not do much. Since then, through someone he trusts, he has sent me word of what he would like to
be done for him for the greater part, and, seeing that it did not touch the good of the King's affairs, that the most
reasonable thing he asks should be granted him: namely, that the King should write to this King that there is a great
rumour over there that he has put him away from about him and far from his good grace, so that it is said he is to be
destroyed, which he does not think is altogether as it is said; nevertheless, for the good brotherhood they have together,
and the great communication of all their greatest affairs, he has wished to beg him to have regard to it, so that no bad
fancy should suddenly arise towards those who have seen that, with such great solemnity and authority, he served as
instrument in this perpetual friendship so renowned throughout Christendom; and that if by chance he had become
discontented with him, he would be pleased to moderate his feeling a little, as he is quite sure those who are about his
person and in the handling of his greatest affairs will advise him. That, Monseigneur, is the most reasonable of all his
requests, in which I do not wish to intrude by giving my opinion; but I will say that there is no one here who ought to
take such a letter ill, and especially when they consider, as indeed they do, that they are forced to take and hold your
side more than ever. And moreover I will assure you that the greatest hold they could have on him at the beginning, and
what most served them to set him at odds with this King, was that on my coming here he declared too openly that he wished
to go to Cambrai; for the others persuaded the master that this was only to avoid being present at the dispatch of the
marriage. And besides that, I promise you that without him the others were putting this King on a terrible course of
breaking off the peace negotiations, of which I wrote you a word at that time; but I left ten times more of it in my pen,
seeing that everything had been patched up; I shall tell you of it when I am there, and I am sure you will find it very
strange.

It seems to me, Monsieur, that all this, and several other things that you well understand yourself, deserve some regard.
You will, if you please, inform the King and Madame of all this, so that they may consider what they will be pleased to do
about it; if they think they will not worsen their affairs by it, I believe they will gladly — besides its being a work of
charity — wish it to be known that they have drawn back from the gates of hell one of their devoted servants, held as such
by everyone. But above all, Monseigneur, he wishes that this King should not know that they have been asked to do it, and
that he had them asked in any way in the world; that would finish driving him mad. For to tell you the truth, and setting
aside all affection, I assure you that the greatest hold his enemies have had on him, besides that of the marriage, has
been to persuade this King that he always had, in time of peace and war, a secret understanding with Madame, from whom,
during the said war, he had received great presents, which were the reason that, when Suffolk was at Montdidier, he did not
help him with money as he should have, whence it came about that he did not take Paris; but they speak of this in
whispers, so that I may not be informed of it. As to the said presents, he hopes that Madame will not harm him where it is
spoken of; for all other things he commends himself to her good grace.

The fancy of these lords is that, once he is dead or ruined, they will at once overthrow the state of the Church here and
take all their goods, which I would hardly need to put in cipher, for they shout it at a full table. I believe they will
work fine miracles. Yet your great Prophet with the bronzed face told me that this King will not live much longer than
[…], to whom, as you know, so far as I see from his writings, he gave no term beyond the muster of May. I do not want to
forget to tell you that if the King and Madame wish to do something for the Legate, they would have to hurry; even so the
letters will never be here before he has lost the Seal; however he no longer thinks of that; they will serve for the
rest. Also, when my successor comes here, as everyone expects him to within a few days, they should give him charge to
speak of it. The worst of his trouble is that Mademoiselle Boleyn has made her lover promise that he will never hear him
speak; for she well thinks that he could not keep from pitying him.

Monseigneur, whatever is good in all this discourse you will know how to take as such; if there is anything that seems
to come from too much affection, I beg you to help me excuse it and that it be taken in good part; for even where the
matter might be bad, I can still assure you that the intention is not; and thereupon it is high time, for you and for
me, that I make an end of this letter, commending myself humbly to your good grace and praying our Lord to give you a good
and long life. From London, the 17th of October. Signed, your humble servant J. du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne. And on the
back of the letter is written: To Monseigneur, Monseigneur the Grand Maître and Marshal of France.
