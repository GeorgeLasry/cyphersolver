# R9613: Alonso Sánchez to Charles V, Venice, 18 June 1522 — English

Translation of `R9613_reading.md` (the contemporary decipherment, ff. 206r-207v). Same holes.

I received Your Majesty's letter of the last of last month and went to the lords of this Republic. I told them that
their ambassador had discussed the peace in this way and handed in a paper, to which a reply was given on Your
Majesty's behalf in another paper, of all of which they would certainly have had a copy from their ambassador; that I
informed them that Your Majesty was well disposed, provided they came to honest and reasonable conditions, because, as
Your Majesty is emperor and head of the Christian commonwealth, so you desired more than anyone to establish and set
peace in it, especially in Italy, and to unite its forces against the infidels and Turks; that I was well instructed in
the whole negotiation and in Your Majesty's will in it; if they wished to discuss it with me, all of them or by
deputing some persons for it, they should do so, or see what seemed good to them; or if, having consulted among
themselves, they wished to answer without discussing it otherwise with me, only to let me know what they would decide.
They answered that their ambassador had been told to draw up a paper on how he thought the peace should be settled, and
that this showed that what he said was approved, since he was ordered to draw up a paper; that it seems that on Your
Majesty's side one is leaving that first negotiation of peace and league, which they wanted as the stronger bond, and
entering on making a new truce; and that, on the other hand, it is written that in the peace you cannot promise for the
most serene lord Infante, while in the truce it says that His Highness would also enter. They also told me that the
Cardinal of England had told their ambassadors he wished to see the said papers and give his opinion. I replied that if
they considered well all that had been answered on Your Majesty's behalf they would recognise Your Majesty's good mind
and will, and that they should consider well the last article, in which Your Majesty asks the King of England to be
conciliator; that they should weigh well that Your Majesty was asking what they themselves ought to ask, and they would
see whether there was a wish to keep for them what was agreed. I said this because I know that one of their grounds is
that what was promised them would not be kept.

On the lord Infante's matter I said: how was Your Majesty to promise what was not in your hand nor yours? And I also
told them that I [?…] Your Majesty said it, because they already knew what was answered to their ambassador when he
proposed that each side return to the other as before the war: that Your Majesty had not accepted those conditions, nor
[?was there need] to accept them. They replied that Your Majesty had not rejected them altogether. To what they said
about Your Majesty leaving the first negotiation, I told them not to be deceived: you were not leaving it, rather this
was entering it on a sound footing, stating and removing the causes that could hinder it; that if they came to the
conditions contained in Your Majesty's paper they would see that it was no departure at all from the first negotiation
of the peace; and that if they asked for a league for defence, Your Majesty asked for more, a league for offence, with
the limitation laid down; but in case, these being matters of great weight and quality, they thought more time was
needed to settle them, Your Majesty said that a truce could be made meanwhile; that this of the truce showed them Your
Majesty's good will, since you asked for it only in case they thought the said peace could not be settled so; that,
coming to the conditions contained in the paper, Your Majesty spoke not of a truce but of the peace. And because I saw
that they were much stung that Your Majesty offered that the lord Infante would promise the truce and says he cannot
promise the peace, since it seemed to them, though they did not say so to me, that this left in the peace a great back
door open by which they could be harmed at any time, I told them they could come to such and so honest conditions that
Your Majesty would see to it that the lord Infante entered the peace. They left it that they would consult among
themselves and answer me.

I have been assured that in the Pregadi, which they held a few days ago, they discussed at length what they should do
about the surrender of Cremona, should it come, and that one of them said God had always helped this Republic, that
some days would thus pass before its surrender, and they would see what happened; that they were not now at liberty, so
they could not well speak of what had to be done until they were, showing that surrendering it [?…] was outside the
obligation to France; and many of them hold this opinion. I hold it needful that they should not declare this except
when they can do no other, and when they have their affairs settled with Your Majesty.

From France they say they have letters of three [days], with the usual boasting. Their ambassador was told by the King
of France that France has at other times resisted the Empire, Spain and England, and that joined with this Signoria it
would not only resist but attack them. Monsieur de Lescun at Cremona was sent a copy of a letter he has from Turin of
the 7th of this month, that six hundred lances and twelve thousand Gascon adventurers had arrived there, and that they
had [?their pay]; and Andrea Gritti is again writing to them, encouraging them towards France.

As far as I can tell, the Turk is not arming at sea this year, and these men say so, and they have released the ships
they had detained. Those of Rhodes defend themselves valiantly and with loss to the Turks. But these men still go on
with their arming at sea, so that one can understand they are not arming because of the Turk; I have also been told
they arm for their reputation, and that their consul at Constantinople has written to them that they must not leave off
arming, for reputation's sake; but I do not believe they arm for that, for they are not people to spend their money on
air. I also understand they mean from now on to sell all their offices for money, and have resolved on it, since by
doing so they will raise a great deal of money; and that is usually the last effort they make when they are in need. And
these signs do not please me as to their wishing to come to an agreement with Your Majesty; rather I understand that
some, not however people of the government, say that it could be they would not come to terms with Your Majesty, and
others say no agreement can be made with Your Majesty.

The Viceroy of Naples wrote to me from Mantua what was happening; the rector of Verona and Prospero [Colonna] wrote to
him that it was well to come to a resolution with these men, and I advised Your Majesty of it; I shall not trouble to
repeat it here. He also wrote asking me to press these men to answer him on what their rector said, and I told them so
some days ago, and they replied that when they answered Your Majesty they would answer the said Viceroy, since it was
all one thing; especially as the said [Viceroy] would stay five days in Mantua, and he left at once. In short, this
coming of the French keeps them in suspense, and while they have that hope they cannot leave them except when they can
do no other. I have written to the lord Infante what Your Majesty commands me by yours of the 30th, and I will
continually write to and serve His Highness with the same diligence that Your Majesty commanded. From Venice, 18 June.
