# R43 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, 23 December 1588: the killing of the Duke of Guise)

Translation of `R43_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 308r] […] this morning […] the Duke of Guise was killed in the King's chamber, by order of His Majesty, with dagger
blows, and his death came about in this way. His Majesty had been warned from several quarters of the evil intention
that the said Guise had against his person; and Signor Alfonso Corso having arrived here, as Your Most Illustrious
Lordship will have heard by my last letters, sent by the Duke of Mayenne for the reasons written, with warnings that His
Majesty should keep good guard against Monsieur de Guise his brother, who was plotting against his person, and that he
himself would have come to Blois had he thought he could arrive in time to turn him from his designs; and the said Duke
of Guise having told His Majesty yesterday [likewise] that, were the Estates General not sitting, he would with his own
hand have killed many of those who stood around him and always did him bad offices, adding many other threatening words
— for these reasons the King at last, without communicating this thought to any living person, resolved in his mind to
take his life. Yesterday evening he gave out that he would go hunting this morning, and he let some men know (who are
not named so far) that they should be in the cabinet at five in the morning, as they did; and when they had come to His
Majesty he opened his mind to them, and having found them most ready to carry it out, he had them [f. 308v] stay in the
chamber and sent to call the Duke of Guise, who was in the Council of State. He came, and as soon as he entered the
chamber he was seized by two men from the side and thrown down by others with many dagger blows, after making a great
defence in vain with his hands and his teeth, without ever being able to put his hand to his sword. While the deed was
being done His Majesty stayed withdrawn in the cabinet with Signor Alfonso Corso alone, whom he keeps with him as his
guard. Guise dead, and the Cardinal of Guise (who was also in the Council), the Duke of Nemours, the Duke of Elbeuf, the
Prince of Joinville, the Archbishop of Lyon, the prévôt des marchands of Paris and the president de Neuilly made
prisoners, and a guard set on the Cardinal of Bourbon, the King went to find the Queen Mother and told her the reasons
why he had been forced to forestall the Duke's designs, to free himself from danger and the kingdom from tyranny. The
Queen Mother remained astonished and could not answer a word. Until all this had been done the guards stood under arms,
and all the gates and principal […] of the town were taken, in which […] and no disturbance has followed.

As soon as I was told by my household, who from the windows had seen the castle gates being shut and the guards
taking up arms, I tried to learn [f. 309r] the reason, and was told that the King wanted to have a man arrested who had
put his hand to a dagger in His Majesty's chamber; nor could the truth be learned until the Cardinal de Gondi, in His
Majesty's name, came to give me the news of what had happened and the reasons that had forced him to that execution,
both what has been said above, learned through the Duke of Mayenne, and other things written by that d'Aumale, his
first cousin. I did not know what to answer, being left half dead by this news; and yet I told him that I wept for the
misfortune of that prince and prayed God that it might not be to the greater harm and ruin of this kingdom. I tried to
have an audience, but it has not been possible, His Majesty excusing himself with the many business he had. I will not
cease to go on asking for it, to perform at least the office I owe for those who remain alive; and from what the
Cardinal de Gondi tells me, it can be held for certain that they will not die. His Majesty then went to mass and dined
in public without showing any change [in his face?], and the Cardinal de Vendôme gave him the napkin for his hands. A
proclamation has been issued that all the masters of houses must today give in the names of all those lodging with
them; and His Majesty has since let the Dukes of Nemours and Elbeuf know [f. 309v] that they should be of good cheer,
for they will not be harmed. It has [likewise] been […] ordered, on pain of death, that no one may leave Blois without
a written licence. For all that, many servants and friends of the Duke of [Guise] have cleared out. I am assured that His
Excellency was warned yesterday from several quarters to beware of the King, but […] that he could not persuade himself
that His Majesty would take such a resolution — or else his hour had come. […] I have said […]. […] I am assured that the
Cardinal of Guise too is dead, which however I cannot know for certain, though I much fear it, knowing the King's great
displeasure with His Most Illustrious Lordship for the words he spoke freely against his person, and because he knew
that it was he who pushed the Estates against him. I am also told that His Majesty has sent to the Duke of Nevers,
begging him urgently to attend more than ever to the war against Navarre, being quite resolved to continue with all his
power to root out heresy from his kingdom, so that the world should not believe that he has made it rather to please
the Duke of Guise than from his own inclination [f. 307r] [up to now]. His Majesty has also sent Signor Alfonso Corso to
Lyon with letters patent as his lieutenant general of the forces that are with the Duke of Mayenne, and writes to His
Excellency that, [moved] chiefly by the warning that he had sent him through the said Signor Alfonso Corso, in agreement
with many others of the same tenor he had had from several quarters, he had resolved to carry out that execution against
the Duke of Guise, which he would understand, and which he believed […] blame; […] he had been driven to it not by
hatred of his house but by pure necessity of saving his life and quieting his kingdom; and therefore on every occasion
he would show him, in his own person, his wish to help and gratify him; but for the present he urged him to withdraw to
his government and hand over the forces intended for the Dauphiné enterprise to the said Signor Alfonso, because when
[…] he sees him take this deed in the way he should, he will not fail to honour his person with greater favours. I find
myself so troubled and confused, and I shall await with most ardent desire an answer […] with the order that it will
please [f. 307v] Our Lord to give me; and I kiss Your Most Illustrious Lordship's hands again […].
