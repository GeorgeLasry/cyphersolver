# R36 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, 10 November 1588, duplicate: Poigny's mission fails)

Translation of `R36_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 249r] […] with my last letters I wrote to Your Most Illustrious Lordship that I intended to make some further
representation, beyond what I had already made to Their Majesties, about the affairs of Saluzzo; but that very evening
a high fever came upon me, which, although it turned out to be an ephemeral one, has nevertheless taken from me the
means of leaving the house until this hour. Meanwhile the ambassador of the Duke of Savoy has arrived […], with news of
what Monsieur de Poigny had negotiated with His Highness and of a certain accord made about the affairs of [Rouen?];
whereupon the said ambassador, having asked audience of Their Majesties and not having been able to obtain it from the
King because of his other occupations, went today to the Queen Mother, to whom he gave an account of all that passed
between his Duke and the said Monsieur de Poigny: [the] conclusion was that His Highness could not refrain from taking
out of the hands of La Valette's dependants what remained of the marquisate of Saluzzo, not wishing to have as
neighbours men who had already declared themselves his enemies, and that for many reasons of his own he could not
restore those places to Poigny either, saying that since Poigny has two heretic brothers he could not trust him; with
which the Queen [f. 249v] was very ill satisfied. The said ambassador, after leaving the Queen Mother, came to me and
brought me a letter of His Highness, of which I send a copy herewith to Your Most Illustrious Lordship, and in
conformity with what he reported to the Queen he also gave me an account of the same. So Your Lordship can well imagine
into what thoughts this […] of His Highness has put me, since he tells me that all he has done is by order of Our Lord;
and you have very well what you wrote to me on this subject, having sent me a copy of the letter His Highness wrote to
you on the taking of Carmagnola, from which the contrary is clearly inferred; and so I have always vigorously maintained that
Our Lord had no part in it, seeing plainly that nothing could be more prejudicial in this kingdom to that Holy See than
to let it be impressed on the people […] that the Duke of Savoy had made that enterprise by Our Lord's order, as these
men were spreading abroad, since that made Our Lord most odious not only to His Majesty but to the whole […], all three
Estates (as I wrote to Your Lordship in other letters) having stirred up the King and offered him very great aid to [f.
250r] avenge himself on the said Duke, calling him ingrate, perjurer and abettor of heretics. So it seems to me more than
necessary to go on undeceiving everyone and to assure His Majesty that Our Lord, as common father, will always procure
peace and union among Catholic princes, and will never consent that one take another's state, but rather will procure
with all his authority that each be content with his own; […] terms […] I have governed myself so far, and will continue
in future, unless Your Most Illustrious Lordship commands me otherwise; to whom I will not fail to say that although I
shall not fail to do every office so that it does not come to arms, I fear I can do little good, seeing minds enraged
[…] on all sides, and the King very resolute, having told me he would sooner lose all he has in the world and his very
life than endure this injury. I also know that His Majesty is being urged by some […], and that many […] think the
salvation of the kingdom would be to make war on foreigners; the others […] want nothing else, since that would be their
complete salvation. So, if the Lord God does not please to put out this [f. 250v] fire, I fear that very grave harm may
follow for Christendom, with irreparable detriment to the Catholic religion. I wanted to tell Your Lordship all this,
begging you to give me light on the way in which it will please Our Lord that I govern myself; adding that the said
ambassador of Savoy told me that, although he believes his Duke will be content that the government of the marquisate be
given to Monsieur de Nemours, he will nevertheless [want] other conditions, about the lieutenant and other such things,
which they will never accept. […]
