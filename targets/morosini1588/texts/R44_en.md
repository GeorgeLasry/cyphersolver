# R44 — English (Morosini to Montalto, [Blois], 24 December 1588, duplicate: the death of the Cardinal of Guise)

Translation of `R44_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 313r] When [I] wrote to Your Most Illustrious Lordship yesterday evening that, besides the death of the Duke of
Guise, […] it was said that his brother the Cardinal was dead too, it was not true then; but only this morning has he
been deprived of life: some say by the sword like his brother, others strangled with a rope, for the truth is not yet
known. I am told that the King resolved to have him die having discovered that he was planning to flee, and that for
this purpose some rising of people was to be seen. By this death I have been much more confounded than by that of the
Duke of Guise, he being an ecclesiastical person and a cardinal, and I do not know how to conduct myself, fearing to err
in every way. But after maturely considering all that may happen, I have resolved — since no prejudice follows from it
to His Holiness or to that Holy See, and he can always use his authority — to choose rather to proceed with dexterity,
showing the King his error and keeping him in due obedience to the Holy Apostolic See, than to be myself the author of
some scandalous decision, of which, from what I see, there is much to fear if one had to deal in harsher terms; and in a
matter of such importance and consequence I have thought it more fitting to leave the judgement to the supreme wisdom
[f. 313v] of Our Lord than to wish to decide it myself. I therefore beg you with all humility to let me know as soon as
possible Our Lord's settled will. Tomorrow I shall go to the King, His Majesty having today sent me a note written in his
own hand, the translation of which will come with this letter.

The Cardinal de Bourbon is still guarded by the King's guards, although in bed with fever and a flux of blood, which,
added to his discontent and the fear he is in, could easily cost him his life; as […], they say he will be sent to prison
together with the Prince of Joinville in the castle of Amboise, as will the Archbishop of Lyon, to some other safe place.
Today they examined Monsieur de Guise's secretary, having first promised him his life if he told the truth about what he
would be asked; and among many other things he said that a few days before, Our Lord had written to the said Duke a
letter of three sides of paper, all in his own hand, in favour of the Duke of Savoy over the affairs of the marquisate of
Saluzzo. And I am told that His Majesty related this to the Queen Mother, telling her that these were not the offices of
a common father, and that if Our Lord chose to proceed with him in this manner, he too for his part would do what suited
him, not wishing [f. 314r] […]: "Monsignor Legate, now I am King, and I have taken such a resolution not to suffer any
more insults or ill treatment, that I shall carry on my generous resolution, to the harm of whoever it may be, with the
example of our Holy Father, Our Lord" — for I well remember the form of words he constantly uses, that one must make
oneself obeyed and punish those who offend us. Since I have reached the end, [I] shall see tomorrow whether […] God
[…].

[f. 315r, a separate leaf] […] fail to tell Your Most Illustrious Lordship that His Majesty, this event having turned out
so entirely to his satisfaction — since without the least movement or disturbance of the people he has got the upper
hand over these enemies of his, who were reckoned invincible by all the world — has gained such confidence from it that,
were he pressed, he could very easily show resentment […] heretics […].
