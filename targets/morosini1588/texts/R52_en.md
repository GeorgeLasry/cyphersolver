## Translation

[f. 473r] […] which; the castellan of Angers having noticed that those of the town, through the work of Monsieur de
Brissac, were about to join the other confederate towns and were waiting only for the arrival of Monsieur de
Bois-Dauphin to carry out their design (he was approaching the town with 100 horse and 1,200 arquebusiers), he at once
wrote to Marshal d'Aumont that, to forestall and break this plot, he should with all possible speed come that way with
his troops, the castellan meanwhile pretending not to be wholly averse to the offer they had then made him of […]
thousand écus and of the confirmation for life of that charge, […] that he should hold the castle at their devotion —
the castle that is reputed the strongest in France. Whereupon the marshal, who was […] of [Le Mans], on the news of
such an important event set off at once without losing time, reached the castle, and through it entered the town at
the very hour when the townsmen were beginning to entrench and rampart the houses nearest the fortress, and when
Monsieur de Bois-Dauphin was already very close with his troops; at the marshal's sudden entry the townsmen were left
so dismayed and overcome with fear that without further movement they showed themselves ready to obey and serve the
King. The King was told of this event by a gentleman sent express, who arrived here on Easter day; His Majesty showed
infinite joy at it and greatly praised the loyalty and generosity of mind of the castellan, who had stood firm against
the blows of […] thousand écus, and at once wrote him a most affectionate letter in his own hand. This town is most
important not only as a curb on all Brittany but also for the revenues drawn from it, which come to more than 200,000
écus a year; and so His Majesty has at once sent Monsieur d'O there to get money, and according to some will also have
their arms taken from them, […] they cannot raise their horns again against [f. 473v] him. He has also sent the Count
of Soissons, who was here for the feast, into Brittany, [joined] with the Duke of Montpensier, who as they say has more
than 400 horse together, nearly all of them noblemen, [who] can fight the Duke of Mercœur; the latter, despite the
offices made with him by the Queen his sister, has declared for the League, at which the Queen feels infinite grief for
love of her husband. While His Majesty was going to mass on Tuesday, he was presented with the cornet of a company of
Monsieur de Mayenne's men, which had been routed in a village near Chartres by a band of 60 of His Majesty's horse, who
fell on the others at night while they were sound asleep. Those of Abbeville too have cut to pieces two companies of
League arquebusiers for the outrages they were committing in their territory. It is also said, though so far without
confirmation, that those of Amiens have driven out Balagny, as one who wanted to arrogate too much authority to
himself, and set at liberty the mother, the wife and the brother of the Duke of Longueville, into whose hands they
wanted to put back the whole government of that province. His Majesty has already written twice to the Duke of Nevers
to go into Champagne and make war in that province, to divert and divide the League's forces all the more; but His
Excellency has not yet accepted the charge, showing that he wants more troops than Monsieur de Dinteville now has,
which as they say are about three thousand foot and 150 horse. On Thursday a courier reached the King with news that
Signor Alfonso Corso had appeared in Auvergne with 4,000 foot and 300 mounted arquebusiers, part of whom would come to
His Majesty and the other part would go to the relief of Burgundy, where he has been appointed by the King as his
lieutenant and head of that [f. 474r] nobility, which, because none of them would obey another, had very insistently
asked for a captain of valour and experience. The Duke of Épernon, as is said and publicly affirmed, has together 400
lances, 600 mounted arquebusiers and 3,000 foot, and waits for nothing from His Majesty to come and serve but his order
and command. It is true he lets it be understood that he wants to exercise his charge of colonel-general of the
infantry, since otherwise he cannot serve with honour, and so far His Majesty has not been willing to agree to it; and
this is all I can tell Your Most Illustrious Lordship about the particulars of the war on the King's side. The Duke of
Mayenne has for many days, as I have written several times, been due to take the field with his army; but His
Excellency is still in Paris, waiting perhaps for his troops scattered through the kingdom to come together and for the
foreign aid to be near, so as then to resolve on and undertake whatever enterprise he judges most fitting; some hold
that he will go towards Beaugency and Blois, others that he will come straight this way. And today it was said that
Colonel Saint-Paul has arrived at Gien with some troops, and that he had left at Fontainebleau 4 regiments of infantry
following him; so many have come to think that Monsieur de Mayenne might have some idea of besieging Sancerre — a place
famous for the long and hard siege the Huguenots suffered there in the earlier wars — before Monsieur de Montigny, who
is inside for the King with 300 foot and a company of horse, has more opportunity to fortify himself. But as I expect
from hour to hour a man from Paris, I hope perhaps to be able to write Your Most Illustrious Lordship some further
particular of the said Monsieur de Mayenne before I send off this packet. Navarre, who loses no time and trades on [f.
474v] other people's troubles, after making a show of going towards Saumur, has turned […] Monlione (Mauléon?), which was lately taken and
garrisoned by the Duke of Nevers, and has with him 4,000 foot and 600 horse; yet it is hoped that, Lavardin being
inside, his bitterest enemy and a captain of great valour, he will show him a fearless face and make all his designs
vain. The Abbé del Bene, who was confined to his house on the suspicions I wrote of to Your Most Illustrious Lordship
in my last, has been allowed […] all these holy days and feasts of Easter to go to church in the company of only two
men appointed by the Grand Provost, and it is believed he will soon be wholly freed. The Archbishop of Tours, who lives
in Anjou as he is sick and already [an octogenarian], but a prelate of great learning, has issued the ordinance you will
see enclosed here, which could give much to say to many preachers of the kingdom, who are already beginning on other
grounds too to contradict one another, without any gain for our Catholic faith and with much scandal to all good men.
In Paris, in the house of Molan, treasurer of the Épargne, 300,000 écus have been found so far, and another 60,000
should be there according to a memorandum of his; so this money stolen from His Majesty could keep the war going for
them for some time. The Cardinal of Lenoncourt says that the Parisians have sold all his furniture and conferred two of
his abbeys, because he is at court. These three days of Holy Week His Majesty attended the offices at San Francesco di
Paola, at Saint-Martin and at the Franciscans, and took communion on Thursday, Saturday and Easter day, having washed
the feet of the poor as usual in a great hall richly adorned, and touched for the scrofula, with other offices of piety;
