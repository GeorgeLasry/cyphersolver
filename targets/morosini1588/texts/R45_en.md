# R45 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, [mid-]January 1589: audience with Henry III on Navarre, Nevers, the Council of Trent)

Translation of `R45_reading.md` (pages in the cipher's catchword order); holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 359r] […] although I wished, before returning to audience with the King, to wait for some word from Your Most
Illustrious Lordship to learn Our Lord's mind after the unhappy event of the deaths of the Cardinal and the Duke of Guise
(may they be in glory), nevertheless, having heard that a man sent by the King of Navarre was here to negotiate some
accord with His Majesty, and having also learned beforehand that he would not come to the declaration already settled
against Navarre, as all the Estates of the kingdom demand, so as not to fail on my side to do all I can to prevent
anything prejudicial to the Catholic religion, I thought I should not fail to seek audience of His Majesty. He granted
it me today, and I was with him a long time. After making the office of condolence fitting for the death of the Queen
Mother (may she be in heaven), I told His Majesty that the last time I spoke with him he had earnestly begged me to
write to Our Lord assuring him that he was more than ever resolved to make every effort to drive heresy out of this
kingdom, meaning to proceed with all rigour against the heretics, to confiscate and sell all their goods and use the
money to pursue the war against them; that I had not failed [f. 359v] to write this to Your Lordship so that you could
report it to Our Lord; but that I feared I must now write the very opposite, since I heard that His Majesty would not
carry out what he had promised, namely to declare Navarre a heretic and incapable of the succession to this kingdom, and
moreover that a man sent by him was here to negotiate an accord with His Majesty; which, added to his having recalled
the army that was making war on the heretics in order to use it against the people of Orléans, seemed to me to give
great scope to those who show they hold His Majesty to be a poor Catholic to prove their opinion very well by these
arguments. Therefore I begged him to consider carefully what befitted [a] Most Christian King who professed to be a true
Catholic, remembering how great is his obligation to the Lord God, who had him born so great a king, and how much he
must seek to appease the wrath of His Divine Majesty, which must be gravely provoked against him by past events; to
consider the ill satisfaction he would give all the Catholics of his kingdom and of all Christendom if he failed to make
the declaration he has promised against the said [f. 360r] Navarre, for it would surely make people believe that his
aim was that Navarre should succeed him in this kingdom — a thing abhorred by all good men, and which His Majesty more
than anyone ought to abhor in his own interest; since, Navarre being not only a heretic and enemy of God but also
claiming the succession to this crown, His Majesty could never be safe from his plotting against his person to reach
such greatness sooner; and that unless he were declared unfit and [?incapable] of the succession he would have a much
larger following than if everyone knew he could never be King of France; and that he should take great care not to
negotiate an accord with him without Our Lord's consent, because if he came to that I would leave France at once, and
he must forgive me if I did it without coming to take leave of him. I begged him to consider his affairs carefully,
taking care not to put Our Lord under the necessity of making every severe demonstration against him, as I held it
certain he would if he saw His Majesty, after what had happened, make some other patched-up arrangement with the
heretics. His Majesty answered me quite calmly, though it seemed to me his face was troubled, and told me that what he
[f. 360v] had said about wanting more than ever to pursue the war against the heretics he confirmed again, and hoped the
effects would prove this firm resolve of his quite fully; that recalling Monsieur de Nevers with those troops now was
not contrary to his word, because this was a present necessity which could not be remedied otherwise for now, and he
hoped to be done with this need so soon that there would still be time to do against the heretics what he had said;
that it was true he would not make that declaration against the King of Navarre, because in the Edict of Pacification it
was already declared that no heretic could succeed to this crown, which was quite enough to exclude Navarre without a
more express declaration against his particular person, since that would drive him to despair of ever becoming a
Catholic — contrary to what I, by Our Lord's order, had told him, that every effort must be made to bring him back to
the obedience of Holy Church, as he believed everyone ought to wish; but that Navarre had sent a man here to negotiate an
accord was utterly false, and that if it were so he would certainly tell me, if not as [f. 358r] legate, at least as his
friend; and that I could be sure he would never make an accord before Navarre was reconciled with Holy Church. I
replied to His Majesty that for my part I would have wished him to provide for his needs some other way, without
recalling Monsieur de Nevers; and that as to Navarre, it seemed to me he should be so certain Navarre could never be a
good Catholic that he ought not on that account to refrain from making the declaration he had promised to all his
Estates and to the whole world, taking away from Navarre all hope, and from all Catholics the fear, that he might ever
come to this crown; and that it seemed to me that for reasons of policy too His Majesty ought by all means to do it, to
take away his following and to secure his own life, against which Navarre would [conspire] more boldly, [hoping] he
might be King, than when he is sure he cannot be. His Majesty said to me: "I want to speak plainly to you in confidence,
knowing you will keep it to yourself, for it is a matter of very great importance, as I beg you. Know that after the
death of this Guise the Cardinal of Vendôme and his brothers already believe themselves Kings of France, and so they have
contrived that in the article proposed by the Estates to exclude the King of Navarre [f. 358v] a most important clause
has been added, which is that, although he is declared unfit, the right of succession is nevertheless reserved to those
of the House of Bourbon. Now know that if the succession to this crown belongs to them, I do not want to take their
rights from them; but neither do I want to approve […] that they be confirmed in the Estates General of the kingdom, so
that with this security of the succession they cannot plot against my life." I answered His Majesty that if this clause
displeased him he should have it removed and publish the rest. He answered that this could not be done without uproar
and without danger of raising these men too against him, whereas by removing the whole article together they had nothing
to complain of, since this seemed rather in their favour than otherwise; but that he had resolved to proceed against the
King of Navarre by another way, since the next day he meant to annex all his goods to the crown as a rebel and heretic,
which would be worth as much as publishing it by that other way. To which I replied that provided His Majesty made the
declaration, with the consent of the Estates, that he remain wholly excluded from ever succeeding [f. 361r] to the crown
as a heretic and incapable, I did not say it must be done by one way rather than another. With this the conversation
ended; it was much longer than is told here, to spare Your Lordship tedium.

I then spoke again to His Majesty about the publication of the Council, and set out to him exactly what is contained in
Your Lordship's cipher of the [5th?] of last month, showing him Our Lord's just indignation, against me as well, at the
conditions His Majesty meant to add to the publication of the Council; and so I begged and exhorted him to think it over
and take counsel with intelligent and God-fearing persons, for he would find no Catholic who would approve that general
councils confirmed by the Holy Apostolic See could be mutilated in this way by any king or prince however great; and
that if His Majesty persisted in his first resolution he would give great occasion to those who do not love him to prove
by this argument that he has not the zeal for the Catholic religion befitting a Most Christian King, and that for a false
shadow of interest in his authority he would lose the finest occasion that could be offered him to show His Holiness and
the whole world how falsely His Majesty [f. 361v] is accused in matters of religion; whereas by doing the contrary he
would have to fear not only the just wrath of God but also Our Lord's indignation and the ill opinion of all
Christendom; and that in the grief I shall feel at the adversities that may befall him for this, I shall have in my own
conscience this comfort, at least to know I did not fail, by word many times and in writing too, to remind him of what he
is bound to do in this matter by duty of conscience and for the good of his affairs. Afterwards I sent him a memorial to
the same effect, that he might have it read in his council, together with a paper drawn up at the instance of the Bishop
of [Évreux?] and signed by many bishops and others of the Estates General, who say the Council must be published without
any condition. His Majesty answered that he would not fail to consider what I told him; but that it seemed to him I
ought to be content with the conditions *saluis iuribus regis et regni*. I told His Majesty that he ought to publish the
Council as it stands, without other condition, as all the other Catholic kings and princes have done, who no less than
he wish to preserve their authority; and if he claims some reservation, that in accepting the Council he should ask it
[f. 357r] of Our Lord, who, as I had told him many times in His Holiness's name, would oblige him in all honest things.
His Majesty answered nothing more, and I took my leave. I have wished to tell all this to Your Lordship so that you may
remain informed of everything that happens, though I do not know whether I shall have done well or ill, considering what
you write me in your said cipher, where you tell me that I […] let myself be fooled, or that I content myself with talk.
As to being fooled, I do not hold myself so able that it could not happen to me; but as to talk, it seems to me I do
nothing in my letters but represent to Your Lordship the truth of all that happens; and if in this I do not satisfy your
wish, you may be sure it grieves me much and I feel extreme displeasure; but I beg you to believe that I could not do
better, and that I have no greater wish than to serve well, nor think of anything else day and night. But if things do
not turn out as you would wish, I know that in your great prudence you understand that it is not in my hand to make the
King of France do what he will not. The trials against these Guise lords are being prepared, and the other morning they
sent two councillors to examine the Archbishop of Lyon; but he said that, being archbishop and primate of France, he
could not be examined by laymen; and I too let His Majesty know that he should refrain from it, as he has done […].
