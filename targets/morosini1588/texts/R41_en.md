# R41 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, [8?] December 1588, duplicate: Savoy refuses the Nemours solution; Guise urges war)

Translation of `R41_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 298r] […] from letters of Signor Girolamo Gondi written to His Majesty from Seville […] it is learned that the Duke
of Savoy had not accepted the proposal to put Monsieur de Nemours, his cousin, into the marquisate of Saluzzo; but that
His Highness, persisting in [?wanting] to keep those places in his hand until the Dauphiné should be free of the
Huguenots, offered, as security for restoring them at that time, to send his wife into France and to give one of his
sons as hostage. With these proposals they are very ill satisfied here, and preparations for war go on, though not with
the vigour seen at the start, because of the ill feeling that still persists between His Majesty and Monsieur de Guise,
which greatly hinders every other decision; although on this particular they are very much agreed, and Monsieur de
Guise even shows himself more ardent than His Majesty, knowing that he gains by it greater favour with the nobility and
peoples of France, who cannot rest at seeing that the Duke of Savoy has chosen to start a war with this kingdom. And
indeed yesterday morning, having been with me a long time, the said Monsieur de Guise told me that on no account could
he, for his honour, refrain from making every effort to avenge the injury France has received from the Duke of Savoy,
who [f. 298v] was already beginning to recognise that he had taken a bad decision in breaking with this crown, since
all his peoples of Bresse and Savoy offered to open the gates of their towns to His Majesty whenever he sends his forces
there; and that the ambassador of that Highness had several times asked him for an audience, and he had answered that
if he wished to treat of the restitution of the marquisate he would listen to him, but otherwise he had nothing to do
with him; and for all that the said ambassador had told Madame de Nemours, his mother, that the Duke his master would
be content to restore everything if His Excellency would promise him that, should the King die without children, he
would put him back in possession of the marquisate; and that he would not give him a hearing. I did not fail to put
before him the great harm the Catholics of France would suffer from this war, and the advantage to the Huguenots, with
whatever else seemed to me to the point; but he answered that all this was the fault of the Duke of Savoy, who under
pretext of pursuing heretics was coming to favour [them], and that it was a maxim in the profession of war and of the
good government of a kingdom always to hold the frontiers, because, these being kept, it was then easy to remedy [f.
299r] the disorders that occur in the body of the kingdom; and that to preserve his state and his honour a man exposes
himself to every kind of danger; with many other words to this effect. Although I did not fail to answer, using the
arguments Your Most Illustrious Lordship gives in your cipher of the 8th of last month and many others besides, he
remained firm that for his honour he could not fail to advise the King to do all in his power to recover what had been
taken from him with such indignity to him and to this kingdom, and that if His Majesty would not do it he would take
leave to retire to his house, so as not to share in the shame the King would incur if he put up with this affront. And
from his words it seems to me he meant to say that he had been the author of having the King send the Savoy ambassador
his passport to return home, as Your Lordship will learn outside the cipher, and of having Monsieur de Poigny recalled
too; to whom yesterday a gentleman was sent post-haste with orders that he return to France as soon as possible.

Yesterday Signor Alfonso Corso arrived here, sent by Monsieur de Mayenne to let the King know that the Duke of Savoy was
to be at Chambéry within a few days, and would have with him a good number of soldiers; that [f. 299v] he therefore
wished to know from His Majesty how he wanted him to act, having the enemy so near, and asking for a larger number of
cavalry than he now has, since he has no more than 500 horse and about 4,000 foot. [However] they say that until the
troops I have already written are destined for that war are raised, another 500 horse and as many more foot as possible
will be sent him, the dispatches already given being pressed for the rest. The Estates are moving to their conclusion,
and from what Monsieur de Guise has told me they might be finished within [twenty] days; but before it comes to the
conclusion His Excellency, who knows his advantage from the great power he has with these Estates, would like to use
their authority to force the King to set up a secret council to treat all the affairs of the kingdom, in which there
would be no one opposed to his party. What he will be able to do about this I [do not know], since it is not possible
that His Majesty will consent to it willingly, and to use violence, as they have done in various other matters I have
already written to Your Lordship, could be dangerous and bring about a break. And so I do not fail to do every kind of
office so that union and peace may be kept […]
