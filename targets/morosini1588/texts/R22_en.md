# R22 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, 7 October 1588: the Council of Trent, the faculties, Soissons' absolution, Avignon, Épernon)

Translation of `R22_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 132r] This […] to the sheet of cipher that came in Your Most Illustrious Lordship's letters of the 19th of last
month […] there is no need to say, that as to the publication of the Council of Trent I have never failed to do every
most effective office so that it be published freely and without any condition, as I have also reported to Your Most
Illustrious Lordship in several of my letters; and I have already had the promise of all three Estates, though
separately from one another, that all together they will ask for it in their articles (cahiers) to His Majesty. I
shall not fail to continue, using those persuasions that are set out in the said cipher. As to presenting the
faculties of the legation to the Parlement, I already wrote to Your Most Illustrious Lordship with mine of the 12th of
last month that I had presented them […], so advised by persons of the first rank and most devoted to Our Lord and to
that Holy See. His Majesty nevertheless keeps them by him, not having had time to see and consider them; I shall not
fail to do whatever office […] commits to me. But it must be known that if the […] faculties are not verified
(registered) by the Parlement, all the acts I made would be [f. 132v] without [force?] […], since the Parlement is what
has to have them executed and obeyed, as also happens with all the edicts and ordinances the King himself makes: if
they are not verified by the Parlement they are not obeyed, such being the form of government of this kingdom.

As to the absolution of the princes of Vendôme: having seen what Your Most Illustrious Lordship writes me outside the
cipher in several letters, that I must in any case [wait for] further orders before coming to that absolution, and
having then considered what the cipher contains, I have not known what course to take; for though you tell me that you
are sending me the faculty and the manner of absolving them, and that I, as I think fit, may carry it out or delay it
as I judge good for the kingdom, and although I thought I could now give the absolution by virtue of this order, I did
not think I could alter the form that you send me. Since the Count of Soissons would in no way accept it, saying that
he should not be accused [f. 133r] of a sin he had not even thought of, let alone committed, I resolved to defer it,
though with the greatest resentment both of the said Count and of all his people and of the King himself; but, loving
rather to obey [precisely] the orders of Our Lord than to satisfy others, I believed I could not go further with their
complaint. […] that, having […] I made known […], as Your Most Illustrious Lordship wrote me with yours of 22 August,
and I with mine of the 2nd of last month informed Your Most Illustrious Lordship that I had communicated this […] to
Their Majesties and to the Most Illustrious (Cardinals) of Bourbon and of Vendôme, on which they had treated with me of
the form of the absolution, and made the said Count condescend to be content to abjure in the form Your Most
Illustrious Lordship will see in the copy I send you, in which the truth of the fact is told and the substance of it
set down; and it seemed to me that it mattered more to remove those words [f. 133v] that offended these lords. And on
the very day your letters reached me I was to give him the absolution, it having been arranged that the Count,
arriving shortly […] go elsewhere, should alight at the house of my lodging, where the office would have been done
with the due circumstances. But having left his house and come within two leagues of here, it seemed to him he was
receiving a great affront if I did not absolve him. I excused myself by saying […] that I could do no more than I am
[ordered], and that Our Lord [loves] the Count of Soissons and will in any case give him the said absolution, but that,
His Excellency having sent a gentleman to Our Lord for this purpose, […] will send whatever order he sees fit. This
being heard, the Cardinals of Bourbon and of Vendôme came at once to find me, pressing me hard to oblige them with the
absolution; and I, after seeing that they were not [f. 134r] content with the form sent by Your Most Illustrious
Lordship, answered their Most Illustrious Lordships in conformity with what I had said before; whereupon they resolved
to go to the King, with whom they have made a most vigorous office that His Majesty should send […] post-haste to Rome
on this account. And now they come to tell me that the courier leaves within two hours; so I write this in great
haste, but I will not omit to tell you that here they show they know that this change [proceeds] from bad offices done
with Our Lord at the instance of the Cardinal of Sens, by the Most Illustrious Alessandrino, and they complain of [me],
that I exhorted them to send a man express to Our Lord, since many bishops in France had authority to absolve them
without going to Rome. I, from what I know and am informed, find that the Count of Soissons is not more than 20 years
old, and that when he was given to the Cardinal of Bourbon he was not more than 4, and since then he has been brought
up by His Most Illustrious Lordship and has always lived a Catholic, and now he shows himself [f. 134v] most attached
to the Catholic religion; and I fear that making difficulties over the absolution can help neither the Catholic
religion nor the kingdom, because it will certainly make harder the return of Navarre to the obedience of the
Apostolic See, and will be cause of renewing the hatred between this King and those of Guise, since they hold for
certain that all the evil comes from them, though at present, through offices done by me too, they show themselves
quite well disposed together. It seems too that the King resented it much, and wants to put off the opening of the
Estates until the return of this […]; but [with] me His Majesty has not yet spoken of it, as I have not been able to go
and find him, having hurt a foot, which does not let me walk. I hope in one or two days to be able to go out, which I
greatly desire, to make certain whether it is true that the ambassador of the King of Denmark, who I write outside the
cipher has come to this court, comes to persuade His Majesty to unite with his King in defence of the Queen of
England, so as to do in [f. 135r] that case the office that befits the honour of the Lord God and the service of Our
Lord.

I have been much surprised that Monsignor the vice-legate of Avignon complains, as Your Most Illustrious Lordship
writes me in the said cipher, that, though he wrote to me several times on matters important to his government, I
have not answered him; which, were it true, Your Most Illustrious Lordship could with great reason say that this does
not please Our Lord. But you may rest assured that I have had from the said Monsignor […] only three letters on some
business, to all of which I have not only answered by letter but also with all the effects that could come from me, as
Fioravanti his secretary knows very well, who for many months now has stayed at court handling the vice-legate's
business without communicating with me anything but what suits him. The same I can say on the last head of the said
cipher, where you tell me that the Duke of Épernon and La Valette his brother complain of being abandoned by that Holy
See, and that Our Lord wonders that I have not done [f. 135v] the proper office for these lords, as I have been
written by familiar letters, by briefs and by instructions; and I take comfort that Your Most Illustrious Lordship, in
your third letter of the same date of the 19th of last month, outside the cipher, writes me that the office done by
[…] with the Duke of Guise for the benefit of Épernon and his brother, and the letters written to them, have pleased
Our Lord, and that he wills that this business go forward. I therefore pray Your Most Illustrious Lordship to assure
Our Lord that I shall never fail to obey with all promptness the orders of Our Lord, nor do I fear that he will ever
have cause to hold me negligent, my obligations being too great not to watch always over his service, as I shall do
with the favour of the Lord God all the time of my life. […] will know that, besides what I have written from time to
time of having done in this matter, I have also worked a great deal more, and so as not to tire Your Most Illustrious
Lordship with too long a letter I have left off writing it; but in effect it seems to me that they ought not to refuse
