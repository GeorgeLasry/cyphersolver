# R38 — English (Morosini to Montalto, n.d. [Blois, late November – December 1588], duplicate: Savoy, Saluzzo and Mayenne; Lenoncourt; the Avignon legation)

Translation of `R38_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 282r] […] I was with His Majesty at great length the day before yesterday; and because the day before the
ambassador of the Duke of Savoy had told me he had been informed as a certain thing that His Majesty had sent word to the
Duke of Mayenne to enter Bresse with his forces and […] to the harm of his master, of which he said he had informed His
Highness in haste, it seemed to me right to do every effective office with His Majesty, urging him not to begin a war
before he had tried every other way to recover what was his, reminding him that those who were persuading him to take up
arms sought nothing else than to keep him always occupied, so that he could never make himself obeyed in his kingdom;
and that it would therefore be much better for His Majesty to work first for the extirpation of the heretics, because
when he attends to this, Our Lord will at his own expense have the marquisate of Saluzzo restored to him — it being
credible that the Duke of Savoy took those places with the intention of taking them from the crown of France, with which
he knows he cannot contend, but so that [they] should not come back […] to his own harm and Italy's; and that therefore
His Majesty ought not to take offence, but rather, as his kinsman and servant, [to] make use of him, and that Our Lord
would see to it that he serves him well; and, wanting security, that Our Lord would strive with spiritual and temporal
arms to assure it to him. His Majesty answered me that he had given no order to the Duke of Mayenne, for these were [f.
282v] inventions of the ambassador of Savoy, who was waiting for the answer to be given him on the proposal to put
Monsieur de Nemours in the government of the marquisate; which, if the Duke of Savoy would not accept it, would make his
ill will plain to all; that he was more than resolved to make war on the Huguenots and to drive them entirely out of his
kingdom, but that he did not believe anyone would advise him to be content to leave his states in other men's hands, and
that he knew for certain that if Our Lord were in his place he would never tolerate it; that he and his kingdom did not
lack [the means] to obtain justice, should necessity force him to take up arms, and that justice was on his side, because
Our Lord, by the place he holds, could not fail to favour his cause. On this he spoke at length, showing some
displeasure, though in very moderate words, that Our Lord not only did not show the resentment that His Majesty thought
fitting against the said Duke, but rather showed himself favourable to him; saying that if the Duke of Savoy had such
good zeal against the Huguenots as he [would] have it believed, he ought first to drive them out of his own states, and
that he nevertheless tolerates them […], for in Italy many freely and publicly preach and practise Calvinism.

[f. 283r] I did not fail to answer His Majesty in conformity with what Your Most Illustrious Lordship charges me in your
cipher of […] of last month, begging him, after many words, not to believe that Our Lord ever [?] the Duke of Savoy more
than him; [and] I could assure him that Our Lord's mind could not be better than it is towards His Majesty and this whole
kingdom, but that as common father and Vicar of Christ, having regard to the universal good, he urged His Majesty to make
war on the heretics rather than on the Catholics, and that he should be certain that Our Lord was doing the same office
with the Duke of Savoy too. But whatever I said, he showed himself not satisfied, as if he thought I had said too much.
Nevertheless he told me of the office […] that the King of Spain had made with him on this matter, of which I was also
informed by the same ambassador, and it ran in this substance: that although the past actions of the King of Spain, and
in particular the constant vigilance he had always had to maintain good friendship with His Majesty and to preserve peace
in Italy, could be sure signs that what happened in the marquisate of Saluzzo had not been done with his counsel or
knowledge, he nevertheless affirmed on the word of a prince that he had [?had] no part [f. 283v] in it; on the contrary, as
soon as he heard the news he had sent one of his counsellors to the said Duke of Savoy to [learn] from him […] the cause
that had moved him to this act, with the intention, should he not be willing to proceed by the [?way] of reason, of
persuading him to embrace it; and that he would give His Majesty an account of the answer His Highness would give,
offering to interpose his authority to bring his son-in-law to reason. To which His Majesty answered that he was assured
that all the ambassador told him in his King's name was true, and that he thanked him very much, adding that all the
offices done [with] the Duke of Savoy to make him resolve to restore what was his would be most dear to him, as he much
wished that this should come about rather by way of peace, preserving love, than that he should be forced to obtain it
otherwise. His Majesty sent this answer to the said ambassador by [one of] his secretaries of state, to […], a village
three leagues from here, where the ambassadors are lodged, and it is awaited whether he will reply anything further.

In this audience I made […] what Your Most Illustrious Lordship writes to me about the legation of Avignon for the
Cardinal of Guise, who then came [f. 284r] to see me at home and asked me the particulars of Your Most Illustrious
Lordship's letters on this matter; I told him what seemed necessary to me, and especially that he would have to swear to
maintain that legation according to the obligation agreed with His Holiness Pius IV; and on this account His Most
Illustrious Lordship told me that he would have the King write, doing the same himself, and then send a courier [?] to
give Our Lord all the […] [?satisfaction] he desired in this respect.

I have pressed His Majesty in every way that the sentence given by the Parlement of Rennes against the indult of the
Cardinal de Lenoncourt be revoked, and that His Most Illustrious Lordship may exercise the charge laid on him by Our
Lord; he has promised me to remind the Keeper of the Seals of it, to see whether the answer of the said Parlement had
come, which had been written to give the reasons that moved it to give that sentence. And I shall not fail to do and
press it as much as I can in the service of His Most Illustrious Lordship, who for some days has left the court and
withdrawn to his house, it being said that he did so out of dissatisfaction, seeing himself hated by Monsieur de Guise
[f. 284v] and less favoured by His Majesty than he used to be.

Now I have heard that His Majesty was a long time in council on the affairs of the marquisate of Saluzzo, and that in
fact they are about making provision for war, should the Duke of Savoy persist in his resolve to keep those places. And
Monsieur de Guise has told me that war will be made on Savoy; the King would have to make […] a truce with the Huguenots
of Dauphiné, so as not to have enemies at his back and in front. And although I answered His [Excellency] that this
should in no way be done […], that he saw no remedy […] Our Lord […].
