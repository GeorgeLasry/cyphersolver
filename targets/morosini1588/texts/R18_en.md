# R18 — English (Morosini to Montalto, [Paris], 10 August 1588, duplicate: the Council of Trent and the Gallican liberties)

## Translation

[f. 32r] […] concerning the publication of the Council of Trent, [I] have not failed and do not fail to do all the most
effective offices, and [to oppose (?)], so that it be done without any limitation or condition; but in fact I find very
great resistance in everyone, for they will not in any way hear anything said against this pretended liberty of the
Gallican church. And they say that in Spain too, in the kingdom of [Aragon], they chose to accept it so as not to
prejudice their privileges; that in what concerns dogmas of faith they will accept it without any exception or
condition, but as to the reform — and it is a plain thing that no universal and uniform rule can be given that suits all
kingdoms, each having its own particular customs, from which they could be drawn away only with too great difficulty —
and that this being very well known to the Holy Apostolic See, at the time when Monsignor the Bishop of Rimini, of good
memory, was nuncio here, some arrangements had been reached to settle the whole to the satisfaction of Our Lord, but
that it had not been possible to do enough. These views are held universally on all sides, even by those who at my
instance have put this article on the publication of the council among their demands; but there is no lack of those who
speak [f. 32v] more freely and impertinently, to whom, although I do not fail to answer as is fitting, I fear that if
one had to go on to more violent offices than those made so far, it would easily come to a rupture, which I think it is
well to avoid as much as one can, since I do not see that anything could come of it but loss to that Holy See.
Nevertheless, if I had a wrong opinion, when I am advised of it by Your Most Illustrious Lordship I shall not fail to
follow exactly all that you have recommended to me.

Now that I am in Paris I do not fail to [show] to these men of the Sorbonne — as I also did with those of the King's
council before I left Chartres — with the reasons furnished to me by the Lord God, that [162] are bound to assist so
useful and holy a work; and that it was very fitting that, if [the King] of France holds the title of Most Christian, he
should be content also to receive the said council in his kingdom freely, as has been done in the other kingdoms and
provinces of Catholic Christians; and that it was already wrong to have delayed so long, with such grave scandal to
Christians and provoking the wrath of God; nay, that His Majesty ought to be the one who had [given] the example to the
others. But in fact I find very great difficulty and reluctance in everyone.

The last time I spoke [f. 33r] with His Majesty on this matter, besides what I have already written in other letters, I
told him that, wishing to establish his affairs, he needed to show himself more ardent than the others in the things
that concern the honour of God and the exaltation of the Catholic religion; that he saw that the honour of the
publication of the Council of Trent would be attributed to those of the League, because they had asked for it in their
articles, but that it was in His Majesty's hands to take this advantage from them and gain it for himself, by willing
that what they had asked for with condition and restriction be published and accepted freely and without condition.
And when he asked me how I wanted him to go against what had already been agreed, so as to return once more to arms, I
answered that this would not be going against it but rather helping it; and that at least, when the others complained
and wanted execution according to the form of the agreement, His Majesty would gain, in the opinion of Our Lord and of
all Christendom, what by delaying its publication he was coming to have [lay] on the others — the blame for the
impediment. He answered that with this they too were gaining more favour in the kingdom, since everyone universally
wants the preservation of the privileges and [f. 33v] liberty of the Gallican church, to which, were he to show himself
contrary, he would make himself universally hateful to all. I said that in the things of God one must not proceed with
worldly regards or other interests, but that His Majesty at least ought to give an account of all this to Our Lord and
leave the decision to […] Our Lord, assuring him that in whatever could be done he would find greater readiness in Our
Lord to please him and to show himself kind and favourable to this kingdom than he himself could desire, with many other
words to this effect. To these His Majesty answered me nothing except that those who were now asking for the
publication of the council were precisely those who more than the others had opposed it, and especially the Archbishop
of Lyon; that he had always desired it, trusting that in the things that might prejudice his authority Our Lord would
have provided a remedy. I said that His Majesty could expect every fatherly demonstration from Our Lord, but that there
was nothing in the council that would bring any harm or damage to him as a true king and just prince, if he wanted the
affairs of his kingdom to proceed with justice and with discipline in the clergy; that His Majesty should have those
who dissuade him [f. 34r] from the publication tell him which are the points they find contrary to his authority,
because I was persuaded that he himself, once he has considered them, will see how little reason they have to put
these thoughts into his head. I shall not fail to continue the same offices, but with little hope of a better decision,
since words are not enough to persuade the contrary, and coming to stronger remedies could easily lead us to some
rupture, into which, as one can fall with great ease, so one may fear that it could be remedied only with much
difficulty and without greater harm. So perhaps it would be more opportune to try to gain this point, that the council
be published in the manner they have already agreed, and that the points of difficulty be referred to Our Lord, giving
them greater hope of satisfaction, since they do not seem disposed to renounce the graces and privileges they claim to
have had long since from the Apostolic See in order to ask for new ones. Your Most Illustrious Lordship will consider
all this and be pleased to advise me what I must do, for I shall not fail to obey whatever I am commanded.

The [f. 34v] affairs of the kingdom outwardly go quite well, for the King shows that he wants to give every
satisfaction to Monsieur de Guise, and His Excellency proceeds with very great humility and reverence towards His
Majesty; but with all that one may well fear some new accident, for the King is most resolved to hold the Estates
General of the kingdom, hoping by this, besides many other particulars for his service, to bring Monsieur de Guise and
those of his party to restore the places they hold; and on the other side the Duke greatly fears these Estates, and
does all he can underhand to dissuade the King from holding them, or else to gain time so as to have the means to carry
on his dealings, so that persons to his liking are deputed to the Estates. But he cannot do this so secretly that the
King does not see clearly that he would not want them; from which there grows in His Majesty a greater desire to hold
them and to hasten the assembly, thinking that what they dislike must turn out to his greater advantage. From which it
is quite plainly seen that minds are still divided and full of suspicions. It is also seen that the King's intention
would be, should he have no offspring, to settle the succession of the crown [f. 35r] on those who are of his blood, to
whom by right of the kingdom it belongs to succeed; which cannot in any way please the others, and although they do
not dare to reveal this feeling of theirs, nevertheless under other pretexts they do not fail to hinder these designs
of the King [as much as] they can. I do not fail to do all that is in me to show both His Majesty and the others that
returning again to a rupture would be the total ruin of this kingdom, and now with the one and now with the other I go
about removing suspicions, and trying with the authority of Our Lord to exhort them to true union among themselves, so
as to be able to attend all the better to the extirpation of the heretics.

The Spanish ambassador has complained loudly to me that in some ports of this kingdom the English fleet has received
supplies, but these people deny it openly; and so far I have not been able to get a sure report of it. When I am certain
of it I shall not fail to complain of it to His Majesty and do the office that is fitting in such a case. […]
