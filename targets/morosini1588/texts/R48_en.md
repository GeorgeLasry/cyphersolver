# R48 — English (Morosini to Montalto, [Blois, January 1589]: his defence; news of Paris; the audience on the prisoners' removal to Amboise)

Translation of `R48_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 399r] […] having already written enough to Your Most Illustrious Lordship about my own affairs with my last letters,
and meaning not to dispute with one whom I wish to revere, [obey] and serve, it will suffice now to tell you in answer
to your cipher of the 7th of this month that in so much affliction and sadness I still feel some spark of relief, seeing
that Our Lord remembers the commands His Holiness was pleased to give me when he laid on me the charge of this
nunciature, which, and everything that has been laid on me until now, I have obeyed most exactly, as can be seen from
the letters I have written from time to time and from the answers I have received, which alone can serve better than
anything else to make my actions fully known; and these, though they be weak, are nevertheless full of the utmost
sincerity and fidelity towards Our Lord's service. And although I cannot deny that the dissatisfaction he shows with me
and the stings he continually gives me pierce me to the depths of my heart, I would nevertheless receive death itself
as a very great reward if by it what His Holiness desires could be remedied and satisfied, in whose most pious mind
[f. 399v] I cannot however persuade myself that there does not remain some spark of goodwill towards a most humble and
devoted creature of his, who for any accident whatever will never fail to acknowledge the undying obligations he owes
him, nor will cease to pray, as he continually does, to the Lord God for his entire happiness and prosperity. On which
I shall add nothing more, except that I await with infinite longing the leave I have humbly asked for with my preceding
letters, so as to be able to come in person to give account of all my actions in this nunciature and legation.

Things here are going poorly for His Majesty, for it has been learned that those of Paris, after obtaining a promise
from the Duke of Mayenne to take their protection and to accept the command of the war against His Majesty, have come
to the demonstrations Your Most Illustrious Lordship will have learned outside the cipher; and that colonel who had
given His Majesty to understand that he would bring that city back to obedience, as I wrote with other letters, has let
it be known that on returning to Paris he found the humours so changed that it is no longer in his power to do what he
had promised; so it is believed that [f. 400r] […] to do this office with the King, both to satisfy this and also to
remind His Majesty of what I judge necessary to see whether some way could be found to quiet this kingdom. But His
Majesty has gone on deferring and putting off the audience with various excuses — which […] I have not however asked
for with insistence except in the last three days.

At this moment the Maréchal de Retz has come to tell me that tomorrow the King will leave here for Amboise, and it is
believed that he will take with him these lords he holds prisoner, so that they may be guarded in that castle, and that
the following day he will come back here. To me His Majesty has not so far let anything be known; but I am told that
the reigning Queen, not knowing of this resolution of His Majesty except from the rumours going round the court, had
the Grand Prior called and sent him to ask the King whether it was true that His Majesty meant to leave, and what she
was to do; to which he sent word that he wished to go and gather his forces, but that she was not to move, as he meant
to return soon. The said Maréchal has also told me that he intends to leave tomorrow for Italy, having had leave from
His Majesty to go to the baths at Lucca [f. 400v] to treat some indispositions of his; that the King wanted him to carry
letters to the Duke of Savoy so that he would agree to have the strongholds of the marquisate of Saluzzo handed over
into his hands; but that he […] had wanted them, saying however that if the said Duke agreed and Our Lord thought it
good that he should do it for the common service, […] he would not refuse to enter them, resolved however to act so
that neither the King nor the Duke should have cause to complain of him […]

[f. 401r] […] having learned very early this morning that the Cardinal of Bourbon was being sent to Amboise, and
fearing some further mishap, without asking another audience I went at once to His Majesty, and having entered his
chamber, while he was dressing in his cabinet I had him told that I wished to speak to him before he left. And when I
had been let in, I told him that, having asked audience several times without being able to obtain it, and learning
that the Cardinal of Bourbon was being sent to the castle of Amboise with the Archbishop of Lyon and that he himself
was leaving here, I had not been able, to discharge my duty, to refrain from taking the liberty of going there to speak
without having been granted an audience, in order to tell him that I begged him to remember the grave excess he had
committed in the person of the Cardinal of Guise, God rest him; with which, besides the offence to the Lord God, Our
Lord had also been most ill satisfied; and that I, as his minister, should he go on adding disorder to disorder, would
have had to do my office; that it seemed to me, since he said he was keeping these men not to do them any harm but
only for his own safety, that he could be content [f. 401v] that they should go on staying here in the same way as they
had been until now, at least until some [order] came from Our Lord. The King answered me that he believed, having done
justice on those who wanted to take away his honour and his life, that he had committed no sin, and that it is well
known that in such cases one does not pardon even one's own children; that he had already told me, and now repeated to
me, that neither the [Cardinal] of Bourbon nor the Archbishop of Lyon would come to any harm, but that he wanted them not
to be able to do harm to him either; that I must already have heard that Monsieur de Mayenne was approaching these
parts, and so he could not stay here, having to mount his horse and draw near to Orléans with his forces, to keep them
all together so as to be able to resist the designs of his enemies; that it was therefore necessary for him to make
sure of those prisoners, since he was certain that in Paris they wanted nothing more than the freedom of the said
Cardinal in order to make him King of France at once; and that he knew well that Our Lord himself would not advise him
to do […] to prevent there being, while he lived, any other King in France, […] as he was most resolved to do,
[f. 402r] even at the loss of his life; and he trusted that Our Lord would not take it ill that, to secure his life,
his state and his honour, he should for some time keep the said Cardinal in a most beautiful castle, where he will have
the use of a chapel, a garden, good rooms and all the servants he wishes to have with him. I said that the world saw
the executions carried out by His Majesty, but that so far the reasons that had moved him were not understood. He told
me that he had sent through the Bishop of Le Mans to give an account of it to Our Lord, and that a declaration of the
whole would also be published here, complaining bitterly that his actions were always interpreted in a bad sense, and
especially by the preachers, who were the sole cause of stirring up the people. I added that if His Majesty had
published the Council he would have shut the mouths of all these preachers; on which, entering again into the usual
disputes, after many altercations he settled on this resolution: to have letters patent sent out now to all the bishops,
letting them know that he accepted the said Council of Trent, [and] that they should have it published and observed
each in his [f. 402v] diocese without adding any condition or reservation; and that through the Bishop of Le Mans he
would put before Our Lord the heads he wishes His Holiness to reserve to him, as I had promised him in Our Lord's name.
And in my presence, having called the Keeper of the Seals and the first Secretary of State, he ordered the one and the
other to draw up this letter and to give me a copy of it, which, if it is sent to me, I shall forward with my next to
Your Most Illustrious Lordship; and I shall see that it […] in good form and without any condition, as His Majesty has
promised me. I did not fail to remind him to have it approved by the Parlement as well; but he answered me that there
was no Parlement now, since he had taken away its authority […]
