# R56 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Moulins, mid-June 1589: the monitory, Lyon closed to him, Lyon turns royalist)

Translation of `R56_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 522r] […] in this my convalescence nothing could afflict me […] more than to learn from Your Most Illustrious
Lordship's letter of the 29th of last month, as my secretary had told me to the same effect, though without knowing any
particular, that I had been written to at length by order of Our Lord; for, the letters not having reached me, nor
knowing where to turn to get them, it follows that not only can I not know, to my infinite distress and perhaps to the
disservice of Our Lord, what I was commanded, but that I must also reasonably suspect some stratagem and fraud, with
[the] aim of whoever wished to disturb for me that favour which I have always so anxiously sought to deserve from Our
Lord's kindness. So I live most disconsolate, and shall never be able to give myself peace until I have from Your Most
Illustrious Lordship that light on this matter which is needed to obey and serve; for which I beg you with the most
humble and reverent affection that can be wished for anything in this world. I was thinking of withdrawing to Lyon to
put myself in safety, since, when the monitory is published here which they write from there has already been posted at
St Peter's, and when the departure from court of the Cardinal of Joyeuse and of the ambassador of France is known, it
may be feared that some affront will be done to me, and perhaps not without danger to my life. But having sent to Lyon
to learn whether I could safely go and stay there until further orders from Your Most Illustrious Lordship, they first
courteously let me know that they would have seen me willingly, but afterwards they wrote me a letter in which they say
that, to remove every cause of ill feeling among those people, they begged me to be content not to stay more than three
days in Lyon. So I find myself in so unhappy a plight that I no longer have anywhere to go or stay; and if Our Lord is not
moved to pity by freeing me from these miseries, I shall run manifest danger of suffering some very serious harm, with
little dignity for that Holy See; which [f. 522v] I wanted to tell Your Most Illustrious Lordship, not because I fear
death, but so that you understand the state I am in; and if it pleases Our Lord that I run this danger, here I am, ready
to bear any martyrdom.

To this day it is not heard here that the said monitory has been published in any of the cities named; nor is that to
be wondered at, though three weeks have already passed, for the commander of Dio[u?] has left Lyon to go to Paris, but
the roads are so broken up by soldiers that one can no longer go about in any way. Here it is said that the King had
gone to Chartres and had pardoned that city its rebellion with much kindness, and that all His Majesty's affairs were
going very prosperously; […] they say that the Swiss were to be at Langres on the 15th of this month, as Your Most
Illustrious Lordship will see from the copy of a paragraph of a letter written to Monsieur de La Guiche. At this news,
true or false, these people have fallen into great fear; and so, although before it seemed that the greater part
inclined to the League, and that the governor-president himself had already signed the Union, they have all changed
their mind, and on the arrival of Monsieur de La Guiche they have taken a solemn oath of loyalty to the King, in the form
Your Most Illustrious Lordship will see from the copy that will be with this, and driven out all those of the League;
though the most attached, who showed themselves more passionate than the others, were the first to swear they would die
in obedience to His Majesty — for which reason my staying in this place will become even less safe. They say that
Monsieur de Mayenne had gone to a castle of a treasurer of His Majesty, eight or ten leagues from Paris, called La
Grange-le-[Roy?], reckoned quite strong, and that he had taken it in the hope of finding a quantity of money there, but
that up [f. 523r] to that hour they had found nothing. It was hoped that by now the Archbishop of Lyon [would be] freed
with the money I have written of to Your Most Illustrious Lordship at other times, but it seems that Monsieur d'Épernon
is hindering the release. Today it was said that Signor Alfonso Corso had crossed the Rhône towards Mirabel with two
thousand foot and some horse, to go and join the Swiss who are coming for the King. I am not sure that all this news is
true; but I can write to Your Most Illustrious Lordship only what I hear, and sparingly at that, because if I wrote all
that is said here I am sure I would tell you much more falsehood than truth. […]
