# R20 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, 9 September 1588: Paris opposes the letters patent for the Count of Soissons)

Translation of `R20_reading.md`; holes as there. Page order 88r–90r, then 87r–v (a second sheet).

## Translation

[f. 88r] […] here last Sunday, in the company of a gentleman of Monsieur de Guise, sent by His Excellency, […] to find
me, an échevin of the city of Paris; and after some offices of compliment made to me in the name of the city, he told
me that the prévôt des marchands and the others who are in the government had thought it necessary that I be informed
of what had happened in Paris in the days before, so that I might take up their protection with His Majesty. He said
that, having learned that His Majesty had granted the Count of Soissons letters patent by which he [cleared] him of all
the guilt he had incurred by having been with the King of Navarre and with the Huguenots, and that these letters had
been presented to the Parlement for [it] to approve or register them, as they say here, many pious and Catholic men
of that city had gone to the said Parlement to oppose this registration, and had presented a request of the tenor that
Your Most Illustrious Lordship will see from the translated copy I am sending you. He said that here the Cardinal of
Vendôme and the Princess of Condé had made such an uproar with Their Majesties that they wanted all the citizens of
Paris for dead men, and therefore he begged me to be willing [f. 88v] to defend them and protect their cause, since
they had moved for nothing else than zeal for the Catholic religion, fearing that, if this discharge of the Count of
Soissons passed without opposition, His Majesty might one day do the same for Navarre, or put them in danger of falling
under the tyrannical rule of the heretics; with many other considerations on this subject. I did not fail to answer
[as] was fitting, assuring them first of my affection and good will towards that most Christian and most devout city,
and praising the zeal they have for the preservation of the Catholic religion, exhorting them […] to keep the peace
that had been newly concluded, by which all must subscribe to the Union; and the said Count of Soissons having […]
subscribed and sworn like the others to make war on the heretics, and having always been a Catholic, having also lately
sent a gentleman to [the] Queen Mother [to] obtain from Our Lord absolution for his error, it seemed to me a great gain
to have drawn him over to us and separated him from the heretics, and that he should now want to be one of those who go
to make war on them. The gentleman [f. 89r] of Monsieur de Guise confirmed what I said, but all the same said he did not
know what security there could be that the said Count does not still inwardly favour Navarre. To which I said
that it would be a great security if he himself went to make war on him. While I was talking with him, the Cardinal of
Vendôme came in, who asked me whether I had had any answer about the absolution of his brothers, showing that the delay
weighed on him […], since the Estates were about to open. I said that time did not allow that answer to have come yet,
and that as soon as I had it I would not fail to let His Most Illustrious Lordship know; but that it displeased me
greatly that, while Our Lord's order was being awaited, letters patent of the King were being drawn out, which ought not
to have been done until after the absolution had been obtained from Our Lord. The Cardinal told me that what had been
done was for no other purpose than to gain time, of which they are in extreme need; and his brother having sent a
gentleman to [112], from whose Holiness they expected a most benign answer, while that was coming they had [f. 89v]
obtained these letters so that they would be ready as soon as his brother was reconciled with Holy Church, of which he
wished to be a most obedient and devout son and servant; and that he did not think they were to be presented to the
Parlement before then, and that this had been done without his consent; and I could see that, since I had told him it
was not good for the Count to come to court before there was an answer from Our Lord, he had not let him come, though
there were many who urged him to the contrary, his interest being handled here too coarsely, […]. And speaking of the
opposition made to His Majesty's letters by those of the city who had gone a thousand strong to the Parlement, he spoke
with great passion, saying that this had been a great boldness, that it touched the King's dignity too much, and that it
put His Majesty under the necessity of resenting it, with other similar words. I told him that the fault was theirs,
because they ought first to have awaited the judgement of Our Lord, which would have stopped everyone's mouth [f. 90r]
at court; and the Count of Soissons would have come to Blois in these days: I did a strong office with Their Majesties
that they should not allow it unless the absolution came from Rome first, as they promised me to do. […]

[f. 87r] […] that I urged His Most Illustrious Lordship, adapting himself to the necessity of the present times, not to
make a noise about this affair, because he was making himself more hateful to the chief city of the kingdom, and would
again be trying to set the King at odds with that people, who would easily return to their usual tumults without there
being any means to prevent them. The Cardinal showed that it was very hard for him to have to bear this affront, and
although he said he valued my advice highly, it did not seem to me that he was altogether calmed; so, so as not to let
some other trouble arise, [I asked for] the audience of Their Majesties. With His Majesty I complained greatly that,
while grace for the Count of Soissons was awaited from Our Lord, he should have granted those letters. The King
answered me that he meant the letters not to serve until after the absolution had been given to the Count, which he
thought Our Lord would not refuse him, and that after it these were needed to free the said Count from the temporal
penalties he had incurred under his [edicts]; that he was much grieved by what had [f. 87v] happened in Paris, because
in such a matter it [?was not for] the people to oppose his will, and therefore a remedy was needed, so that this did not
become an example. I told His Majesty that, [if] the presentation of his letters had been put off until after the
Count had obtained absolution from Our Lord, this trouble would not have happened; and that the best remedy that could
be found was to do nothing more until an answer came from Rome on the matter of that Count. And His Majesty showing
that he would do it willingly, were he not [afraid] of displeasing the Cardinal of Vendôme, I told him that I begged him
to have more care for his own service than for pleasing others. His Majesty told me that this was right, but begged me
to speak of it myself with the said Cardinal. I said I had done so, but that His Majesty should also, as in matters of the honour of
the Church and in his service, he would not depend on the will of others but govern himself by reason alone. His
