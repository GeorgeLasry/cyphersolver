# R34 — English (Morosini to Montalto, [Blois, November 1588]: Mendoza's offer of a Franco-Spanish union; Saluzzo; Gondi; Orléans)

## Translation

[f. 198r] […] their […], [and] by His Majesty it was felt with very great displeasure, as he knows very well the cause
of this move. It is also held for certain that Monsieur d'Épernon and La Valette his brother are in close dealings to
unite with Monsieur de Guise, which would cause greater suspicion in the King's mind. I do not fail to do all the
offices I can to remove mistrust and to bring in a good and sincere union, which would be much helped if the business
of friendship with Spain, of which I spoke [above], has the good effect that is expected […] Our Lord […] this […]

[f. 199r] […] the ambassador of Spain was at audience the day before yesterday with His Majesty and with the Queen
Mother. She said that, he having many times pressed her to work to bring about good understanding and union between his
King and this one, and considering that she, being mother of the one and of the other and already laden with years,
would not propose such a thing unless it came from the heart and in sincerity of mind, he had been moved to write of it
to his King and to put before him many strong reasons why he should not only lend an ear to this proposal but also
greatly desire to bring it to an end […]; in [answer] he had had that the King of Spain praised highly the [good]
intention of the said Queen and begged her to carry this negotiation forward, offering for his part not to remember
first all the ill satisfactions that had been given him until now; and in proof of this he has given the said Queen a
copy of the very letter his King writes to him, which I have seen and read, and I send a copy of it with this in […]
language. It seems to me that things are going in such a way that this friendship could very easily come about. The
ambassador, although he was with me quite a long time before he went to the audience, and although I have often
[f. 199v] treated with him of this union, as I have already written to Your Most Illustrious Lordship, has [made] me
no sign of it at all; rather it seems he does not want to use anyone else's mediation, which will spare me the effort
and the trouble. Even so I hope to learn from time to time all that will be treated, and I shall send word of it to
Your Most Illustrious Lordship.

What there is beyond the letter is that on Spain's part the restitution of Cambrai is desired, and in exchange to try,
with the [remains] […] armada, which he says has reached Spain, and with some help of men that can be had from here, to
put La Rochelle into the King's hands, which he hopes can be done very [soon] and easily. Further, that Spain be helped
in the affairs of Flanders by means very easy for France and without expense; and conversely, that she will help His
Majesty to drive all the heretics out of France and make him obeyed by his vassals, saying […] that when His Majesty
thinks of enlarging the bounds of his kingdom she will help him to make himself master even of […], and in return […]
she be helped in the enterprise of England. In conclusion it seems to me that so far there is nothing prejudicial to
any Catholic prince, but rather much to the advantage of the Holy Apostolic See, and that a good [f. 200r] resolution
of this most important business can be hoped for. It will be well to keep it most secret, for the reasons that the
singular prudence of Your Most Illustrious Lordship can consider; in which I will not fail to tell you that to see that,
this business having been introduced by Our Lord and pursued here by me with very great zeal, it is […] now treated
without any participation of Our Lord or of me, gives me some suspicion, which will make me more watchful so as to be
able to inform Your Most Illustrious Lordship. Even so, as far as I can, without however mixing in more intimately than
I am asked, I shall not fail to assist the good conclusion of the business, believing it will serve the Catholic
religion and be a great instrument for the extirpation of heresy.

The said ambassador of Spain has spoken with me about the affairs of the marquisate of Saluzzo, much blaming the Duke of
Savoy for having made this move, and assuring me that neither his King nor any of his ministers consented to it or had
a part in it. The same, it seems to me, he told Their Majesties, [f. 200v] who still feel this deed keenly and are
pressing the departure of Signor Girolamo Gondi, which could be next Monday. He will come straight to Rome to do office
with Our Lord, not only about the affairs of Saluzzo but also for the reconciliation of Montmorency and for the rank of
cardinal for the Archbishop of Lyon […]. Of this Signor Gondi I have already written to Your Most Illustrious Lordship
how much he deserves the good grace of Our Lord and of Your Most Illustrious Lordship, since he has always shown himself
most devoted to that Holy See and to Your Most Illustrious Lordship's own house; and I must beg you, for the service of
Holy Church, to make much of him and to show him that you know his good will and the continual services he has always
done and does to Our Lord's ministers, so that he may be the more confirmed in doing the same in future.

Things here, although they go on continuing in peace and union between His Majesty and the Duke of Guise, nonetheless
new occasions for trouble often arise, as now: His Majesty having drawn the governor of Orléans to his side, these
others have stirred up the people and had ambassadors of the city come to demand that the citadel be put into the
hands […]
