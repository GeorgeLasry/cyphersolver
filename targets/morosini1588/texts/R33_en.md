# R33 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, [Oct–Nov 1588]: the clergy and the publication of the Council of Trent)

Translation of `R33_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 189r] […] His Holiness, since I have continually pressed, according to the orders His Holiness was pleased to give
[me] about the publication of the Council of Trent, that His Majesty should without further delay carry out what he had
promised in the articles of the agreement lately made at Paris with these lords of Guise — namely that the said Council
be received […] freely, as has been done in the other kingdoms and provinces of Christians — […] His Majesty decided to
call to him some prelates and other doctors who are here assembled for the Estates, to whom he made known the
pressure I was applying for the publication of the said Council, and […] and the [d]esire she [His Majesty] had not only
to give satisfaction to His Holiness but also to do this good to his kingdom, which greatly needed a good reform;
therefore they should meet together to find a way to make this publication without diminishing his authority. The
prelates took time to communicate this business to their congregation of the clergy, where, when it was proposed —
notwithstanding the offices I had first made in private and the promises made to me — there were many disputes and
much division; because, although all in general praised the publication of the Council, nevertheless [f. 189v] each
one, coming down to consider his own particular interest, found great and unbearable difficulties. First, the chapters
said they did not want to lose their privileges of being [exempt] from their bishops; and because these are more than
eighty in number and the bishops no more than twenty, [and] to try to appease them, many of those bishops offered to
ask His Holiness, jointly with the chapters, to preserve their exemptions. Others will not hear of it, saying it was an
impertinent thing that the bishop could not correct the vices and abuses of his canons, and that, [this] disorder
[continuing], no fruit at all could be expected from the said Council. Others complained of the plurality and
incompatibility of the benefices they [hold]; others, for this pretended liberty […] of the Gallican Church, which
they want, as a point of honour and to have this superiority [among] all [the] other Christian nations, to [maintain]
obstinately. So, after long disputes and after bringing the nobility and the Third Estate round to their will, they
went back to the King and told His Majesty that all together they thanked him for being willing to publish the
Council, but since it would take [f. 190r] at least [two years] to set out the interests of the kingdom, they begged
him to publish it with the reservation of only four exceptions: first, to reserve his authority; second, the liberty of
the Gallican Church; third, the exemptions of the chapters; and last, the particular privileges that various persons
had obtained to hold several benefices together.

Being informed of this, after complaining gravely and privately to several of all the Estates about so exorbitant an
answer, I went to an audience with His Majesty and made with him the most […] representation I could to persuade him
to keep his promise and not listen to those who, [in]stigated by the devil, wanted to divert him from this holy and
necessary publication, and that in any case it must be done without any exception: because it had never been heard in
the Church of God that a general Council, legitimately [assembled] and concluded, was received with conditions and
exceptions; and they could not deny that the Council of Trent was general and legitimately assembled (literally "could
not deny, and the Council of Trent not be general", the period's double negative), since
their own prelates were there in very good number, and especially the Lord Cardinal of Lorraine of good memory, and
the very [f. 190v] ambassadors of [His Majesty], who all together had signed and approved it; and therefore they
should consider whether what had been resolved, with the consent and opinion of a great number of most learned and
pious fathers of all the nations of Christendom and with the assistance of the Holy Spirit, and confirmed by the Holy
Apostolic See, should now be limited and corrected by those who have no authority at all; that this was a thing too
indecent, and would greatly displease His Holiness; begging [him] to consider that, since he holds the title of Most
Christian, he should not have delayed so long in publishing it, to the scandal of all the rest of Christendom, but that
it would have become him to have given the example to the others, and that I feared this delay had provoked the wrath
of God upon this kingdom. The King answered that he desired as much as I did that the [?Council] [be] published and
observed; but that […] it should be considered that the Gallican Church had for many hundreds of years, for the merits
of the services done to the Catholic Church, enjoyed privileges and exemptions not granted to other [f. 191r] states or
empires, and that it was not right that they should be lost, since the Kings his predecessors, who have always been not
only Catholic but even saints, had always chosen to preserve them, and that for this they were never thought less
Catholic and pious than any other king of Christendom. I told His Majesty that I found in the Apostles' Creed only one
Church, and therefore the Gallican Church had to be one with the Roman, otherwise there would be several churches,
against what we profess to believe, and against what His Majesty himself, both in the Edict of Union and in his speech
to the Estates, had many times protested and promised: to want no other religion in his kingdom than the one taught by
the Catholic [apostolic] Roman Church, to which he must have recourse if he wants some grace or privilege, because as a
loving mother she will not fail to reserve and grant anew privileges and graces to [France]; and therefore the Council
must be published without any other condition, in the same way as all the rest of Christendom has done, and then he
should send to His Holiness the reservations he wants, because I was sure that in all honest things he would be
satisfied by His Holiness. In this I made the most vigorous [f. 191v] and effective pressure I could, since in His
Holiness's instructions it is said that without this condition no agreement is to be reached. His Majesty told me that
he [could not] disregard his authority, nor [against] the consent of all the Estates of his kingdom make a resolution
that could cause some great trouble, with ruin and destruction of his affairs; and so he begged me to have a little
patience, because within two or three days he would have me called and have his Keeper of the Seals tell me, in his
presence, all that he could do, and that he greatly wished to be able to give me satisfaction, both out of respect for
His Holiness and for my own particular contentment. I told His Majesty that I claimed no other contentment than the
honour of the Lord God and the good of this kingdom; with which I left, and since then I have not failed to deal with
the same with all those I thought could help this business. [But] I have been told by someone that I was getting too
heated, and that His Holiness did not take it this way, because he had ordered the ambassador of France resident there,
when at the beginning the publication of the Council was asked for in the articles proposed by those of the League,
[f. 192r] to write to his King not to worry about it, because he did not intend the Council to be published by force,
but by His Majesty's good will; and that I therefore ought not to do more than His Holiness wanted. I answered that I
knew very well what my orders were, and that if the King believed this was my own motive he could write about it to His
Holiness, because if I had exceeded my commission I would have received the reprimand I deserved. This is what has been
done so far, from which Your Most Illustrious Lordship can see that it will be very difficult, and perhaps impossible,
to win this point, that the Council be received without any condition, since neither the King nor the Estates will
hear of it, and those who ought most to work for it, namely those of the clergy, make [more] difficulty than the
others. Therefore I beg Your Most Illustrious Lordship to do me the favour of ordering me distinctly and decisively what
I am to do, without leaving anything to my judgment, because I confess I would not know what course to take, it seeming
to me very reasonable that the Council be published and freely received, and on the other hand very dangerous if they
persisted in their first intention to proceed to some […] [f. 192v] […] general congregation of the Estates of the whole
kingdom. So I shall go on with the same offices, trying by skilful means to gain more than […] on this point, and […]
can be carried, I shall temporise until I can have light from Your Most Illustrious Lordship on [what] I am to do to
satisfy fully […] the will and desire of His Holiness. […]
