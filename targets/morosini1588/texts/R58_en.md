# R58 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Moulins, 5 July 1589: Jargeau taken, the King's march on Paris)

Translation of `R58_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 533r] […] this […] of things here, I shall not fail to tell you what is heard, although one can learn the truth only
with great difficulty, since everyone speaks according to his own feeling. It is heard that the King has taken Jargeau,
those inside having waited for the cannon before being willing to surrender; and because the place is very weak,
though important for having a stone bridge over the river Loire and for being only four leagues from Orléans, they say
His Majesty would accept no conditions from them, only [surrender] freely at his discretion; nevertheless he saved it
from the sack, not without much difficulty and the displeasure of the soldiers, who, not being paid, hoped at least to
gain something on that occasion. [Having entered] […] with the principal knights who are with him, His Majesty has had a
captain hanged who was accused of having persuaded the people not to surrender, and had a suburb outside burned, to
take away the convenience of quarters from the army; besides, this captain had shortly before received money to raise
men in the King's service, and nevertheless, after receiving it, had turned to the League's side. His Majesty had also
condemned the governor of that town to the same penalty; but when he had been brought to the place of execution, and
several lords had interceded for him, saying he was a good soldier and would in future serve His Majesty, […] pardoned
him; but he is still kept prisoner. His Majesty has also had some of the principal men of the town jailed to get money.
It is heard that those of Gien, a town ten leagues from Jargeau, where there is another stone bridge over the Loire,
seeing the King approaching, had sent to ask help of the Lord Duke of Mayenne; but not having had it, without waiting
for the army they had sent to surrender to His Majesty, as almost all the other towns round about do, which cannot
[f. 533v] hold out long; and he had received them kindly. At the same time they say that Navarre had taken Pithiviers by
a ruse, and that His Majesty had set out there; he now finds himself master of all the bridges on the Loire, Orléans
and Nantes apart; and they say he is resolved to go straight towards Paris. It is said that his Swiss are also on the
way there, who are coming to meet him again; to whom His Majesty had sent the Duke of Longueville in place of Monsieur
de la Guiche, who had first been appointed to this charge and had already set out with a good company; but two days
later he had letters [from the] King ordering him to return to these parts of the Bourbonnais, saying that he would
soon send him men to make war in Auvergne against those of the League. It is said from the other side that the Duke of
Mayenne had retaken Montereau and also castle […], and that he was on his way to fight the King's Swiss before they
could join His Majesty, and that Monsieur de Nemours was making every effort at Lyon to get money to give to the Swiss
coming in the League's service, of whom they say eleven thousand have reached Chambéry, all good troops, who will not
go further unless they are given money; but that as soon as [they have it] they will set out through Burgundy on the
tail of the other, heretic, Swiss to fight them. The King's supporters say that His Majesty has, without the Swiss and
reiters, 30,000 men in being, and that the Lord Duke of Mayenne has very few troops; and the League's supporters say
that he has more than 25,000 men and the King not six thousand, and that the heretic Swiss, who others say […] well
ahead, have not yet found a way to enter France. So I would not know what to affirm to Your Most Illustrious Lordship,
although I [f. 534r] have spoken with one of the Duke of Nevers's men, who tells me he himself left the said Swiss at
Langres many days ago; but he too might be saying what is false. At this moment Monsieur de Forges has come to see me,
who left Pithiviers last Wednesday, [where] he says the King had also left the same day for Étampes, having had word
from Navarre that the suburbs of that town were all for His Majesty and that they had already won the ditch and
planted the artillery, so that they hoped to have the town very soon. He also says that four of the suburbs of Paris,
namely Saint-Marceau, Saint-Victor, Saint-Jacques and Saint-Germain, had let His Majesty know that they held for him,
and that if he wished to go that way they would receive him very gladly; and that for this reason, as soon as the
enterprise of Étampes is finished, without waiting longer for the foreigners, the King will march that way with his
army, it being understood that the Duke of Mayenne was at Meaux, somewhat ill. He reports further that the Count of
Soissons, who was a prisoner in the castle of Nantes, had escaped by putting himself in a basket in which they used to
carry his food into the castle: a thing very hard to believe. I did not want to fail to tell Your Most Illustrious
Lordship what this monsieur has reported to me, although I give him little credit, being a very partial servant of the
King. [?Now] certain [news] coming from the court, having brought me letters from Signor Galeazzo Fregoso, and he
referring for the news to what I shall hear from the said monsieur, it did not seem to me I should fail to give an
account of it to Your Most Illustrious Lordship, who will give it the consideration your singular prudence thinks fit.
