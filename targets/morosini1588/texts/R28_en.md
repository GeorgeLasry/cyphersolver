# R28 — English (Morosini to Montalto, Blois, [8 October 1588]: the garden talk with Henri III on Montmorency and Guise)

Translation of `R28_reading.md`; holes as there.

## Translation

[f. 142r] […] before I made myself […], I found myself one morning walking in an avenue of this garden of His Majesty,
as I often do, where the Queen Mother having come upon me, I started to walk and talk with her; but His Majesty coming
up shortly after, accompanied by the Cardinals of Bourbon and Vendôme, [?by] Gondi and much nobility, I withdrew with
the said cardinals, leaving the King to talk with the Queen his mother. But they called me at once and took me between
the two of them, and walking in that way the King began to tell me that I had several times, also in the name of His
Holiness, urged him to try to draw Marshal Montmorency to himself, and that in this he recognised the good will of His
Holiness towards him; and therefore he had resolved to send him a gentleman — he said it would be Signor Girolamo
Gondi — to set before His Holiness everything he intends to do for the benefit [and] [?satis]faction of the said
Montmorency, in order to [with]draw him from the friendship of the former King of Navarre and the other heretics and
bring him into his service; and because, His Holiness recognising [f. 142v] not only as reasonable but also as
important all the securities he intends to give the said Montmorency, [?…] she might with her authority interpose
herself to bring this business to a good end, he wished to hear my opinion; but that above all […] it should remain
most secret, because if it became known [?there would not be lacking] many who would try to disturb this
reconciliation. I told him that, as to secrecy, I would not speak of it to anyone; but that to give my opinion on this
particular seemed superfluous to me, since many times and with lively reasons I had urged His Majesty to do everything
to draw that lord away from the friendship of the Huguenots, and that I held it for certain that His Holiness, who has
no other aim than the universal good of Christendom and the union of the Catholics for the destruction of heresy, could
have no news dearer to him than to learn His Majesty's good will in this matter, and that I believed he would not fail
to interpose his authority to bring it to the desired end; praising [f. 143r] highly the person whom His Majesty
intends to send to Rome, because he is truly most worthy of any office, and very devoted to and deserving of that Holy
See, having at all times served [?with] all his heart all the legates, nuncios and others sent by the Apostolic See to
this court.

Then, having come to speak of Monsieur de Guise, I made a most earnest representation to persuade His Majesty to take
him into his confidence and remove from him every cause of suspicion that she [His Majesty] might bear him some ill
will, because otherwise I foresaw matters returning to the worst condition; and I put it to him that if Monsieur de
Guise, without doing any other harm, left the court one morning with those of his faction, there would be a rising
again throughout this kingdom, and that she [His Majesty] would not [?consider herself] safe in Blois, and that, leaving,
she could not be sure of withdrawing to any place where she would be willingly received; [?they do not] fear Monsieur
de Guise more than His Majesty, but fear more for themselves than for the others; and they know very [f. 143v] well,
and in time of war, when one must have soldiers and guards about, that they suffer [?as much] harm from the one side as
from the other. His Majesty told me that what I said was very true, and that for her part she was trying to give every
satisfaction to Monsieur de Guise and to assure him in every way of her good will, and that she could do no more, that
she wanted to be reminded of anything that would make him […]. I said that His Majesty had no need of my reminder, but
that I would nevertheless not fail to tell her that in my opinion two things would greatly help to assure the said
Guise of His Majesty's good will: the first was that His Majesty should truly have this good will in his heart, and the
other that he should take care to show it also by deeds; that Monsieur de Guise was loaded with debts and yet went on
spending more than his income, and that His Majesty, who was most liberal with others, should use this liberality with
him too, because I was persuaded that the money would be very well spent, and [it would assure] [f. 144r] the said
Guise of His Majesty's good will; and so, remedying his need, they would not allow him to think of other ways of
providing for his necessities. The King thanked me much for this advice, and told me that he wanted to do it by all
means, but that he was sorry not to have at present the means to do it in deeds, since he could not be in greater
straits for money than he presently is; but that he […] would give him such intentions as he could be assured of. And I
have since learned that His Majesty on the same day sent Monsieur d'O to tell him that he wished to assign him, on next
year's revenues, a gift of 200,000 scudi; […] he would not accept it, saying that His Majesty now had too much need of
money, and therefore he reserved himself to accept such favours when matters were in a better condition. All the same,
it seems that since then he has […] dealt with His Majesty much more openly and with much [f. 144v] satisfaction on both
sides. But the enemy of human nature wills that, both about His Majesty and about the Duke of Guise, there are persons
who attend to nothing else than feeding mistrust and [?sowing] discord. I do not fail on every side to do all I can to
bring about […] the good [?understanding], [from which] one can hope for the quiet of this kingdom and the rooting out
of heresy; and to this end I would think it very much to the purpose that His Holiness should be content to grant the
absolution to the Count of Soissons in the form in which he has already been content to do it, deferring however to the
singular prudence of His Holiness and of Your Most Illustrious Lordship.

Now I am told by the Lord Cardinal Gondi that the most illustrious [Cardinal] of Vendôme has told him he has heard that
the Lord Cardinal of Joyeuse, and not the one of Sens, was the author of the bad offices done with His Holiness against
the Count of Soissons his brother, and that in the consistory, at His Holiness's chair, they had spoken against the
Lord Cardinals Alessandrino and Santi [f. 145r] Quattro, but that the same Joyeuse had been in His Holiness's rooms to
do the same; which will serve as a notice to Your Most Illustrious Lordship. […]
