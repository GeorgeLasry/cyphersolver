# L7, Louis XIV to Catinat, Fontainebleau, 14 September 1691: English translation

LOUIS. 14 September 1691. M. Catinat, I had a very full account given me at once by the Sieur de [?Chamlay] of what he
did by [?my] order at my army in Italy, and of the conferences you had together on the present state of my affairs in
that country. I saw with regret that the result of the report he made me and of your conversations, after examining
everything thoroughly, came down to withdrawing my army from Piedmont at the end of the campaign and making it recross
the mountains, for want of enough quarters and subsistence to maintain it entire [?over there] during the winter, or at
least a part considerable and numerous enough to be able even to hold out against the enemy's efforts.

I saw at the same time that, according to your opinion and to the description the said Sieur de Chamlay gave me of the
state in which Carmagnola now stands — the little likelihood there is of completing its fortifications before winter,
the ease the enemy would have in attacking it, the difficulties there would be in bringing in everything necessary for
the subsistence of a garrison such as it ought to be, of establishing one there that could hold out and make a good
defence, and finally the risk there would be of exposing the said garrison and those who compose it to being made
prisoners of war because of the weakness of the place if the enemy came to master it — all these [?difficulties] showed
me that, according to the rules of prudence, Carmagnola should be abandoned at the same time as my army left Piedmont;
and that, moreover, given the difficulty of maintaining it beyond the mountains except at immense expense, and of making
any progress there before so great a number of troops as the enemy's, who have as many advantages and conveniences for
making war there as mine lack, it seemed more suitable for the good of my service to decide not to make them recross the
mountains next campaign, and to confine ourselves to a defensive war which, [?] my places and my frontier provinces
towards Piedmont, will prevent the enemy from penetrating the Alps to enter France and Savoy, and will reduce them to
wasting themselves uselessly in their own country without being able to make any progress in mine.

The account of everything stated above having been given me as fully as I could wish, I examined with much calm [and]
application the harm that this [?course] could do to my affairs in Italy: making the enemy absolute masters of that
country; exposing my allies and the neutral princes and states to the necessity of declaring for them, or at least to an
extreme diffi[346?]culty in holding out against them; losing Casale from sight for ever and putting it, so to speak, into
the enemy's [ha]nds; in a word, compromising the reputation of my arms with my other enemies, and even with my [?]
allies, by giving them an idea, false at bottom but clothed with some appearance, of my weakness and impotence in
[?Italy], which will raise their courage and engage them, in the hope of better fortune later, to pursue the war more
vigorously than before. I examined with care all these drawbacks and all these reasons, which must count for much
because they tend to maintain or to impair the reputation of my arms.

I reflected [?at the] same time on the difficulties of maintaining my army in Piedmont, of holding out there when one is
not master of Turin and is dealing with a powerful enemy; the impossibility of making any progress there; the difficulty
of making the necessary and timely movements there, because of the lie of the country, which is very narrow and
extremely enclosed, [?and] cut by rivers and torrents liable to swell and overflow in a short time, and then not to
become passable for several days, for want of being able to [?] make bridges of boats there because of [?their]
swiftness and their breadth, and of having fixed bridges at one's disposal; the little ease of relieving Casale because
of its great distance from my places, the difficulty of marching there for want of [w]ater, and of forcing the enemy in
the posts they can occupy around that place; the exposure to which the climate, the air, the fruit and the corruption of
the waters ordinarily put my troops, of falling sick every campaign without my being able to have them cared for as well
as I would wish, for want of hospitals able to hold them; the convenience the enemy have of engaging my arm[y] when they
please in an [?] action without the thing being reciprocal for me, [?] because of [?] and of the rivers Dora and Po,
which, thanks to the solid bridges they [?have] over them, give them the means of making to their advantage such
movements as suit them.

On the other hand, I examined the arrangement you [?propose] to me for the conduct of the defensive war in question: the
way you think the troops must be [?posted] on my frontiers of Provence, Dauphiné and Savoy, at the principal passes that
lead there from the enemy country, and on the heights of San Pietro near Pinerolo and around Susa, for the protection of
those places and to prevent the enemy from venturing into the mountains to enter my country; the convenience I shall
have of maintaining my troops at less cost and of preserving them from the illnesses that ordinarily afflict them in the
plain of Piedmont; the almost certain assurance I shall have that the enemy will not be able to penetrate the mountains
or undertake anything against my frontiers; the embarrassment [?of] throwing themselves [?into] the enterprise of
Casale, which they will perhaps not dare to form for fear that I may bring my army back into Piedmont, particularly if I
decide to leave on the Saône or in the County of Burgundy for part of the campaign the surplus of my cavalry that is now
in Piedmont and will not be occupied in guarding Savoy, Provence and Dauphiné; the advantage I shall draw from this
cavalry, which I can send, if I see fit, into Germany, either to recall the Germans from Piedmont or to make progress
against them [?in the Empire] if they decide to remain in Italy; the extreme confusion and embarrassment into which I
shall throw Monsieur de Savoie: either to keep the army [?of the allies] that is now [in] Piedmont, to prevent my army
from coming back there, with no surer fruit than the ruin and entire desolation of his states; or, to avoid that ruin,
to send that army back into the Empire, and believe himself by a certain [?] [?to see] my troops come back into his
country, and to see himself [?forced] to recall the Germans to drive them out of it, and to fall back into the abyss from
which he would have wished to escape by sending them back into the Empire.

After all these discussions of the reasons on both sides, I compared the drawbacks and advantages of one course with
those of the other; and finally, after overcoming the delicacy and point of honour I made of having my army winter in
Piedmont, of making it act in that country next year, and of not, by making it recross the mountains, impairing the
reputation of my arms not only in Italy but throughout Europe — following your advice and the account the Sieur de
Chamlay gave me of the ease of guarding [the] mountain passes and of watching over the preservation of Pinerolo and Susa;
as indeed, although I have not seen it, the thing seems likely to me, since after all it must be more difficult for
Monsieur de Savoie to force the passes of the Alps guarded by large bodies of infantry and to penetrate into my country
without having any place of arms there to furnish them their needs, than for me to cross the same mountains, unguarded,
to enter Piedmont, where I have Pinerolo, which is a considerable place for supplying my army's subsistence; and further,
since it is certain that I have trouble sustaining this war, whatever immense expense I make in it and whatever
precautions I take to succeed in it, which are means the Duke of Savoy must lack more than I — I compared the drawbacks
of the one and the advantages of the other, and I have determined to prefer the solid course to the honourable one, that
is, to make my army recross the mountains at the end of the campaign, not to send it back into Piedmont next year, and to
wage the defensi[40?] war in the passes of the Alps and on the heights of Pinerolo and Susa, according to the arrangement
you sent me, which I hope you will rectify further by examining things more closely and at leisure on the spot, and
according to what you judge most suitable for my service.

This course taken, it remains for me now to instruct you in the things that concern the good of my service, that is, to
prolong the campaign as much as you can without ruining my army and without compromising it, and to abandon Carmagnola
only as late as possible. These two conditions are essential for the conduct of the negotiation I am now having carried
on with the Pope and several other princes and states of Italy, whose success would be of infinite advantage to me in
getting me out of the affair of Casale, and perhaps even out of the war in Piedmont. I know all the drawbacks you will
represent to me about abandoning Carmagnola too [?late], and about the difficulty there will be in withdrawing quickly
all the effects I have there; but, [given] the importance of not letting the enemy or the other powers of Italy know that
I mean to leave Piedmont with the plan of not [?returning] there, [I] prefer keeping Carmagnola for some time longer to
the risk [?I run] of losing something there in abandoning it. [?Even] when you are obliged to withdraw, the work on the
fortifications must not even be discontinued; but you may skilfully have it pushed with a little less vigour and expense,
without however revealing to the enemy what is intended; and when I send you word to leave that place — which I am very
sorry cannot be kept, for the reasons given me, that is, the mediocre state of the [?place] and its fortifications, the
need to put much money, munitions and troops into it at the risk of losing them through the ease the enemy will have in
attacking it and making themselves masters of it, and through the difficulty, or rather the impossibility, of being able
to relieve it under the defensive war I am adopting in accordance with your advice — when I send you word to leave that
place, you will do your best to withdraw from it the most considerable effects in the greatest quantity you can. I know
as well as you the difficulty of transport; but you can use the cavalry to carry on the croup to Pinerolo as much wheat
[?] as possible, and the carts will serve for the cannon and the munitions. After all, one will have to be consoled for
what one is obliged to leave there, which one must take care to throw into the water lest the enemy profit by it, as
well as to tear down the fortifications as much as possible, and to burn the palisades, the [?fascines], the gates, the
hay and the grain that remain there.

So I repeat to you once more that it is in my service to keep Carmagnola and to prolong the campaign as long as you can.
I do not fix for you precisely the length of this prolongation; but to do well it ought to extend to All Saints, [?or]
even to Martinmas, which will give me the means of seeing clearly into the negotiation I am having carried on in Italy,
and will let me judge whether there is reason to hope for something from it. Employ all your skill to manage this
length of campaign, and use the grain you have in the places of Piedmont other than Pinerolo to give the cavalry the
means of subsisting more easily.

As it would not be impossible that the enemy might think of bombarding Pinerolo, and as that would cause me much
mortification in the presence of my army, I do not doubt that you will do everything that may depend on you and
everything practicable and reasonable to [?prevent] them; but as they may put off doing it until after you have
recrossed the mountains, and then the thing will be [?], you will take care to give your orders at Pinerolo so that the
munitions of war and of food are put in a place of safety as far as possible. I count at the same time on your
continuing to watch with great application over the preservation of the valleys of Pragelato, [?of Cesana and Oulx] and
of La Perosa, which seem to me of infinite importance for the communication of Briançon with Pinerolo; on your continuing
to give your orders in Savoy so that the enemy can undertake nothing against that province by the Val d'Aosta; as for
the relief of Cuneo, which I judge you should attack at the end of the campaign after you have recrossed the mountains,
this you can easily do despite the rigour of the season, because you will lack nothing in provisions, and, [?having] no
enemy on your hands nor being able to have any, you can put the infantry under cover in the villages near that place;
and finally, that in passing back near Pinerolo and Susa you will examine with great care [the] heights of San Pietro
and around the said Susa, where you plan to establish the troops that are to watch over the safety of those places, and
that you will give me a very exact account of it, so as to set my mind entirely at rest on this part, as you and the
Sieur de Chamlay have already done.

Meanwhile I am going to give the necessary orders to have conveyed diligently to [?] Grenoble, if I see fit, the
artillery and the munitions of war and food, and everything necessary for the siege of Cuneo, all in such abundance that
you will lack [nothing]; I shall also have there in time the number of engineers [?and] other officers you indicate in
your memorandum. At the same time I am going to give orders that work be done diligently on fortifying the towns, posts
and castles of the frontiers of Provence, Dauphiné and Savoy, and above all Briançon, which you judge necessary to set
against the mountain passes, particularly on the side of the valleys of Luserna, San Martino and Angrogna, to prevent the
enemy not only from entering my provinces with a large body, but even from making any raid there with parties. I shall
order munitions of war and food to be carried into those places, and even some pieces of artillery if needed; and I
shall have a magazine of the said munitions made at Grenoble, so as to be able to replace [?at once] those consumed daily
in the advanced posts. In a word, [?I shall arrange] everything so that nothing will be lacking on those frontiers,
whose preservation I know to be of the utmost importance for the good of my service. Likewise I am going to give my
orders for the disposition of the winter quarters, as far as can be done, according to the memorandum you send me and
that you think suitable for [?my] service, and so that the troops lack nothing for their subsistence; on which it seems
to me very difficult to have meat supplied to them, as you and the Sieur Bouchu propose to me, and for which nevertheless
provision will be made as well as possible.

Having explained to you very fully my thoughts and my intentions on the affairs of Italy, it remains for me only to
recommend you once more to prolong the campaign in Piedmont as long as you can without ruining my troops; to keep
Carmagnola until the end without letting the enemy know that one means to leave it; to withdraw from it as many effects
as you can and spoil the rest; to give all the necessary orders at Pinerolo in case the enemy wish to bombard it after
you have recrossed the mountains; [?to] withdraw in such a way and with such precaution, when you [?leave], that the enemy
can undertake nothing against you; to examine once more the heights of Pinerolo and Susa carefully; to reflect well on
the way you propose to wage the defensive war next year, and see what is best to be done to put my frontiers in greater
safety, and above all Savoy, over whose preservation you cannot watch too much, particularly for the rest of this
campaign, because I have not many troops in that province — although the arrangement you have made, and propose for next
year, seems good to me, and probably there is not much to fear on the side of the Val d'Aosta, because of the difficulty
of the mountains to be crossed, which will soon be covered with snow; to recommend to the Sieur de la Hoguette to take
good care that neither provisions nor troops enter Cuneo; and finally, after your withdrawal from Piedmont, [?to finish]
your campaign with the [?taking] of that place, and then to send the troops [?] establi[595!] in the winter quarters.
And this letter being for no other purpose, I pray God, M. Catinat, to keep you in His holy care. Written at
Fontainebleau, 14 September 1691. Signed LOUIS.
