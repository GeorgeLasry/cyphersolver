Ranzo’s code, 1528: the letter to “Garbino” and Ranzo’s letters

An imperial code in which each word is written as its initial and a number; this project could not break it; George Lasry and Paolo Rosson each recovered the key from the ciphertext on 6 October 2026, and with the two keys Ranzo’s letters in fr. 2988 and the copy in fr. 20506 are deciphered to the site’s complete extent.

Method: key recovered from ciphertext-only · Extent: partial (93% of the cipher)

With George Lasry (solution) and Paolo Rosson (earlier solution, verification) and Norbert Biermann (transcription)

Summary. No. 20 of français 3022 is a letter to “Garbino”, Madrid, 11 April 1528, in the code of Hieronimo Ranzo, a kinsman of the Chancellor Gattinara. The same code is used in Ranzo’s signed letters in fr. 2988 and fr. 3019 (DECODE R1894). All 1,315 groups of no. 20 were transcribed here, with about 2,600 more from Ranzo’s own letters, but this project’s solver recovered only function words. Two independent decipherments came on 6 October 2026: Paolo Rosson’s, with Claude, built on this project’s transcription (section 03 measures it), and George Lasry’s, with Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, from Norbert Biermann’s transcription of five letters (section 04). Outcome: key recovered from ciphertext-only, by George Lasry and, independently, by Paolo Rosson; partial. Measured per letter with Lasry’s key first and Rosson’s second (section 04): Ranzo’s two letters in fr. 2988 give sense for 96.2 % and 97.0 % of their groups, over the 95 % line, and fr. 20506 is a copy of the second; no. 20 reads 94.4 %, fr. 4050 87.7 %, fr. 6638 89.0 %; 92.9 % of 6,428 groups in all. In letter 4 (fr. 4050) runs of open codes break most sentences of the middle, so it counts as decoded, not read; in the four letters that read, 4,606 of the 6,428 groups give sense (71.7 %, reading audit, 22 October 2026). On 23 October 2026 letter 4 (Madrid, 10 March 1528) was joined into words by hand from Biermann’s transcription with Lasry’s key, Rosson’s key (which confirms nine of Lasry’s tentative values there and gives three more: e173 express, s72 sara, d46 debia) and the code list’s rough alphabetical order (texts/letter4_reading.md, English letter4_en.md). With 39 code values resting on sense and list position only, about 70 tentative values seen once or twice and 47 code types still open, and one run of eleven codes (f. 79r l. 27 to f. 79v l. 2) that does not build, it stays decoded, not read; the partial reading is kept.

01 The letter to Garbino and Ranzo’s code

No. 20 is an Italian letter, dated at Madrid on 11 April 1528, endorsed on the back to “Garbino”. It alternates clear and code. The clear passages report that the Chancellor was ill with gout, that fifty galleys were being fitted out, that a million ducats had been raised in Castile and 500,000 more were expected from the Cortes of Aragon, and that the Empress was in the seventh month and the prince would be sworn heir. The coded passages, about two thirds of the letter, are what it was sent for.

Bound after the letter are papers of Garbino’s network, not keys to this letter. On f. 48 is a jargon, with more on f. 49: cover letters signed “Antonio de Cosenza” to “Andrea Romano in Valencia”, news disguised as the price of grain, and a list of cover names. On f. 49v the Emperor is “Joan Jacobo”, del Vasto “portolano de Gergenti”, Garbino himself “antonio petra”. On f. 50 is an “Aditione nel zifra”, an addition to a code key.

The “Aditione” is not the key to this letter. It belongs to another code of the same design, an initial letter with a number, and is shown here only to illustrate how such a code is built. Checked against the transcriptions: of its 113 groups, only one (g217, once) occurs anywhere in the 3,912 groups of no. 20 and Ranzo’s letters, and its numbers overlap those the letters already use (it starts the b list at 157; the letters run to b215). No value in the decipherments below was taken from it as a key.

In a code of this design each group is the initial of the word with a number; in the illustrative addition apresso is a327, Cartagenia c327, soa santità s395, and common syllables get groups of their own. The letter to Garbino follows the same design with its own lists: its groups run to a323, b215, c325, p383, s387. The same code is used in the signed letters of Hieronimo Ranzo in BnF fr. 2988 ff. 2 and 9–10 and fr. 3019 f. 73. Ranzo was a Vercelli nobleman at Charles V’s court, of the family of Gattinara’s mother. Norbert Biermann discussed the addition sheet alongside Ranzo’s code in a 2017 Cipherbrain thread; it is of the same design but, as above, not the same key. The Garbino letter is very likely Ranzo’s.

The letter is bound in français 3022 among imperial papers of 1527–28 that reached French hands, next to letters of the Marquis del Vasto; those are in different ciphers and have their own page.

02 No decipherment of the time

The catalogue says “no decipherment noted”, and the obvious place for a French decipherment was checked first. The eighteenth-century Clairambault copies were searched. Clairambault 327 ff. 279–280 copies no. 20 as “Lettre non signée écrite au seigneur Garbino du 11 d’avril 1528 … partie en chiffre, partie non”, with the cipher copied and nothing deciphered. Clairambault 314 f. 337 copies the addition sheet. Neither carries a decipherment, and none turned up in the neighbouring français volumes.

Cipherbrain (Klaus Schmeh, 2016, with comments by Norbert Biermann and Thomas in 2017) found no known nomenclator for Ranzo’s code.

03 Rosson’s key applied to this transcription

On 14 October 2026 a Cryptoole alert led to Paolo Rosson’s page “The Venetian? cipher with superscript digits of 1528” (Cryptoole IT1520-1, listed solved on 6 October 2026 with George Lasry). Working with Claude, from this project’s transcription of no. 20 and of Ranzo’s letters in fr. 2988, he built a key table of 779 values (117 sure, 595 probable, 67 guesses). Numbers 0–5 are nulls, 6–9 spell single letters, 10 and above index a list of stems under the initial. He estimates about 85 % of tokens right, and checked the transcription against the images, finding that in fr. 2988 57 “g” are s and 63 “t” are r (two hands). No palaeographer has checked the reading.

His key and corrections were applied here to the existing transcriptions (measure_rosson.py). The fit is not independent, since the key was built on them.

The decoded text is coherent Italian of 1528. On the last page of Ranzo’s letter, for example, the writer says that fornari has left for Italy with Julian to carry a sum of money for exchange payments to the army; earlier in the same letter he tells the addressee to address letters through Hieronimo Ranzo and to mention nothing of them in his others. The blind controls of his page (87.6 % and 93.4 % on made-up codes) were not rerun here.

The earlier verdict that no. 20 was blocked for lack of key material was therefore wrong in one respect: a key could be built, by a reader who could look at the images and the clear stretches together. This project’s annealer, validated on a control, had stopped at function words.

Both letters of fr. 2988, and so the copy in fr. 20506, clear the 95 % bar: George’s verdict holds. Letters 4 and 5 fall short only because his key, built on letters 1–3, cannot confirm Lasry’s tentative values in them. No. 20 misses the bar by 0.6 point: 27 groups that neither key reads and 47 tentative values the keys do not share.

Both keys were recovered from the ciphertext: Italian cribs, repeated contexts and the structure of the code, with no decipherment, clear copy or key of these letters to start from. The addition sheet of f. 50 that Lasry spot-checked belongs to another code (one of its 113 groups occurs here), so it could not supply values. The entry is therefore classed key recovered from ciphertext-only, by others; this project’s share is the transcription and the measurement.

05 Why our solver could not decode it

An initial-letter code is usually weak for one reason: if each letter’s list is in alphabetical order, the number tells you roughly where in the dictionary the word sits. That was the first thing tested. The distribution of the numbers within each initial was compared with where frequent Italian words fall in an alphabetical list, against shuffled orders. No. 20 scores z = −0.5. The same test run on Castiglione’s letters encoded with a genuinely alphabetical code scores about +6.5. Ranzo’s numbering is not alphabetical, so a number says nothing about its word beyond the initial.

What is left is a word substitution under one constraint, the initial letter, with about 550 group types in no. 20 and 730 in Ranzo’s own letters. All 1,315 groups of no. 20 were transcribed from full-resolution scans, and about 2,600 more from the six Ranzo pages. The two share 316 group types, which confirms the key is one table. An annealer then assigned each group a word with the right initial, treating the z groups as nulls, and scored the result with a word-bigram model built from Castiglione’s Lettere (diplomatic Italian written from Spain in these same years) and Guicciardini. It was first run on a control: 3,900 words of Castiglione held out of the model and encoded the same way.

That is the limit of the method, and the control shows it. Frequent words come out, because their contexts fix them: c170 che, i100 il, p149 per, n38 non, r41 re, s233 sua, m170 mi, p246 più. The words that carry the news occur once or twice each and do not. Nor was there a crib to be had. No Ranzo page repeats a passage of no. 20 (the longest shared run is a stock three-group phrase), the copy marked “dup.ª” duplicates another Ranzo letter rather than this one, and the Clairambault copy carries no decipherment. The other “pièces en chiffre” of fr. 2988 are a different, French, symbol cipher.

06 What would finish it

The code is open at the margin. Ranzo’s letters in fr. 2988 are deciphered; what remains is no. 20, 0.6 point short of the bar, where both keys’ readings should be compared with the images page by page, and fr. 4050 and fr. 6638, which this project has not seen and where Lasry’s tentative values have no second key to confirm them. Ranzo’s own base table would settle the rest: any of his letters that was deciphered at the time or survives in clear. The places to look are Gattinara’s papers in the Archivio di Stato di Vercelli, the imperial correspondence in Simancas and Vienna, and the other side of the Garbino network. The transcriptions and the solver are ready for a key or a crib; with even a few hundred words of known plaintext the substitution would close quickly.
