Siena’s cipher letters, Concistoro 2308: the unread pieces

A never-studied bundle of 25 ciphered pieces sent to the Sienese government, each in its own key; eight letters are now deciphered on their own pages, and this page follows the ones that still resist.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 25 September 2026 · updated 20 October 2026

Method: not solved · Extent: text not obtained

17 October 2026 update: George Lasry has contributed partial decipherments, translations and a visual working key for nos. 20 and 23 (R4808/R4811). They share a cipher and now have a separate page, so they are no longer counted here. The earlier attempts on them are kept in the repository under targets/siena2023/project_attempt/.

18–20 October 2026 update: George Lasry sent solutions of most of the remaining pieces: the unnumbered slip R4794 and nos. 9, 11, 12, 13, 15 (in part) and 17, together with his independent solution of no. 18, which he dates to Pisa, 1496. His keys were run here on our own transcriptions, made before his work: they give his readings of nos. 11, 17 and R4794 at 93–94% (below). The slip of no. 8 was also aligned with the memorandum above it: the memorandum is its decipherment.

At a glanceNot solved by this project · most pieces since deciphered by George Lasry and checked; nos. 7, 19, 21 unread by anyone

The documents
The bundle of cipher letters of the Concistoro of Siena: 25 pieces photographed, from before 1429 to 1547, with 43 key sheets filed beside them. Each letter has its own key.
Where
Archivio di Stato di Siena, Concistoro 2308, fasc. 2 “Lettere in cifra”. On DECODE as records R4790–R4814 (letters) and R4746–R4789 (keys), all catalogued “1500–1599” with no status.
Deciphered elsewhere
No. 25 (96.2%), no. 18 (87.1%), no. 14 (58.6%) and no. 4 (79%) here; nos. 6 and 24 by George Lasry with filed key R4755; nos. 20 and 23 by George Lasry, in part, with a key rebuilt from the ciphertext. Each is a different cipher, with its own page.
On this page
Nos. 7, 9, 11, 15, 17, 19, 21 and an unnumbered slip, which this project could not read, with no shared key claimed; George Lasry’s October solutions of most of them, checked here; and Meister’s no. 1 of 1421, not among the photographs.
How they were tried
Every filed key compared with every letter, ten tested sign by sign; homophonic and null-aware solvers on the longer pieces.
Still open
Nos. 7, 19 and 21, most of no. 15, and the number and name codes in the solved pieces. The 1421 letter needs the archive.

Until 14 October 2026 this page carried the whole bundle. Under the rule of one key per page, the letters whose keys were found now have their own pages (above); this page keeps the bundle’s history, its keys, and the pieces still unread.

01The bundle, and where it was found

Aloys Meister saw the bundle in 1902 as “Lettere in cifra e loro chiavi”, in two fascicles: Cifrari, 42 original keys, 18 of them of the fifteenth century and the oldest dated 1433; and Lettere in cifra, 29 pieces, “dated no. 1 1421; no. 4 1456; no. 11 1478; no. 17 1528”. Giovanni Cecchini’s printed inventory of the Concistoro (1952) lists it as no. 2308, “Cifrari e lettere cifrate”, and says the letters are only partly deciphered. The Ilardi microfilm at Yale (reel 1503) put only the first thirteen frames online, all of them keys, so the letters were taken to be out of reach.

They are on DECODE. The whole busta was photographed in the Siena reading room: the keys are records R4746–R4789 (Concistoro_2308_01 to _43), the letters R4790–R4814 (_44 to _68), behind a cover sheet reading “Fasc. 2, Pezzi 25”. DECODE dates every letter “1500–1599” and gives none a status; the dates and identifications below come from the letters themselves. The pencil numbers on the pieces match Meister’s dated items, so they are his numbering. What the photographs hold:

02The 43 keys

The key fascicle is a fine run of Sienese chancery ciphers: a key of 1433 for Antonio Petrucci (Meister’s example), the “Cifra cum fratre Bernardino” of the time of the antipope Felix, the key of the ambassadors at Milan of 1454, a key for the podestà of Casole, Antonio Bichi’s of 1478, keys of the Borgia years naming Duke Valentino and Pandolfo Petrucci, a letter-key dated 4 August 1502, the 1536 key, and code books of the 1540s. Their alphabets were compared by eye with the sign sets of every letter. Six fifteenth-century keys (R4750, R4753, R4754, R4756, R4757, R4762) were transcribed in full and tested sign by sign on nos. 4 and 7, and four later ones (R4760, R4764, R4768, R4778) on nos. 15, 17 and 20.

No filed key fits any of the pieces on this page. The nearest miss: no. 7, from an orator at Milan in the 1450s, looked made for the 1454 Milan key (R4750), but the letter’s own interlinear glosses give values that contradict it, a fifth of its signs are not in the key, and the key’s only sign for e is 2.5% of the letter. One filed key was missed: R4755, “Cifra cum Christofano”, was indexed here but never tried on nos. 6 and 24; George Lasry found that it fits them.

03George Lasry’s solutions, checked here

On 18 October 2026 George Lasry sent a page with keys, crops and excerpts for most of the pieces this project had left unread, solved in October 2026 with CTTS and with LLMs (Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra), ciphertext-only except where a contemporary decipherment existed. His numbering is the pencil numbering, except that his “no. 5” is the unnumbered slip R4794. In brief: R4794 is about pieces of artillery, with four number codes; no. 9 about Castiglione “this winter” with the King of Aragon’s assent; no. 11 has Forlì and Faenza sending two squadrons each and Giovan Francesco Gonzaga offering 80 men-at-arms for 7,000 ducats; no. 12 is a marriage offer to Sforza Attendolo from Naples, 10 March 1413; no. 13 is deciphered with its clear twin no. 16; no. 15 is deciphered in part (75 of 240 signs); no. 17 is a 1528 letter in the cipher of the Balìa about bishoprics, the Cardinal of Naples and Pitigliano.

His key tables are pictures of the signs cut from the photographs. Each of his signs was matched by shape and count to the sign names of our own transcriptions of 24 September, made before his work; our transcriptions were then deciphered with his keys, with no fitting, and lined up with his readings:

04The pieces still unread

  • No. 1 (1421), nos. 2, 26, 27, 28: not among the DECODE photographs. Only the Archivio di Stato di Siena can supply them; the 1421 date, which would be older than the project’s oldest reading, stays untested.
  • Nos. 7, 19 and 21 (363, 118 and 107 signs): no filed key fits and nobody has read them; no. 7 has nine values from its gloss.
  • No. 15: the 165 signs George Lasry left unread, with Latin-letter code words; the candidate key R4764 (Buoninsegni, orator to His Majesty) does not match.
  • The number and name codes in the solved pieces: 26, 44, 67, 74 in R4794, the sender and name signs of no. 12, “el Mustetto” and the “Otto” of no. 17. No key for them is filed.

These pieces are listed together only because they are in one bundle; each may use its own key. Before George Lasry’s results this page called pieces of 165–282 signs too short to break; he broke them, so “too short” now stands only where he failed too.

Nos. 20 and 23, listed here until 17 October 2026, are on their own page.

05The pieces deciphered at the time

Four pieces carry their own contemporary decipherment; they are other keys, not results of this work, and are noted for completeness.

  • No. 8 (c. 1440–47): a slip pasted below a clear memorandum that mentions the antipope Felix, the Duke of Milan, Bianca Maria and Leonello d’Este, and Niccolò Piccinino. George Lasry thought the link between the two “possible but speculative”; it holds. The slip is the end of a letter, clear words mixed with cipher, and its clear words (“Anchora disse”, “antiveduto questo”) are the memorandum’s underlined heads. Three cipher runs match the memorandum letter by letter, with the same values throughout: aveva dato la donna biancha, el marchese di ferrara (the seven signs of marchese recur in a second line), e non può manchare che.
  • No. 10: one run glossed letter by letter “presura di Montepulciano”.
  • Nos. 13 and 16 (1527–29): no. 16 is the clear decipherment of no. 13, on the Pope’s coming to Bologna and the taking of Modena and Reggio.
  • No. 22 (1455–58): four drafts of one letter, about signal fires, spingarde and “la lettera di Calisto”; a clear draft pairs with one where numbers stand for key words (4 = L., 7 = denari, 15 = parti, 16 = accordo, 17 = speranza).

06Method

Every piece was transcribed from the DECODE photographs by line crops at three to six times native size, sign by sign, with a named inventory of shapes. The solver is a homophonic annealing search (a sign may stand for any letter, several signs for one letter), scored by an Italian or Latin n-gram model of the period and a letter-frequency prior, run in stages from trigrams to five-grams with restarts seeded by frequency; where the scribe divides words, the model scores the spaces too. The same program recovers a synthetic Italian homophonic cipher of 2,673 signs and 58 symbols perfectly, and it is the program that gave the keys of nos. 14, 18 and 25.
