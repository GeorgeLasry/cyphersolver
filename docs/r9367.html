A son to his father, 22 March 1535

A young man writes home that he was questioned and denied everything; the dangerous lines are in a key copied in the Bavarian chancery’s key register, and about half of them are deciphered.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 15 October 2026

Method: read with known key · Extent: partial (55% of the cipher)

At a glanceKnown key applied · partial · about half of the cipher words

The letter
A son to his father (“gunstiger herr vater”), Monday after Palm Sunday, 22 March 1535, in German; the father and the place are not named in the clear text.
Where
Bayerisches Hauptstaatsarchiv, Munich, Kurbayern Äußeres Archiv 4591 f. 169; DECODE R9367, part of catalogue entry 161, listed with no decipherment.
The cipher
Runs of invented signs inside the clear German: one sign per letter, a few signs for words (und, auch, das) and for ch.
How it was deciphered
R9423, a register in the same volume copying out nineteen keys of the 1520s and 1530s, has a block headed “H. Wilh. [15]25”; once its frequent signs were matched to the letter’s by shape, it gives German. Five values were added from the letter itself.
What it says
He was asked about “seltsam khinst”, strange arts, and denied everything; he is taken for a poor writer and has learned nothing reliable yet; he fears for his person; the Turks are coming up.
Still open
About half of the cipher words: two signs that are not in the key block, and six lines that do not yet give sense.

Until 15 October 2026 this letter was filed on one page with thirteen other ciphertexts of the same key volume. They are different ciphers, each with its own page; the largest group is System A′ of the dukes’ agents in Hungary.

01 The letter

The clear German opens with the date, “Anno dni. 1535 den 22 marcij an montag nach palmarum”, and asks the father’s pardon. The ciphered passages say that he was questioned about strange matters (“seltsam khinst gefragt”) and denied everything (“aber a[ll]es verlaugnet”); that he is taken for a poor writer (“schlechter schreiber”) and was sent to learn because he is still young; that he has learnt nothing reliable yet (“noch nicht freintlichs erfaren”); that he fears for his person (“meines leib”); and that the Turks are coming up (“Tirckhen herauf ziechen”). A name reads like Nothaft. The postscript names a Friedrich and “Schmalcz” (perhaps Schmalkalden): “euch nicht guets”, nothing good for you. Who the son was, and why his letter was kept among the duke’s keys, is not known.

02 The key

Block 2_4 of the key register R9423, headed “H. Wilh. [15]25”, gives A 9, B v, C Δ, D +, E 6, F ⊥, G o, H 4, I w, K ‡, L 8, M ¥, N 3, O a barred D, P two rings, Q 2, R 7, S y, T ♉, V a doubled ‡, X o-o, Y ε, Z π, with und 9, auch Z, ch t, das T. Applied with the original sign assignments it failed; it fits once the letter’s frequent signs are matched to the block by shape. The block’s name signs (for Bavaria and Duke Ludwig) do not fit the letter: here they behave as nulls or word dividers. The letter adds five values of its own (q a, Λ r, C s, H k, K z).

03 What stays open

  • Of the words in the cipher runs, 78 of 143 are deciphered (54.5%, an upper bound: some counted words come from a working reading). The runs on lines 1–2, 7, 9–10 and 13 give no sense yet.
  • Two signs are not in the key block: a triple-barred cross (13 times) and a circle sign; the context does not fix them.
  • The back of the letter, with the address, was not examined in the first session; it may name the father.

The cipher runs are read in part with key block 2_4 of the register R9423 (# and O open).

Summary: A young man (still "jung", sent to learn) writes to his father on Monday after Palm Sunday 1535 from a
court or journey. He says he has been questioned about strange things ("seltsam künste gefragt") and denied
everything, that he is held a poor writer, that he has not yet learned anything friendly/reliable, and that he
carries great worry for his own body/safety. In cipher he reports news: the Turks are to come up ("Tirckhen herauf
ziechen"), that he was asked by some people, a name like Nothaft, and in the postscript something about (Count
Palatine?) Friedrich and Schmalkalden(?) and "euch nicht guets" (nothing good for you). The father is told he will
be informed more fully in the next letter.
