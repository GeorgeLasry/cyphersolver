The comte d’Affry’s intercepted letters, 1757–58

Koninklijk Huisarchief, The Hague, Prins Willem V, inv. 192: code letters of the French ambassador to the foreign secretaries Rouillé and Bernis, opened by the Dutch post and copied for the stadholder’s cabinet. DECODE lists ten of them, all in one code, as not decrypted: no decipherment was imaged with them. Clear copies of all ten turned out to be in the Fagel registers of the Nationaal Archief (found by WAINUT after the decryption).

The Dutch codebreaker Pierre Lyonet’s decipherments of eight sibling letters are among the images, filed as ordinary clear pages. The key was recovered by aligning them group by group with their cipher, and applied to the letters with no decipherment on DECODE; 89% of the groups decrypted as sense from that key alone, 94% after values checked against clear copies found later in the Fagel registers.

Summary. DECODE lists eleven d’Affry letters of 1757–58 in the Dutch royal archives as not decrypted, with a note that the siblings’ solutions were “written on separate sheets”, not imaged. They were imaged: eight clear pages marked by the Dutch decipherer’s clerk, which look like d’Affry’s own clear letters, are Lyonet’s decipherments of the cipher beside them. Transcribed from the scans and aligned group by group (7 letters, about 3,000 groups), they give a key of 651 groups. It is one code for the whole year, to Rouillé and then to Bernis. With it nine letters that have no decipherment on DECODE decrypt at 60–95% each: the navy and the convoys, the Gouvernante’s tears («faut-il que ce soit moi qui favorise les moyens de faire du mal à mon père?»), Ostend and Nieuport, spies sent to England, a French loan tried in London. The decryption was checked against print: Bussemaker’s 1906 extracts of 19 and 22 July and 1 April 1757 recur group for group, and Coquelle printed the Gouvernante’s words in 1904 from the Paris original. Lyonet deciphered these letters too at the time; his copies are in the Nationaal Archief and not digitised. A tenth letter, R2067, had no transcription and was transcribed from the images. The eleventh, to Stainville in Vienna, is in another code, a different cipher with its own page (d’Affry to Stainville, 1757). Measured as sense over the letters in this code, 89% of the groups decrypted from the key alone; with 144 values corrected or added from the Fagel register copies (October 2026, see §05) the figure is 94%, so the result is partial: R1064, R1068, R1076 and R2067 still give under 90%.

01 The documents

Louis-Auguste d’Affry (1713–1793), a Swiss officer in French service, was Louis XV’s envoy and then ambassador at The Hague from 1755 to 1762. His despatches went by the Dutch post, and the stadholder’s cabinet had them opened, copied and given to Pierre Lyonet (1706–1789), the naturalist who was also the Republic’s cipher secretary. Karl de Leeuw’s history of the Dutch black chamber (2000) records that Lyonet broke d’Affry’s first code in June 1756 and then “two more French ones”. The decipherments were copied for the Princess Governess and the regents. One set is in the Nationaal Archief (Stadhouderlijke Secretarie, 1.01.50 inv. 221 and 223, “grouped by the cipher used”, not digitised). Robert Fruin excerpted it in the 1850s, and Bussemaker printed his excerpts in 1906, as Dutch summaries with some verbatim French sentences.

Another set is inv. 192 of the Prins Willem V archive in the Koninklijk Huisarchief, catalogued on DECODE as nineteen records, R1052–R1076 and R2067. Each record is one letter: the intercept copy of the cipher, with the clear passages of d’Affry’s original written in. Seven are marked “solved by Dutch codebreakers, the solution written on separate sheets”, and eleven as not decrypted. Ten of the eleven are this target; the eleventh, R1071, is in another code and has its own page (d’Affry to Stainville, August 1757):

02 Lyonet’s decipherments among the images

A first pass took the DECODE note literally and treated the siblings’ solutions as lost. The attack started from cribs: Bussemaker’s verbatim sentence of 19 July placed “des précautions” (800 698) in R1054 by its repeat twelve words apart, and “le danger de la religion” turned up in the 22 July letter. That showed a word-and-syllable nomenclator, but cribs and a language-model solver got no further than a few dozen groups.

The clear pages among the images are Lyonet’s decipherments. On R1066 (17 February) the clear page 5578, headed “N° 142 … D’A. à R.” with a clerk’s mark, begins with the same sentence as the intercept (“Mon courrier n’est arrivé que le 15 au soir”). It then goes on, where the intercept switches to figures, with “J’ai vu Mr de Slingerland chez moi ce matin”. It is Lyonet’s decipherment of the whole letter. The same mark heads clear copies in R1052, R1053, R1062, R1063, R1065, R1069 and R1075. Their openings fit their ciphers group for group: J’ai l’honneur de vous… = 865 1028 875 1018 569.

R1065, listed as not decrypted, is one of them. Its cipher is cut into thirteen numbered paragraphs that alternate between two sets of pages, and its clear copy is on four others. DECODE had noted a clear letter “whose relation to the ciphered periods is unclear”.

03 The key

The clear copies were transcribed from the scans and aligned with their ciphers one group at a time, each group graded H (forced by the text on both sides or repeated) or M. The seven letters were R1052, R1053, R1062, R1063, R1065, R1066 and R1069, plus the ciphered parts of R1075, about 3,300 groups. The alignments were made letter by letter and cross-checked; a vote over all of them gives the key. It is a two-part code: the numbers are not in alphabetical order. It has about 1,200 groups for letters, syllables, words and names, with homophones for the common ones (de 219, 1018, 1138; la 583, 382, 10; que 197, 194, 31). Some examples: 13 is a full stop, and 105, 107 and 797 are usually nulls. Leurs Hautes Puissances is 399 627 261. Le Roi de Prusse is 16, Hollande 294, Reischach 488, Sa Majesté 22, religion 578. The Jan–Feb and Apr–Jul letters differ in their habits (which homophones the clerk favoured) but not in the code.

The DECODE transcriptions needed correcting on the way. Two copyists wrote 8 as a looped figure that the transcribers read as 0, so 056 is really 856 and 099 is 899. This affects R1064, R1067, R1072 and R1075. Elsewhere groups are run together (58291 = 582 91) or lines dropped or repeated.

04 The decrypted letters

Each letter was decrypted with the key, and the few groups missing from the key were inferred from context and recorded with their evidence. Inferred values are in braces in the working files. The full French decryptions are in the repository; extracts follow.

6 January 1757 (R1072, no. 123): the Italian

The whole letter concerns “the Italian”, a spy who wants to be paid for earlier warnings of English designs on the French possessions in the two Indies. For a hundred louis and a pension he offers to leave at once for England and report on the English forces by land and sea, the secrets of the London cabinet, their intrigues in foreign courts, the plans for the 1757 campaign, and England’s correspondents in France. D’Affry thinks the money well spent if the man is sound. This is the weakest decryption (about two thirds in sense), and Bussemaker has no entry for this date. The Italian reappears on 3 February, when d’Affry pays him the hundred louis and he leaves for Helvoet.

7 January 1757 (R1076, no. 124): convoys and herring

A convoy the Dutch feared for had arrived: four ships were to escort it, and the storm kept two of them in port. D’Affry expects the navy to be enlarged sans augmentation dans les troupes de terre, and he expects the case to be pressed harder now that people see the English respect Dutch shipping only as far as it is protected. So every convoy through the Channel must be strongly escorted. In Amsterdam he talked with a regent about the herring. Bussemaker’s summary (no. 56): enlarge the navy without the army, protect trade, herring privileges for the republicans.

25 January 1757 (R1073, no. 133): a false rumour

It was said that d’Affry had given the States-General a memorial calling any increase of the land forces a breach of neutrality. The rumour was quashed, but he went to see the Grand Pensionary. He told him the King neither wished to meddle nor ever had, but as a friend would regret seeing the Republic take on heavy expenses. Steyn answered that the increase would meet many difficulties. If its opponents could not stop it, he said, they could at least get a declaration made first to Versailles, Vienna and London, by which the Republic would confirm its resolution to stay strictly neutral; nothing would come of it before about the twentieth of next month. The register copy (scan 42R) gives these words exactly, and corrects the key-only reading in about ten places: à cette occasion for à cet égard, avec le même froid for avec le même succès, vers le vingt du Mois, Il faudroit voir si celà ne va pas mieux.

1 February 1757 (R1064, no. 136): taxes and herring

The States of Holland had adjourned until the 20th, when the augmentation comes up. The war funds were no larger than usual, and the means proposed (a sixth added to the house tax, a general rise in excises, interest on public funds cut to 2%) were so heavy that the augmentation looked unlikely. D’Affry then argues for a temporary favour to the Dutch herring fishery while France lacks sailors. It could be revoked when no longer deserved, and it would widen the quarrel between the Republic and England. He asks for it only if Holland blocks the augmentation. Then comes intelligence on suspects travelling through Antwerp, Breda and Liège. Bussemaker’s no. 59 gives the first part point by point and leaves out the agents, as Fruin said he did.

1 April 1757 (R1067, no. 161): the Gouvernante

The Maastricht convoy crisis. D’Affry found the Pensionary “fort embarrassé”: ce qui se passe ici aujourd’hui n’est […] que pour le perdre. The same afternoon he read the King’s declaration to the Princess Governess:

«… tu n’es pas ministre; faut-il que ce soit moi qui favorise les moyens de faire du mal à mon père?» Je lui ai répondu que S.A.R. savoit jusqu’à quel point le Roi mon maître a cherché à éviter la guerre … Je demande à S.A.R. d’employer non seulement ses bons offices, mais son crédit et son pouvoir pour déterminer une réponse prompte et telle que le Roi a droit de l’espérer.

Coquelle quoted the Governess’s words in 1904 from the Paris original, with the day given as 5 April. The piece that Bussemaker’s no. 67 quotes about Wassenaer (“son fils cadet a actuellement un employ en Frise, dont la nomination dépend de Mme la Gouvernante”) is piece no. 7 of this letter. The numbered pieces follow the page order, not the number order.

26 April 1757 (R1068, no. 172)

Il a passé Vendredi au soir un courrier de Vienne à Londres. Vous savez mieux que moi ce dont il est chargé. M. de Colloredo doit faire passer à M. Starrenberg ce qu’on lui aura répondu. The key alone gave hier and read the name as d’Aremberg; the Fagel register copy (below) has Vendredi and Starrenberg, Starhemberg, the imperial ambassador at Versailles. The DECODE note asked whether this note was left undecrypted because a different code was used. It was not: it is the same code as no. 171, which Lyonet deciphered.

19 July 1757 (R1054, to Bernis)

Nobody knows the purpose of the Gouvernante’s journey to The Hague. If it is to push an augmentation of the land forces, d’Affry asks for orders at once:

… il me paroit qu’il seroit essentiel aux intérêts du Roi de m’envoyer des ordres sur le champ [de demander] une confirmation de tout ce que les Etats-Généraux ont promis à Sa Majesté, et en même tems une déclaration de leur part qu’ils ne feront camper aucun corps sur leurs frontières. Je sens la dureté de cette proposition …

The passage runs from group 145 to group 351, and Bussemaker’s French agrees with it word for word except in four places. The cipher has “M. le maréchal d’Estrées” where Fruin wrote “M. le Cte”. It has “de sa communication” for “de la communication du Royaume”. It spells out Leurs Hautes Puissances, and it has “des ordres” for “ordre de demander”. The page ends with an attack of gout.

22 July 1757 (R1070, no. 210, to Bernis): Ostend

The Gouvernante is said to be going to the States of Holland to speak for her father George II, pointing to the danger to the Protestant religion. D’Affry doubts she will: if she proposes land troops, the other side will propose ships. Her party protested at the French occupation of Ostend and Nieuport. D’Affry notes that sensible people see the other side of it: with Ostend closed to the English, Amsterdam and Rotterdam will take over the trade England did with the Low Countries. Then Bussemaker’s sentence, group for group: Il y a quelques villes où quelques ministres hollandois ont prêché sur le danger de la religion, mais le peuple n’en paroit pas animé. If the Princess takes the Ostend affair as a pretext, he will ask her for an audience without waiting for orders.

17 January 1758 (R1074, no. 283, and R2067)

The first part is an extract from a correspondent’s letter. A French loan of two millions had been tried in England by “des gens si maladroits” that the ministry heard of it and a new law followed. It could still be got round: four millions at four or four and a half per cent at most, given “un profond secret, une parfaite connoissance du pays”. D’Affry adds that a Sieur Mac[donald] of the Bulkeley regiment has passed through on his way from France to England, and he suspects him of carrying commissions for the enemy. Some Scots and Irish in French service are spies, he writes, but to act would throw suspicion on the rest. The companion letter of the same day to d’Usson (R2067) has no DECODE transcription. It was transcribed from the images and decrypted at about 91%. It is d’Affry’s own letter of the same number, covering the extract. He thinks money operations in England useless: if found out, they would make France’s needs look greater than they are and put the English on their guard. Englishmen will still lend in France when the interest is right, and some already hold French life annuities. He adds that two or three Englishmen are at The Hague, one of them Mac…, and that two Spaniards from Paris, one a secretary of the Spanish embassy, are on their way to London.

05 What remains open

  • Re-measured 5 October 2026. The 21 September figure (86%) counted every group that had a value. A sense measure (measure_sense.py) now counts a group only if its nine-group window reads as French: 89.0% of the 9,306 groups in this code (Lyonet’s eight letters counted as read; rerun 14 October 2026 without R1071, which is in another code and was counted unread until then, giving 82.3%). Four letters were re-transcribed from the images, which recovered the copyist’s looped 8 that DECODE reads as 0 and 135 groups the earlier parse had lost: R1072 0.66 → 0.89, R1067 0.66 → 0.79, R1074 0.70 → 0.75, R1064 0.77 → 0.81. Pooling every occurrence fixed eighteen more code values (lui, dit, faire, Écosse, la Gouvernante …).
  • R1071 (4 August 1757, to Stainville in Vienna) is in a different code: its own page.
  • Per letter, 5–12% of groups stay open (listed as [n] in each decryption file). R1072 gives only about two thirds in sense: its copy is the worst transcribed.
  • Grades: values forced by a decipherment are H; values inferred from context in the letters without a decipherment are M or ? and listed with their evidence in read/R*_new.tsv. 56 groups of the key are ambiguous between two values (amb_M.json).
  • The eight decipherments themselves are Lyonet’s. The contribution here is the key and the decryption of the nine letters that have none on DECODE.
Contributed, 21 October 2026. WAINUT, a Dutch AI company, found that the clear text of all ten letters without a Lyonet sheet is digitised after all: clerks’ copies in the Fagel registers, Nationaal Archief 1.10.29 inv. 5186 (1757) and 5187 (1758), in date order with number and addressee (pull request #65). Aligned against them, this key gives the register’s word for 4,333 of 5,554 keyed groups (78%), against at most 21% for shuffled keys; the per-letter figures were re-run here and reproduce. The registers were found after the decryption and were not used for it: the 89% is a reading from the key alone. They settle open groups (projets, lundi, mardi, là dessus …), contradict some key values (675 is vingt, not deux; 1106 froid, not succès; 992 is -er, not des, at all eleven places) and correct the 25 January letter in about ten places.

Folded into the key, 21 October 2026. The proposals were checked on sixteen register scans read here, and each candidate was set against Lyonet’s own decipherments. 58 values were adopted: 21 groups that had no value and 37 values replaced (from the key or inferred earlier). Seven were dropped because Lyonet reads them otherwise as often or more: some groups really take two values (moi and mois, le roi de Prusse and prussien, utile and utilité). Re-measured, the ten letters with no contemporary decipherment on DECODE go from 82.4% to 86.0% of their groups read as sense, and the whole code from 89.0% to 91.3% (8,492 of 9,306); R1073 reaches 94%, R1072 93.5%, R1074 77.5%. Against the register text the key now matches 81% of keyed groups (was 78%); that figure is partly circular, since the new values come from the same registers.

Second pass, the same day. 131 groups still had no value. For 86 of them the register gives a clean word, seen on its scan (two more scans read, 63R and 248R) and not contradicted by Lyonet: Allemagne, Berlin, Wassenaer, question, six, toutes, possible, huit … The other 45 stay open: the register piece is a single letter or a syllable cut at the edge of the alignment, or the occurrences disagree. The code now reads at 94.1% (8,761 of 9,306 groups), and the ten letters with no decipherment on DECODE at 90.6%; R1067 went from 79% to 92%, R1074 from 75% to 89%. Still open: those 45 groups and the groups that take two values.

## English summary

The States of Holland have adjourned and reassemble on the 20th to deal with the army augmentation, which d'Affry
hopes will fail. The war funds for this year are no larger than last year's, and the proposed means (a sixth added
to the house tax, a general rise in the excises, and cutting interest on public funds to 2%) are so burdensome that
the States are unlikely to adopt them. On herring: he has learnt how important it is to reduce the Dutch fishery's
profit and encourage the French one, but with French sailors short in wartime he thinks a temporary favour to the
Dutch fishery would cost little, would be revocable if the Republic misbehaved, would please nearly all the Dutch,
and would widen the Anglo-Dutch quarrel over the fishery; he would ask for it only on condition that Holland resists
the augmentation. The rest is intelligence: a suspect (Charles de Quintin, travelling from Antwerp to Breda with a
Lyon merchant, flagged by M. de Hatzel) will be arrested if he comes to Holland; a captain of the Löwendal regiment
has arrived; "the Italian" reports that one La Combe (translator of the Lettres philosophiques) and an old man from
Liège have left England for France to report on the French navy; and a Paris article in the Amsterdam gazette
supplement about two ship captains is answered with an extract of d'Affry's passport register (captains of the
Comte de Maurepas and another Bordeaux ship, and a surgeon from Coutras).

## Summary

A rumour had spread that d'Affry had given the States General a memorial saying the King would regard an increase of
the Republic's land forces as a breach of neutrality. The rumour was killed at once, but d'Affry saw the Grand
Pensionary: the King did not and never meant to meddle in their internal affairs, but out of friendship would regret to
see them commit to heavy expenses whose consequences the Republic should fear. The Pensionary answered that the
augmentation will meet many difficulties, and that if its supporters cannot avoid it they will at least be strong
enough to have a declaration first made to the courts of Versailles, Vienna and London, by which the Republic would
confirm its resolution to remain strictly neutral. Nothing of this will come up before about the twentieth of next
month. The abbé who sends news from London no longer sends anything of interest (register copy, scan 42R).
